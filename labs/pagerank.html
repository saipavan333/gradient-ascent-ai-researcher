PageRank: Importance by Random Walk

How did early Google rank the whole web? Imagine a surfer clicking links forever at random. The pages they'd land on most often are the "important" ones — and a page is important if important pages link to it. That circular idea has a clean solution: the dominant eigenvector of the link graph.

What's happening: every page starts with equal rank. Each iteration, a page splits its rank evenly across its outgoing links and sends it on; a page's new rank is the sum of what it receives, plus a "teleport" term: r(i) = (1−d)/N + d·Σ_{j→i} r(j)/outdeg(j). The damping factor d (≈0.85) is the chance the surfer keeps clicking rather than jumping to a random page — which also stops rank getting trapped in dead ends. Iterating this is power iteration converging to the dominant eigenvector of the link matrix, i.e. the surfer's long-run visit frequencies. Importance is recursive: links from high-rank pages count for more, so a few good backlinks beat many weak ones. This is PageRank & graph centrality.
