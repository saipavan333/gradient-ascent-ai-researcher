The Central Limit Theorem

Here's one of the most surprising facts in all of math: average together enough independent random things — no matter how weird each one's distribution is — and the average always follows the same bell-shaped curve. That's why the normal distribution is everywhere.

▸ Do this
Pick a base distribution (even the lopsided one), set how many you average with N, then Draw thousands of samples.
◈ You'll see
At N=1 the histogram copies the base shape. Crank N up and — whatever you started with — it tightens into the smooth normal curve (white).

Pick a base shape, set N, and press Draw.
What's happening: each "sample" here draws N values from the base distribution and records their average. The central limit theorem says that as N grows, the distribution of that average approaches a normal (bell) curve centered on the true mean μ, with spread σ/√N — regardless of the base distribution's own shape (as long as it has finite variance). Two consequences you can see: the bell emerges from flat, lopsided, even two-peaked bases; and the spread shrinks like 1/√N, which is exactly why bigger samples give more reliable estimates. This underlies confidence intervals, error bars, and why Gaussian noise is the default assumption. This is Probability & Distributions.
