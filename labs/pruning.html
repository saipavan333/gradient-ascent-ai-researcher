Pruning: Cutting the Dead Weight

Trained networks are mostly redundant — a large share of their connections carry almost no signal. Pruning deletes the smallest-magnitude weights, and the model keeps working. You can often remove most of a network before accuracy finally cracks.

▸ Do this
Raise the prune threshold. Connections whose weight is smaller than it are cut (faded out).
◈ You'll see
The output barely changes while you remove the weak links — accuracy holds as sparsity climbs. Push too far and it finally collapses. There's a lot of slack to cut first.

Raise the threshold and watch weak links vanish.
What's happening: after training, we rank connections by the absolute value of their weight and delete every one below the threshold (magnitude pruning), leaving a sparse network. Because most of a network's weights are tiny and near-redundant, removing them changes the output very little at first — fidelity (how close the pruned output stays to the original) holds near 100% even at high sparsity, then drops sharply once you start cutting weights that actually matter. Sparse models use less memory and, with the right hardware, run faster; often you prune, then briefly re-train to recover the last bit of accuracy. This is Pruning & sparsity.
