ROC Curves & the Threshold Dial

A classifier doesn't output "yes" or "no" — it outputs a score, and you choose the cutoff. Slide that cutoff and you trade catching more true cases against raising more false alarms. The ROC curve draws that entire trade-off in one picture.

▸ Do this
Drag the threshold across the two overlapping score distributions and watch the confusion matrix and the point on the ROC curve move.
◈ You'll see
A strict cutoff (right) = few false alarms but missed cases; a loose cutoff (left) = catches everything but lots of false positives. The ROC curve is every cutoff at once; more area under it = a better classifier.

Drag the threshold to trade recall against false alarms.
What's happening: the orange bumps are the scores the model gives to negatives, the teal bumps to positives; they overlap, so no single cutoff is perfect. For a chosen threshold, everything to its right is predicted positive, giving true/false positives and negatives — the confusion matrix. Recall (= true-positive rate) is the fraction of real positives caught; precision is the fraction of positive predictions that were right; the false-positive rate is the fraction of negatives wrongly flagged. Sweeping the threshold traces the ROC curve (TPR vs FPR); its area (AUC) summarizes the classifier independent of any one cutoff — 1.0 is perfect, 0.5 is a coin flip. This is Evaluation metrics.
