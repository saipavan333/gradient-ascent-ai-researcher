Policy gradients (REINFORCE)

Instead of learning how good every action is and then acting greedily, what if we adjusted the policy directly — nudging its knobs to make rewarding behaviour more likely? That is policy gradient, and it powers everything from robot control to aligning ChatGPT.

Track 12 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~26 min Builds on: Lessons 0–1

Q-learning learns values and reads a policy off them with a \(\max\). That \(\max\) is fine over a handful of discrete actions, but hopeless when actions are continuous (a robot's joint angles) or when we genuinely want a random policy. Policy gradient methods skip the value detour: they write the policy as a differentiable function \(\pi_\theta\) with tunable parameters \(\theta\), and climb the reward directly by gradient ascent. The simplest one, REINFORCE, is just three words — "reward good actions" — turned into calculus. It is also, remarkably, the exact machinery used to fine-tune language models from human feedback in Lesson 4.

💡 Intuition: keep more of what worked

Think about how you refine a habit. You try a recipe, it comes out great, and next time you lean harder into the things you did — more garlic, hotter pan. Another night it's a disaster, and you quietly do less of whatever you tried. You never get told the optimal recipe; you just adjust the odds of your own choices based on how the meal turned out. Policy gradient is precisely this: run the current policy, see how the episode scored, then shift the policy's parameters so the actions you took in good episodes become more probable and those in bad episodes less probable.

Notice what you never needed: a model of the kitchen chemistry, or the "value" of a half-chopped onion. You only needed to (a) be able to vary your choices and (b) get a score at the end. That is why policy gradient works in messy, unknown environments — and why it's so general.

📐 The policy gradient theorem (the log-derivative trick)

Let the policy \(\pi_\theta(a\mid s)\) give the probability of action \(a\) in state \(s\), controlled by parameters \(\theta\) (think of \(\theta\) as the network's weights, or a table of "preferences"). Our objective is the expected return over whole episodes \(\tau\) (tau, a full trajectory of states, actions, rewards):

\[ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\!\big[\,R(\tau)\,\big],\qquad R(\tau)=\textstyle\sum_t \gamma^t r_{t+1}. \] (2.1)

We want \(\nabla_\theta J\) — the direction in parameter space that increases expected reward — but \(R(\tau)\) depends on the environment's dynamics, which we can't differentiate. The log-derivative trick rescues us. Using the identity \(\nabla_\theta p = p\,\nabla_\theta \log p\), the gradient of an expectation becomes another expectation we can sample:

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\Big[\,\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\; G_t\,\Big]. \] (2.2)

This is the policy gradient theorem, and it is beautiful. The environment's dynamics vanished — the only thing we differentiate is our own policy, \(\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\), called the score function. Each score is simply weighted by \(G_t\), the return that followed that action (a plain number, not something we differentiate). So the update is: "increase the log-probability of each action, in proportion to how much reward came after it." Good actions (large \(G_t\)) get pushed up hard; actions followed by little or negative reward get pushed down. The REINFORCE algorithm just samples this expectation with real episodes:

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\, G_t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t). \] (2.3)

One problem lurks in (2.2): using the raw return \(G_t\) as the weight makes the estimate extremely noisy (high variance). If most returns happen to be large positive numbers, every action gets pushed up, and only the differences matter — buried in noise. The fix is a baseline \(b\): subtract a reference value (like the average return) and weight by the advantage \(G_t - b\) instead.

\[ \nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta}\!\Big[\,\sum_t \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\big(G_t - b\big)\Big]. \] (2.4)

Subtracting a baseline is a free lunch: it does not change the average gradient (no bias), because \(\mathbb{E}[\,b\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\,]=0\), but it dramatically shrinks the variance. Now actions are judged as "better or worse than typical," which is exactly the signal we want. ✍️ Worked example: one REINFORCE update on a softmax policy

Three actions, preferences \(\theta=[0,0,0]\), so the softmax policy is uniform, \(\pi=[\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13]\). We sample action 2 (the middle one), the episode returns \(G=+1\), the baseline is \(b=0\), and \(\alpha=0.5\).

1
The score for a softmax. For the chosen action, \(\nabla_{\theta_j}\log\pi(a) = \mathbb{1}[j{=}a] - \pi_j\). Here \(a=2\), so the score vector is \([\,0-\tfrac13,\ 1-\tfrac13,\ 0-\tfrac13\,] = [-0.33,\ +0.67,\ -0.33]\).
2
Apply the update \(\theta \leftarrow \theta + \alpha\,(G-b)\,\text{score}\): \(\theta = [0,0,0] + 0.5(1)[-0.33, 0.67, -0.33] = [-0.17,\ +0.33,\ -0.17]\).
3
Re-read the policy. \(\pi=\text{softmax}([-0.17,0.33,-0.17]) = [0.27,\ 0.45,\ 0.27]\). The chosen action's probability rose from \(0.33\) to \(0.45\); the other two fell.
4
That's the whole idea. A rewarding action became more likely; its rivals became less likely — and the total still sums to 1 because softmax renormalises. Had the return been negative, the very same formula would have decreased the chosen action's probability. Reward good, punish bad — as calculus.

🛠️ From scratch: REINFORCE on a grid world

A softmax policy stored as a table of logits \(\theta[s,a]\). Each episode: roll out, compute discounted returns, then push every action's log-probability up or down by its advantage.

import numpy as np
GOAL, PIT, N = 15, 13, 16

def step(s, a):                                  # same grid as Lessons 0-1
    r, c = divmod(s, 4)
    if   a==0 and r>0: r-=1
    elif a==1 and r<3: r+=1
    elif a==2 and c>0: c-=1
    elif a==3 and c<3: c+=1
    s2 = r*4 + c
    return s2, (1.0 if s2==GOAL else -1.0 if s2==PIT else -0.04), s2 in (GOAL, PIT)

def softmax(z):
    z = z - z.max(); e = np.exp(z); return e / e.sum()

def reinforce(gamma=0.99, alpha=0.1, episodes=2000):
    theta = np.zeros((N, 4))                      # policy logits
    for _ in range(episodes):
        S, A, Rw, s = [], [], [], 0               # roll out one episode
        for _ in range(50):
            p = softmax(theta[s]); a = np.random.choice(4, p=p)
            s2, r, done = step(s, a)
            S.append(s); A.append(a); Rw.append(r); s = s2
            if done: break
        G, returns = 0.0, []                       # discounted return-to-go
        for r in reversed(Rw):
            G = r + gamma*G; returns.insert(0, G)
        b = np.mean(returns)                        # simple baseline (variance!)
        for t in range(len(S)):
            p = softmax(theta[S[t]])
            grad = -p; grad[A[t]] += 1.0            # d log pi / d theta = onehot - p
            theta[S[t]] += alpha * (returns[t] - b) * grad   # REINFORCE (2.4)
    return theta
The payoff — and the price. REINFORCE reaches a near-optimal policy on the grid, and unlike Q-learning it extends directly to continuous actions and stochastic policies. The price is variance: because each update rides the full random return \(G_t\), the learning curve is jittery and sample-hungry. The baseline helps, but the next lesson's real fix — learn the baseline as a value function — is what makes policy gradients practical at scale.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Differentiating the reward or the environment. You never do. The log-derivative trick moves the gradient entirely onto \(\log\pi_\theta\); returns enter only as scalar weights. That's why it works when the dynamics are unknown or non-differentiable.
  • Using \(\nabla\pi\) instead of \(\nabla\log\pi\). The score function is the gradient of the log-probability. Forgetting the log gives the wrong update and loses the clean \(\text{onehot}-\pi\) form for softmax.
  • Skipping the baseline. Without it, variance is brutal and learning crawls. A baseline reduces variance for free — it adds no bias because \(\mathbb{E}[b\,\nabla\log\pi]=0\).
  • Reusing old episodes. Policy gradient is on-policy: the expectation in (2.2) is under the current \(\pi_\theta\). Data collected by an old policy gives a biased gradient unless you correct for it (importance sampling — see PPO next).

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Policy gradient parameterises \(\pi_\theta\) and ascends \(J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\text{return}]\) directly — no \(\max\) over actions.
  • Log-derivative trick: \(\nabla_\theta J = \mathbb{E}\big[\sum_t \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,G_t\big]\); the environment never gets differentiated.
  • REINFORCE: \(\theta \mathrel{+}= \alpha\,G_t\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta\) — push up actions followed by high return.
  • Baseline \(G_t-b\): big variance reduction, zero bias. Judge actions vs "typical."
  • On-policy & high-variance — the two facts that motivate actor–critic and PPO.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. Why can policy gradient train in an environment whose dynamics we cannot differentiate?

  • Because it approximates the dynamics with a neural network.
    It doesn't model the dynamics at all; there's no environment model here.
  • The log-derivative trick moves the gradient onto \(\log\pi_\theta\); returns enter only as scalar weights.
    Correct. We differentiate only our own policy; the sampled return \(G_t\) is a plain number multiplying the score.
  • Because rewards are always differentiable.
    Rewards can be arbitrary scalars; differentiability of the reward isn't required or used.
  • Because it uses value iteration internally.
    No model, no value iteration — it climbs the reward objective directly.

Q2. In REINFORCE, an action followed by a large positive return is…

  • made less likely.
    That's the response to negative advantage, not positive return.
  • left unchanged.
    A nonzero weighted score changes its probability; only zero advantage leaves it unchanged.
  • made more likely, by raising its log-probability in proportion to the return.
    Correct. The update \(\theta\mathrel{+}=\alpha G_t\nabla\log\pi\) pushes up high-return actions.
  • copied to every other state.
    Updates are local to the state–action taken; nothing is copied across states.

Q3. What does subtracting a baseline \(b\) from the return do?

  • Reduces the variance of the gradient estimate without adding bias.
    Correct. \(\mathbb{E}[b\,\nabla\log\pi]=0\), so the mean gradient is unchanged, but the noise drops sharply.
  • Makes the gradient biased toward the baseline.
    A state-independent (or value) baseline adds no bias — that's exactly why it's used.
  • Changes which policy is optimal.
    The optimum of \(J(\theta)\) is untouched; only the estimator's variance changes.
  • Replaces the need for a learning rate.
    You still need \(\alpha\); the baseline is orthogonal to the step size.

Q4. Why is REINFORCE called "on-policy"?

  • Because it only works for deterministic policies.
    It's built for stochastic policies; determinism isn't the point.
  • Because it needs the transition model.
    It's model-free; "on-policy" is about which data is valid, not about a model.
  • Because it always picks the greedy action.
    It samples from \(\pi_\theta\); greedy selection is a value-method idea.
  • Because the gradient is an expectation under the current policy, so it needs fresh samples from \(\pi_\theta\).
    Correct. Data from an old policy biases the estimate unless corrected — the motivation for PPO's importance ratio.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) A two-action softmax policy has preferences \(\theta=[0,0]\), so \(\pi=[0.5,0.5]\). You sample action 1, get return \(G=+2\), baseline \(b=0\), \(\alpha=0.1\). Compute the new \(\theta\) and the new \(\pi\).

Show solution

Score for chosen action 1: \([1-0.5,\ 0-0.5]=[0.5,-0.5]\). Update: \(\theta = [0,0] + 0.1(2)[0.5,-0.5] = [0.1,-0.1]\). New policy: \(\pi=\text{softmax}([0.1,-0.1])\); the gap is \(0.2\), so \(\pi \approx [\,e^{0.1},e^{-0.1}\,]/(\cdot) = [0.55,\,0.45]\). Action 1's probability rose from 0.50 to about 0.55.

2. (transfer) Explain, without heavy algebra, why subtracting a constant baseline \(b\) leaves the expected gradient unchanged.

Show solution

The extra term is \(\mathbb{E}_{a\sim\pi}[\,b\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a)\,] = b\sum_a \pi_\theta(a)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a) = b\sum_a \nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a) = b\,\nabla_\theta\!\sum_a\pi_\theta(a) = b\,\nabla_\theta 1 = 0\). Because the probabilities always sum to 1, their gradient sums to 0, so any constant (or state-only) baseline cancels in expectation — it can't bias the update, only calm its variance.

3. (stretch) The full-episode return in front of \(\nabla\log\pi(a_t)\) can be replaced by the return-to-go \(\sum_{k\ge t}\gamma^{k-t}r_{k+1}\). Why is this valid, and why is it lower variance?

Show solution

An action at time \(t\) cannot influence rewards that happened before \(t\); by causality, those earlier rewards are independent of \(a_t\) and contribute zero to the expected gradient (same cancellation as a baseline). So we may drop them and weight \(\nabla\log\pi(a_t)\) only by the reward that came after — the return-to-go. Dropping the irrelevant, purely-noisy early rewards removes variance without adding bias. This "reward-to-go" form, plus a learned value baseline, is exactly the advantage the next lesson formalises.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Policy gradient is the workhorse of modern RL because it scales where value methods stall: continuous control (robot locomotion, dexterous manipulation), stochastic policies (games with hidden information), and — most consequentially — RLHF, where the "policy" is a large language model and the "reward" is a learned model of human preference (Lesson 4). AlphaGo trained its policy network with policy gradients before bolting on search. The recurring obstacle is always the same one you just met: variance and sample efficiency. Every advance that follows — learned baselines (actor–critic), trust regions (TRPO), clipped updates (PPO), and better advantage estimates (GAE) — is a different weapon against that variance. Master REINFORCE and the rest of the track is variations on this one theme.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Instead of learning how good each action is, policy gradient tunes the policy's knobs directly: run it, see how the episode scored, then make the actions you took more likely if it went well and less likely if it didn't. The log-derivative trick means you only ever differentiate your own policy — never the world — which is why the same method aligns language models."
