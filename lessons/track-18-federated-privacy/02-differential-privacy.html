Differential privacy: a mathematical privacy guarantee

Keeping data on-device isn't enough — released results can still betray who was in the data. Differential privacy gives a provable promise: the output looks essentially the same whether or not any single person participated, so no one can tell if you were there.

Track 17 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~19 min Builds on: Lesson 0

Federated learning keeps raw data on your device, but the results it shares — model updates, statistics, aggregates — can still leak private facts. A model can memorize a training example verbatim; a released average can reveal an individual if you know the others; famously, "anonymized" datasets have been re-identified again and again. Differential privacy (DP) is the rigorous answer, and it's one of the most important ideas in modern data protection. It makes a precise, provable promise: any published result should be almost exactly as likely whether or not any single person's data was included. If the output distribution barely changes when you're added or removed, then no attacker — no matter how clever or how much side information they have — can confidently tell whether you were in the data. Your participation is deniable. DP achieves this by adding carefully calibrated random noise, and it comes with a tunable dial, \(\varepsilon\) (epsilon), that trades privacy against accuracy. This lesson builds the core mechanism and shows the trade-off in action; the next applies it to training whole models.

💡 Intuition: a poll where everyone can plausibly deny

Suppose you survey people on an embarrassing question. To protect them, you tell each person: before answering, secretly flip a coin — heads, answer truthfully; tails, flip again and answer yes/no by that second coin. Now anyone who answered "yes" can plausibly claim the coin made them say it, not their real behavior. Yet with enough responses, you can still estimate the true rate (you know how the coins bias things). That's the essence of differential privacy: inject just enough randomness that any single person's real answer is hidden behind plausible noise, while the aggregate signal survives. No individual is exposed; the crowd's pattern remains.

Formally, a randomized mechanism \(M\) is \(\varepsilon\)-differentially private if for any two neighbouring datasets \(D\) and \(D'\) (differing by one person) and any possible output, the probabilities are within a factor \(e^\varepsilon\):

\[ \Pr[M(D) = o] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\,\Pr[M(D') = o] \qquad \text{(add Laplace noise of scale } \tfrac{\text{sensitivity}}{\varepsilon}\text{)} \] (2.1)

Read it: for every possible output \(o\), the chance of seeing it with your data (\(D\)) is at most \(e^\varepsilon\) times the chance without it (\(D'\)) — and vice versa. If \(\varepsilon\) is small, \(e^\varepsilon\approx 1\), so the two are nearly indistinguishable: your presence barely changes anything. We achieve it by adding random noise (commonly Laplace) scaled to sensitivity (how much one person can change the result) divided by \(\varepsilon\). Smaller \(\varepsilon\) → more noise → stronger privacy → less accuracy. \(\varepsilon\) is the privacy budget you spend.

✍️ Worked example: a private average

From differential_privacy.py: release the mean of 1000 people's values in [0,1] privately.

1
Sensitivity. One person's value (in [0,1]) can change the mean by at most \(1/1000\). That's how much a single participant can move the answer — the sensitivity.
2
Add noise. Release true mean + Laplace noise with scale = sensitivity/ε = \((1/1000)/\varepsilon\). At \(\varepsilon=1\) the noise is tiny relative to the mean.
3
Indistinguishability. Flip person 0's value (a neighbouring dataset) and release again, 20,000 times each. The two distributions of released means differ by only ~0.0003 — essentially identical. No one can tell if person 0 was included.
4
Utility survives. The released mean is still within ~0.001 of the truth at \(\varepsilon=1\). Privacy for individuals, accuracy for the aggregate. Shrink \(\varepsilon\) to 0.1 and error grows 10×: the dial at work.

🛠️ From scratch: the Laplace mechanism

def private_mean(data, eps):
    n = len(data); true = data.mean()
    sensitivity = 1.0 / n                      # one person moves the mean by at most 1/n
    scale = sensitivity / eps                  # Laplace scale = sensitivity / epsilon
    return true + rng.laplace(0, scale)        # release TRUE answer + calibrated noise

# eps=0.1 -> error ~0.010   eps=1 -> ~0.001   eps=10 -> ~0.0001   (smaller eps = more private, noisier)

Two lines carry the whole idea: compute how much one person can move the answer (sensitivity), and add noise proportional to it over \(\varepsilon\). The larger the group (bigger \(n\)), the smaller each person's influence, so the less noise you need for the same privacy — privacy is easier in a crowd, which is why DP shines on large populations.

The privacy budget composes. A crucial property: if you answer several queries, each \(\varepsilon\)-private, the privacy loss adds up — ten \(\varepsilon=0.5\) queries cost \(\varepsilon=5\) total. So \(\varepsilon\) is a genuine budget you spend across all the questions you'll ever ask of the data; ask too many and you've leaked too much. This composition is why real systems track cumulative \(\varepsilon\) carefully, and why training a model (millions of gradient queries) needs the special accounting of DP-SGD (next lesson). Typical deployed values are \(\varepsilon\) between about 1 and 10.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking anonymization = privacy. Removing names doesn't stop re-identification via side information. DP is a guarantee about the output, robust to any auxiliary knowledge — a fundamentally stronger notion.
  • Forgetting the budget composes. Each query spends \(\varepsilon\); many queries add up. You can't answer unlimited questions and stay private.
  • Setting \(\varepsilon\) huge for accuracy. A large \(\varepsilon\) (say 50) technically "satisfies DP" but adds almost no noise and gives almost no protection. \(\varepsilon\) must be genuinely small to mean something.
  • Getting the sensitivity wrong. Under-estimating how much one person can change the result means too little noise and a broken guarantee. Sensitivity analysis is where DP is easy to get subtly wrong.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Differential privacy: the output is almost as likely with or without any one person (within \(e^\varepsilon\), eq 2.1) — participation is deniable.
  • Mechanism: add noise scaled to sensitivity/\(\varepsilon\) (how much one person can change the result, over the budget).
  • \(\varepsilon\) is the privacy budget: smaller = more private + noisier; larger = accurate + less private.
  • It's robust to any side information — far stronger than anonymization.
  • Budget composes across queries; privacy is easier with more people (smaller per-person sensitivity).

Q1. What does differential privacy guarantee?

  • The data is encrypted.
    DP is about outputs, not encryption of storage.
  • The output is nearly the same whether or not any one person is in the data.
    Correct. That indistinguishability makes participation deniable.
  • Names are removed.
    That's anonymization, which DP is strictly stronger than.
  • The model is more accurate.
    DP typically costs some accuracy; it's a privacy guarantee.

Q2. What does a smaller \(\varepsilon\) mean?

  • More privacy, more noise, less accuracy.
    Correct. Small \(\varepsilon\) forces the outputs to be nearly identical with/without any person — strong privacy, noisier answers.
  • Less privacy.
    Smaller \(\varepsilon\) is more private.
  • More accuracy.
    Smaller \(\varepsilon\) means more noise, less accuracy.
  • Nothing changes.
    \(\varepsilon\) directly controls the privacy-accuracy trade-off.

Q3. Why is DP stronger than just removing names (anonymization)?

  • It uses bigger datasets.
    Dataset size isn't the distinction.
  • It encrypts the output.
    DP adds noise, not encryption.
  • Its guarantee holds against an attacker with any side information.
    Correct. Anonymization fails under auxiliary data; DP's guarantee is about the output regardless of what the attacker knows.
  • It runs faster.
    Speed is unrelated.

Q4. What happens to the privacy budget when you answer many queries?

  • It stays constant.
    Each query spends privacy; it doesn't stay fixed.
  • It resets each time.
    There's no free reset; loss accumulates.
  • It decreases the noise.
    More queries mean more total leakage, not less noise.
  • The privacy loss composes (adds up) across queries.
    Correct. Ten \(\varepsilon=0.5\) queries cost \(\varepsilon=5\) total — you must budget across all queries.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, slide ε to its smallest value. Can you distinguish the two curves? Now slide it to the largest. What have you traded?

Show solution

At the smallest ε the two curves are broad and lie almost perfectly on top of each other — you genuinely cannot tell which dataset (with or without the one person) produced a given output, which is exactly strong privacy, but the released value is very noisy. At the largest ε the curves are sharp and clearly separated — the output pins down the answer accurately, but now the presence of that one person visibly shifts the result, so privacy is weak. You've traded privacy for accuracy along the ε dial. The art of applying DP is choosing an ε that gives meaningful privacy while leaving the aggregate answer useful — usually somewhere in the middle, informed by how sensitive the data is.

2. (transfer) Why does releasing a private mean get easier (need less noise) as the number of people grows?

Show solution

Because the sensitivity — how much one person can change the answer — shrinks as the group grows. For a mean of values in [0,1] over \(n\) people, any single person can move it by at most \(1/n\), so with more people each individual's influence is smaller, and you need less noise (scale \((1/n)/\varepsilon\)) to hide them for the same \(\varepsilon\). Intuitively, it's easy to hide in a crowd and hard to hide in a group of three. This is why DP is well-suited to large-scale aggregate statistics and large training sets — the more participants, the cheaper privacy is in terms of accuracy — and why DP on tiny datasets is painful (each person is too influential to mask without wrecking the answer).

3. (stretch) Training a neural network involves millions of gradient computations on the data. Given that privacy budget composes, why is naive DP hopeless here, and what must DP-SGD (next lesson) do about it?

Show solution

Each gradient step is effectively a query on the data, and composition says the privacy losses add up — over millions of steps a naive per-step budget would sum to an astronomically large (meaningless) total ε, so there'd be no real privacy left. DP-SGD makes it tractable with two ideas: (1) bound each step's sensitivity by clipping every per-example gradient to a fixed norm, so one example's influence on each update is capped, then add calibrated noise — making each step a controlled private query; and (2) use tight composition accounting (the "moments accountant" / Rényi-DP) plus subsampling amplification (each step sees only a random minibatch, which amplifies privacy) to track the total ε far more tightly than naive summation. Together these let you train for many steps while keeping the cumulative ε at a usable value (single digits). So DP-SGD is exactly the engineering of "how to spend a privacy budget across a million steps and have any left" — the subject of the next lesson.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Differential privacy is deployed at the largest scales: the US Census Bureau used DP to protect the 2020 Census; Apple and Google use it to collect usage statistics from phones without seeing individuals; and DP is increasingly required when training models on sensitive data. It's the gold standard because its guarantee is mathematical and holds against any adversary. The frontier refines the accounting (Rényi and zero-concentrated DP give tighter budgets), designs mechanisms that add less noise for the same privacy, and grapples with the real tension that strong privacy (small \(\varepsilon\)) can meaningfully hurt accuracy and can disproportionately affect underrepresented groups. DP turns "privacy" from a vague promise into a quantity you can measure, budget, and prove — a genuinely foundational shift.
