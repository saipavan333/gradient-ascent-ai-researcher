Quantization: fewer bits per weight

Neural networks store weights as 32-bit floating-point numbers — but that precision is wildly excessive. Quantization rounds each weight to a handful of levels (8 bits, or fewer), cutting memory by 4× or more and enabling fast integer math, with almost no accuracy loss.

Track 19 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~17 min Builds on: Lesson 1

Pruning removed weights; quantization shrinks the ones that remain. By default a weight is a 32-bit floating-point number, able to represent values to about seven decimal digits of precision. For a neural network, that's absurd overkill — the network's behavior barely changes if you round each weight to a much coarser grid. Quantization exploits this by storing each weight with far fewer bits: 8-bit quantization uses just 256 possible levels instead of billions, immediately cutting memory by 4× (32→8 bits). And it's not only about storage: 8-bit integer arithmetic runs much faster and uses far less energy than 32-bit float on modern hardware, so quantized models are both smaller and quicker. The astonishing part is how little accuracy you lose — 8-bit quantization is often indistinguishable from full precision, and even 4-bit (16 levels!) frequently works well. This is why the large language models you can run on a laptop are quantized: a model that needs 28 GB in 16-bit fits in 7 GB at 4-bit. This lesson builds quantization and shows precision was there to be traded away.

💡 Intuition: round to a coarser ruler

Imagine measuring a room with a laser that reports millimeters, then storing every measurement to seven decimal places. For hanging a picture, you don't need nanometer precision — rounding to the nearest centimeter is fine, and the numbers take a fraction of the space to write down. Neural network weights are the same: the training process finds weights to high precision, but the function the network computes is robust to rounding them onto a much coarser grid. Quantization picks a small set of evenly-spaced levels spanning the range of the weights and snaps each weight to the nearest one. Storing "which of 256 levels" takes 8 bits; storing a full float takes 32. The rounding introduces a tiny error per weight, but these errors are small and tend to cancel out across the network, so the output is almost unchanged.

Uniform quantization to \(b\) bits (\(L = 2^b - 1\) levels) maps each weight to the nearest grid point:

\[ W_q = \text{round}\!\left(\frac{W - W_{\min}}{W_{\max} - W_{\min}}\, L\right) \cdot \frac{W_{\max}-W_{\min}}{L} + W_{\min} \] (2.1)

Read it inside-out: \(\frac{W - W_{\min}}{W_{\max}-W_{\min}}\) rescales the weight to \([0,1]\); multiplying by \(L\) and rounding snaps it to one of \(L{+}1\) integer levels; then the outer terms scale it back to the original range. In practice you'd store just the small integer (0 to \(L\)) plus the shared min/max — that's the \(b\)-bit representation. Fewer bits \(b\) means fewer levels, more rounding error, and smaller memory: the precision-vs-size dial.

🛠️ From scratch: uniform quantization

def quantize(W, bits):
    lo, hi = W.min(), W.max();  L = 2**bits - 1
    q = np.round((W - lo) / (hi - lo) * L)      # snap to integer levels 0..L
    return q / L * (hi - lo) + lo               # dequantize back to float for use

# 32-bit 0.974  |  8-bit 0.974 (4x smaller)  |  4-bit 0.972 (8x)  |  2-bit 0.950 (16x)

The whole operation is a rescale, a round, and a rescale back. In a real deployment you'd keep the weights as the small integers q and do the matrix multiplies in integer arithmetic (much faster), only using the scale factors to interpret the result. That's where the speed and energy savings — not just memory — come from.

How you can run big LLMs on a laptop. Quantization is the single biggest reason large language models became runnable on consumer hardware. A 7-billion-parameter model in 16-bit floats needs ~14 GB just for the weights; quantized to 4-bit it's ~3.5 GB — small enough for a laptop GPU or even CPU. Formats like GPTQ, AWQ, and the GGUF quantization levels (Q4, Q5, Q8) are exactly this idea, refined so the accuracy loss is minimal. The reason "run Llama on your MacBook" is possible at all is that those 16-bit weights were mostly wasted precision.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Quantizing everything uniformly. Some layers (and especially outliers) are sensitive; mixed-precision quantization keeps sensitive parts at higher precision. Naive uniform low-bit can crater accuracy.
  • Ignoring the range (outliers). A few huge weights stretch the min/max, wasting levels on empty space. Clipping outliers or per-channel scales fixes this — crucial for very low bit-widths.
  • Expecting speedups without integer kernels. Memory shrinks automatically, but speed requires hardware/software that does integer math. Otherwise you dequantize and run float anyway.
  • Post-training quantizing when accuracy matters most. For aggressive (low-bit) quantization, quantization-aware training — simulating the rounding during training — recovers much more accuracy than quantizing a finished model.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Quantization stores each weight in fewer bits by rounding to a small set of levels (eq 2.1).
  • 8-bit = 256 levels, 4× smaller, usually no accuracy loss; 4-bit often fine; lower bits trade more accuracy.
  • Also faster: integer arithmetic beats float on memory, speed, and energy.
  • Watch outliers and layer sensitivity; per-channel scales and mixed precision help at low bit-widths.
  • Quantization-aware training beats post-training quantization when pushing to very low bits.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, drop from 8 bits to 2 bits. What happens to the weight distribution and the accuracy, and why do the two not fall together at the same rate?

Show solution

As bits drop, the continuous weight distribution collapses onto fewer and fewer discrete spikes (256 levels at 8-bit, down to just 4 at 2-bit), so the rounding error per weight grows. But accuracy falls much more slowly than the level count: it's essentially unchanged from 8-bit through 4-bit and only dips noticeably at 2-bit. The reason is that the network's output is robust to small per-weight errors — they're individually tiny and partly cancel across the many weights feeding each neuron — so it takes a fairly coarse grid (very few levels) before the accumulated error is large enough to change predictions. This gap between "levels lost" and "accuracy lost" is exactly the free lunch quantization exploits: you can throw away most of the precision before the network notices.

2. (transfer) A single weight of value 50 among weights that are otherwise all between −1 and 1 wrecks 4-bit quantization. Why, and how would you fix it?

Show solution

Uniform quantization spreads its levels evenly across the full range [min, max]. That one outlier at 50 stretches the range to [−1, 50], so with only 16 levels (4-bit) the spacing between levels becomes ~3.2 — meaning almost all the real weights (which live in [−1, 1]) get crushed onto just one or two levels, destroying their precision. The outlier hogged the dynamic range. Fixes: clip the outlier to the normal range before quantizing (accepting a large error on that one weight to preserve precision for all the others); use per-channel or per-group scales so the outlier's group gets its own range; or keep outliers in higher precision (mixed-precision quantization). Outlier handling is the central challenge of low-bit quantization for large language models, where a few large activation/weight values would otherwise ruin the quantization for everything else — which is why methods like AWQ and outlier-aware schemes exist.

3. (stretch) "Quantization-aware training" simulates the rounding during training rather than quantizing a finished model. Why does that recover accuracy at very low bit-widths?

Show solution

Post-training quantization takes a model trained in full precision and rounds it afterward — the training never "knew" it would be quantized, so at low bit-widths the rounding shifts weights to places the trained solution didn't account for, and accuracy drops. Quantization-aware training (QAT) instead simulates the quantization (rounds weights/activations) during the forward pass while training, so the network experiences the rounding error and learns weights that are robust to it — it finds a solution that still works after being snapped to the coarse grid. (A technical trick, the "straight-through estimator," lets gradients flow through the non-differentiable rounding.) Because the model adapts to the quantization rather than being surprised by it, QAT recovers much of the accuracy lost by naive post-training quantization, especially at 4-bit and below. The trade-off is cost: QAT requires (re)training, whereas post-training quantization is instant — so you use post-training quantization when it's good enough (often at 8-bit) and reach for QAT when pushing to aggressive low-bit precision.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Quantization is arguably the most impactful efficiency technique in current AI, because it's what lets enormous models run cheaply. 8-bit inference is standard; 4-bit is now common for large language models; and research pushes toward 3-, 2-, and even 1-bit ("binary/ternary") networks. The frontier is dominated by handling the outliers that make low-bit LLM quantization hard (GPTQ, AWQ, SmoothQuant), quantizing activations as well as weights, and hardware co-design (chips built for low-bit math). Together with distillation and pruning, quantization is why the gap between "a model exists" and "you can actually run it" keeps shrinking. Every time you run a capable model locally or a company serves one cheaply at scale, quantization is doing quiet, essential work in the background.
