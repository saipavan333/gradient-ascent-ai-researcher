Mixed precision: training in 16 bits, safely

Training in 16-bit floating point instead of 32-bit halves memory and roughly doubles speed on modern hardware. But 16-bit numbers can't represent tiny gradients, which silently vanish. One clever trick — loss scaling — fixes it, and mixed precision became the default for training large models.

Track 19 · Lesson 4 Concept + code + interactive ~17 min Builds on: Lesson 3

Quantization (Lesson 2) reduced precision for a deployed model; mixed precision reduces precision during training itself. The default is 32-bit floating point (fp32), but modern GPUs have special hardware that does 16-bit float (fp16) math roughly twice as fast, and fp16 numbers take half the memory. Training in 16 bits would therefore be a huge win — faster and letting you fit bigger models and batches. The catch is fp16's tiny range: it can only represent numbers down to about \(6\times10^{-5}\); anything smaller rounds to zero (underflow). Neural network gradients are often exactly that small, so in naive fp16 they silently vanish, and training stalls or diverges. The elegant fix is loss scaling: before backpropagation, multiply the loss by a large constant \(S\) (say 1024). By the chain rule, this multiplies every gradient by \(S\) too, lifting the tiny ones up out of the underflow zone so they survive in fp16. Then, just before applying the update, you divide the gradients back by \(S\) — recovering their true values. The name "mixed" precision is because you keep a master copy of the weights in fp32 for the update (precision where it matters) while doing the heavy matrix math in fp16 (speed where it matters). This lesson builds loss scaling and closes the efficiency track.

💡 Intuition: turn up the volume before recording

Imagine recording a very quiet sound on equipment that can't capture anything below a certain volume — the quiet parts just don't register, they become silence. The trick audio engineers use: turn the volume way up before recording, so the quiet parts are now loud enough to capture, then turn it back down afterward. You lose nothing, because scaling up and back down is exactly reversible, but now the faint signal survived the recording. Loss scaling is precisely this. The "quiet sound" is the tiny gradients; the "equipment that can't capture quiet" is fp16's underflow floor; multiplying the loss by \(S\) is turning up the volume so the gradients are loud enough to survive fp16; dividing by \(S\) before the weight update turns it back down. The gradient information is preserved, just temporarily amplified through the danger zone.

The loss-scaling recipe:

\[ \text{scale up: } \mathcal{L}' = S\cdot\mathcal{L} \;\Rightarrow\; \nabla' = S\cdot\nabla \ \ (\text{now above the fp16 floor}), \qquad \text{scale down: } \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{\nabla'}{S} \] (4.1)

Read it: multiply the loss by a big \(S\) before backprop; by the chain rule every gradient \(\nabla\) becomes \(S\nabla\), which is large enough to be represented in fp16 without underflowing. Compute and store those scaled gradients safely in fp16. Then, before the weight update, divide by \(S\) to get back the true gradient \(\nabla\), and take the normal step. The scaling is perfectly undone, so the training is mathematically the same as fp32 — it just survived the trip through 16-bit storage.

✍️ Worked example: underflow and rescue

From mixed_precision.py: a batch of tiny gradients (magnitude ~2e-5), with and without loss scaling.

1
The problem. These gradients are around \(2\times10^{-5}\), below fp16's floor of \(6\times10^{-5}\). Stored directly in fp16: 100% underflow to zero — the update is garbage.
2
Scale up. Multiply by \(S=1024\): the gradients become ~\(0.02\), far above the floor. Stored in fp16: 0% underflow — all survive.
3
Scale down. Divide the recovered gradients by \(S\): back to ~\(2\times10^{-5}\), now faithfully preserved.
4
Result. Naive fp16 recovered the gradient with 123% relative error (it was mostly zeros); loss scaling recovered it with 0.0% error. Same math as fp32, at half the memory and roughly double the speed.

🛠️ From scratch: loss scaling

S = 1024.0
grad = backprop(loss * S)        # scale the loss -> all gradients multiplied by S (above fp16 floor)
grad = to_fp16(grad)             # store in fp16 safely (no underflow now)
w -= lr * grad / S               # UNSCALE before the update -> true gradient recovered

# naive fp16 (S=1): 100% of tiny gradients underflow to 0, 123% error
# loss scaling (S=1024): 0% underflow, 0.0% error — same as fp32

The two extra operations — * S before backprop and / S before the update — are the entire trick, and they exactly cancel. In practice the master weights are kept in fp32 (so the small update lr * grad / S isn't itself lost to fp16 rounding), while the large fp16 matrix multiplies do the heavy lifting — hence "mixed" precision. Modern frameworks pick \(S\) automatically (dynamic loss scaling), raising it until gradients start to overflow, then backing off.

Why this is the default now. Mixed precision is standard for training large models — it's often a one-line change (autocast in PyTorch) that roughly halves memory and speeds up training 1.5–3× on modern GPUs with essentially no accuracy loss. It's why you can train bigger models and batches on the same hardware. Newer formats like bfloat16 (bf16) sidestep the underflow problem differently: bf16 keeps fp32's wide range (so gradients don't underflow) at the cost of fewer precision bits, so it often needs no loss scaling at all — which is why it's now common on hardware that supports it. Either way, training in 16 bits — with the range problem handled — is how modern large-scale training gets its speed.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Forgetting loss scaling with fp16. Naive fp16 training silently fails as gradients underflow. You must scale (or use bf16, which avoids the issue).
  • Keeping master weights in fp16. The tiny weight update can itself underflow when added to a weight in fp16. Keep an fp32 master copy for the update.
  • Setting the scale too high. Too large an \(S\) makes gradients overflow to infinity. Dynamic loss scaling finds the sweet spot automatically.
  • Confusing bf16 and fp16. Both are 16-bit, but bf16 trades precision for range (no underflow, usually no loss scaling needed), while fp16 has more precision but the underflow problem. Know which your hardware uses.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Mixed precision trains in 16-bit float (half memory, ~2× faster on modern GPUs) while keeping fp32 master weights.
  • fp16 underflow: tiny gradients (below ~6e-5) round to zero and vanish.
  • Loss scaling: multiply the loss by \(S\) before backprop (lifts gradients above the floor), divide by \(S\) before the update (eq 4.1) — exactly reversible.
  • Dynamic loss scaling picks \(S\) automatically; too high overflows, too low underflows.
  • bf16 keeps fp32's range, avoiding underflow — often no loss scaling needed.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, start at S=1 and raise the loss scale. What happens to the cloud of gradients relative to the red underflow zone, and how many are saved?

Show solution

At S=1 most of the gradient cloud sits inside the red fp16 underflow zone, so those gradients round to zero and are lost. As you raise S, the entire cloud slides to the right (multiplying by S shifts every value up by log(S) on the log axis), and progressively more gradients clear the red floor until, at a high enough S, essentially all of them survive. The readout shows the fraction saved climbing from near zero to 100%. Because the shift is a pure multiplication that you undo by dividing by S before the update, no gradient information is distorted — they're just temporarily lifted through the danger zone. This is loss scaling working exactly as designed: rescue the tiny gradients with a reversible scale-up.

2. (transfer) If you set the loss scale too high, training can break in a new way. What goes wrong, and how does "dynamic" loss scaling avoid both failure modes?

Show solution

Too low a scale leaves tiny gradients underflowing to zero (the original problem). Too high a scale pushes large gradients past fp16's maximum representable value (~65504), so they overflow to infinity/NaN and corrupt the update — the opposite danger. There's a window of good scales in between. Dynamic loss scaling finds it automatically: it starts with a high scale and, whenever it detects an overflow (inf/NaN in the gradients), it skips that update and halves the scale; and if many steps pass with no overflow, it doubles the scale to keep gradients as high as safely possible. This automatically tracks the right scale as gradient magnitudes change over training, avoiding both underflow (scale too low) and overflow (scale too high) without any manual tuning — which is why it's the default in modern frameworks.

3. (stretch) Step back over the whole track. You've built distillation, pruning, quantization, parallelism, and mixed precision. Organize them: which make training cheaper, which make deployment cheaper?

Show solution

Training-time efficiency (make it feasible to train big models): parallelism (data/model/pipeline) splits training across many devices so huge models and datasets are trainable at all; mixed precision halves memory and speeds up the math during training, letting you fit bigger models/batches. Deployment-time efficiency (make a trained model cheap to run): distillation replaces a big model with a small one that mimics it; pruning deletes redundant weights from a trained model; quantization stores the surviving weights in fewer bits for less memory and faster integer math. (Some blur the line — quantization-aware training and mixed precision touch both, and you can distill/prune/quantize during or after training.) The unifying theme is that modern models are extravagant with compute, memory, and precision, and each technique reclaims one kind of waste: redundant capacity (distillation, pruning), excess numerical precision (quantization, mixed precision), or single-machine limits (parallelism). Mastering the whole toolkit is what lets you take a model from "exists in a research lab on a supercomputer" to "runs cheaply in production on a phone" — arguably as important to real-world impact as the model architecture itself.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Mixed-precision training is the default for essentially all large-model training today — it's built into PyTorch and TensorFlow as a near-automatic feature, and modern accelerators are designed around 16-bit (and now 8-bit, FP8) math. bf16 has become the preferred format on hardware that supports it precisely because it dodges the underflow problem. The frontier pushes to even lower precision for training itself — 8-bit floating point (FP8) training is now used for the largest models, with its own scaling and stability tricks descended directly from loss scaling. This closes the efficient-ML track: from making trained models tiny (distillation, pruning, quantization) to making training possible and fast (parallelism, mixed precision), you've seen the full toolkit that turns the theoretical possibility of a huge model into a practical, deployable, affordable reality. Efficiency isn't a footnote to modern AI — it's a large part of what makes it work.
