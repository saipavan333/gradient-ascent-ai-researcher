Pruning: most of a network is dead weight

A surprising fact about trained neural networks: you can delete the majority of their weights — often 70% or more — and accuracy barely changes. Pruning exploits this massive redundancy to make models smaller and faster.

Track 19 · Lesson 1 Concept + code + interactive ~17 min Builds on: Track 6

Neural networks are trained with far more weights than they end up needing — the over-parameterization actually helps them train (Track 3's double descent), but once trained, a huge fraction of the weights are near zero and contribute almost nothing. Pruning is the technique of finding and deleting those useless weights to shrink the model. The simplest and most effective version, magnitude pruning, is almost embarrassingly straightforward: rank all the weights by absolute value and set the smallest ones to zero. A weight of 0.0003 is doing essentially nothing, so removing it costs almost no accuracy — and if you remove enough of them, the model becomes sparse, which means less memory to store and potentially much faster to run. The remarkable empirical fact is how much you can remove: real networks routinely tolerate 70–90% of their weights being deleted with only a slight accuracy drop, especially if you fine-tune afterward. That's a model an order of magnitude smaller for almost free. This lesson builds magnitude pruning and shows accuracy holding steady as the network is hollowed out.

💡 Intuition: trim the branches that bear no fruit

Picture the network as a dense thicket of connections, each with a strength (the weight). After training, most connections turned out to be weak — barely-there wisps that don't affect the output. Pruning is gardening: snip away the weakest branches and the plant is essentially unchanged, just leaner. The insight is that a trained network's function is carried by a small subset of strong connections; the rest is scaffolding that mattered during training but not for the final computation. Remove the scaffolding and you keep the fruit. The magnitude of a weight is a cheap, effective proxy for its importance: near-zero weights, by definition, barely move their signal.

Magnitude pruning at sparsity level \(s\): find the magnitude threshold below which fraction \(s\) of weights fall, and zero everything below it.

\[ \tau = \text{quantile}\big(|W|,\ s\big), \qquad W_{ij} \leftarrow \begin{cases} W_{ij} & |W_{ij}| \ge \tau \\ 0 & |W_{ij}| < \tau \end{cases} \] (1.1)

Read it: to prune a fraction \(s\) (say 0.7 for 70% sparsity), compute \(\tau\), the \(s\)-quantile of the absolute weights — the cutoff below which the smallest \(s\) of them sit. Then keep every weight whose magnitude is at or above \(\tau\) and zero the rest. What remains is a sparse network with only the strongest \((1-s)\) of its connections. Often you then fine-tune the survivors for a bit to recover any lost accuracy — and it comes right back.

✍️ Worked example: deleting 70% for free

Dense. The full network scores 0.973 accuracy.
2
Prune 50%. Zero the smallest-magnitude half of the weights. Accuracy: 0.973 — no change. Half the network was doing nothing.
3
Prune 70%. Delete 70% of weights. Accuracy: 0.952 — a tiny drop, and fine-tuning would recover most of it.
4
Prune 90%. Now it hurts: accuracy falls to 0.687. Somewhere between 70% and 90% you cross the threshold where the weights that do matter start getting cut. The art of pruning is finding that edge.

🛠️ From scratch: magnitude pruning

def prune(W, frac):
    thr = np.quantile(np.abs(W), frac)          # cutoff: the smallest 'frac' of weights
    return W * (np.abs(W) >= thr)               # keep large weights, zero the small ones

# accuracy vs sparsity (no fine-tuning):
#   dense 0.973  |  50% pruned 0.973  |  70% pruned 0.952  |  90% pruned 0.687

Two lines. The * (np.abs(W) >= thr) is a mask that zeroes the small weights. Because the surviving weight matrix is mostly zeros, specialized sparse formats store and compute it far more cheaply — that's where the real-world speed and memory savings come from.

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis. A striking 2019 discovery: inside a big trained network there exist small sparse subnetworks (the "winning tickets") that, if you'd trained them from the start with their original initial weights, would reach the full accuracy on their own. In other words, the dense network was partly a search over many small networks, and pruning finds the one that mattered. This reframed pruning from "compress a finished model" to "the essential network was hiding in there all along" — a deep hint about why over-parameterized training works, connecting efficiency back to the theory in Track 3.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Pruning too aggressively without fine-tuning. High sparsity in one shot can hurt; the standard recipe is prune-a-bit, fine-tune, repeat (iterative pruning), which reaches much higher sparsity safely.
  • Expecting automatic speedups. Unstructured sparsity (random zeros) needs special hardware/software to run faster; otherwise you save memory but not time. Structured pruning (removing whole neurons/channels) gives guaranteed speedups.
  • Assuming magnitude = importance perfectly. It's a good, cheap heuristic, but a small weight can still matter and a large one can be redundant. Better importance measures exist for the last few percent.
  • Pruning before training is done. Prune a trained (or nearly trained) network; the redundancy that's safe to remove only becomes clear after learning.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Trained networks are hugely redundant — most weights are near zero and contribute little.
  • Magnitude pruning zeroes the smallest-magnitude weights (eq 1.1), making the model sparse.
  • 70–90% of weights can go with small accuracy loss, especially with fine-tuning.
  • Iterative prune-and-fine-tune reaches higher sparsity than one-shot; structured pruning gives real speedups.
  • Lottery Ticket Hypothesis: a small trainable subnetwork was hiding inside the big one all along.

Q1. What does magnitude pruning remove?

  • The largest weights.
    Those are the important ones — you keep them.
  • The smallest-magnitude weights.
    Correct. Near-zero weights contribute little, so they're safe to delete.
  • Entire layers at random.
    Magnitude pruning targets individual small weights, not random layers.
  • The training data.
    Pruning acts on weights, not data.

Q2. Roughly how much of a trained network can typically be pruned with little accuracy loss?

  • 70–90% of the weights.
    Correct. The redundancy is enormous, especially with fine-tuning.
  • About 5%.
    Far more than that is removable.
  • Exactly 50%, always.
    It varies widely and is usually higher.
  • None — every weight is essential.
    The whole point is that most aren't.

Q3. Why might unstructured pruning not speed up your model?

  • It always speeds things up.
    Not without sparse-aware hardware/software.
  • Because it removes too few weights.

  • Because it changes the architecture.
    Unstructured pruning keeps the architecture.

    Correct. Structured pruning (whole neurons) gives guaranteed speedups.

  • Pruning is random luck.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, slide sparsity from 0 to 95%. Describe the shape of the accuracy curve. Where's the "cliff," and what does it represent?

Show solution

The accuracy stays almost flat as sparsity rises through 50%, 60%, 70% — you're deleting weights that were doing nothing — then drops steeply (the "cliff") somewhere past ~80%. The cliff is the point where you've exhausted the truly-redundant weights and started cutting into the ones that actually carry the network's function. Before the cliff, pruning is nearly free; after it, each additional deletion removes something the model needs. Finding and sitting just before that cliff (and fine-tuning to push it further right) is the whole practical goal of pruning — maximum compression for minimum accuracy loss.

2. (transfer) "Iterative" pruning (prune a little, fine-tune, repeat) reaches much higher sparsity than pruning it all at once. Why does the fine-tuning step help so much?

Show solution

When you remove weights, the surviving ones were optimized to work alongside the deleted ones, so their values are now slightly off for the new, smaller network — causing an accuracy dip. Fine-tuning lets the survivors readjust to compensate for the removed connections, redistributing the lost weight's function among themselves and recovering the accuracy. Doing this gradually (small prune, fine-tune, repeat) keeps the network near a good solution at every step, so it never has to recover from a large shock — whereas one-shot pruning to high sparsity throws away so much at once that the remaining weights can't recover. It's the same principle as gentle continuation methods in optimization: take small steps and re-optimize, rather than one big destructive jump. This is why state-of-the-art pruning reaches 90%+ sparsity with minimal loss.

3. (stretch) Pruning, distillation, and quantization each shrink models. Sketch how you'd combine all three to deploy a huge model on a phone.

Show solution

Stack them, since they attack different redundancies. (1) Distill the huge teacher into a smaller student architecture that captures most of its accuracy (Lesson 0) — this picks a fundamentally smaller model. (2) Prune the student to remove its redundant weights, fine-tuning iteratively to reach high sparsity (this lesson) — fewer weights to store and, with structured pruning, fewer to compute. (3) Quantize the remaining weights to 8-bit or lower (Lesson 2) — each surviving weight now takes a quarter of the memory and runs on fast integer arithmetic. The savings multiply: a distilled model might be 5× smaller, pruning another 3×, quantization another 4×, compounding to an enormous reduction while keeping most of the accuracy. Real mobile deployment pipelines do exactly this, plus hardware-aware tweaks. The lesson of the whole track is that these techniques are complementary tools — you rarely rely on just one to fit a big model into a small device.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Pruning is standard in model deployment, shrinking networks for phones, embedded devices, and cheaper cloud serving, and it's built into toolkits like PyTorch and TensorFlow. The frontier includes structured and hardware-aware pruning (removing whole channels or attention heads for real speedups), pruning large language models (where even a few percent of memory matters at scale), and the theoretical thread from the Lottery Ticket Hypothesis about why sparse subnetworks are so capable. Pruning also connects to a bigger question this track keeps circling: modern models are wildly over-parameterized, and understanding which parameters truly matter is central both to making models efficient and to understanding how they work at all. The fact that you can throw away most of a network and lose almost nothing is one of deep learning's most useful — and most revealing — surprises.
