Knowledge distillation: teaching a small model to think big

The best models are enormous and expensive to run. Distillation trains a small, cheap "student" to mimic a big "teacher" — not just its answers, but its full confidence over every class — capturing most of the accuracy at a fraction of the cost.

The best-performing models — huge neural networks, giant language models — are wonderful in the lab but painful in the real world: too big for a phone, too slow for real-time, too costly to serve to millions. This track is about making models efficient without throwing away what makes them good, and the first technique is one of the most elegant: knowledge distillation. The idea is to train a small, fast student model to imitate a large, accurate teacher model. The twist that makes it work is what the student imitates. A normal training label is a single hard answer — "this image is a cat" — one bit of information. But the teacher, when it looks at that cat, outputs a whole distribution: "80% cat, 15% dog, 5% fox." That distribution is far richer — it reveals that this cat looks a bit dog-like and not at all like a car, encoding the teacher's learned sense of how classes relate. Hinton called this the dark knowledge. By training the student to match these soft probabilities (not just the hard label), you transfer the teacher's nuanced understanding, and the small student learns far better than it could from the raw labels alone. This lesson builds distillation and shows the student closing the gap to its teacher.

💡 Intuition: learn from the teacher's hesitation

Imagine two ways to learn from an expert. In the first, they only ever tell you the final answer: "cat." In the second, they think aloud: "definitely an animal, almost certainly a cat, though the ears are a little dog-like, and it's nothing like a vehicle." The second is vastly more instructive — you learn not just what but how the expert sees the space of possibilities, including which mistakes are reasonable and which are absurd. Distillation gives the student the expert's think-aloud. The teacher's soft probabilities say "these two classes are easy to confuse, those two never" — information the one-hot label completely discards. A student trained on this learns a smoother, better-informed decision boundary from the same data.

To make the teacher's hesitation visible, we soften its output with a temperature \(T\) in the softmax — higher \(T\) spreads the probability out, revealing the small "dark knowledge" values:

\[ p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_j \exp(z_j / T)}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{student}} = \text{match the teacher's soft } p \ \ (\text{plus, optionally, the true labels}) \] (0.1)

Read it: the teacher's logits \(z_i\) (raw scores) are divided by a temperature \(T\) before the softmax. At \(T=1\) you get the usual sharp probabilities; at higher \(T\), the exponentials are pulled together, so the runner-up classes get visibly nonzero probability — exposing the relationships the teacher learned. The student is trained to reproduce these softened probabilities. That's the whole method: soften the teacher, and have the student match it.

✍️ Worked example: the student beats hard labels

Teacher. A big MLP trained on lots of data reaches 0.920 accuracy. Accurate, but bigger than we want to deploy.
2

🛠️ From scratch: distillation

teacher = big_model.fit(lots_of_data)                # the expensive, accurate model
soft = teacher.prob(X_train, T=3.0)                  # softened probabilities (dark knowledge)

student = tiny_model()
student.fit(X_train, target=soft, soft=True)         # match the teacher's distribution...
# ...instead of student.fit(X_train, target=hard_labels)   # ...one-hot labels

# distilled student 0.920  >  hard-label student 0.909   (matches the teacher)

The only change from ordinary training is the target: the teacher's soft probability vector for each input, instead of a one-hot label. The student's loss becomes "make my distribution look like the teacher's," which carries strictly more information than "get the top class right."

This is how big models get deployed. Distillation is everywhere in practice. DistilBERT is a distilled version of BERT that keeps ~97% of the accuracy at 40% of the size and 60% faster. Countless production systems train a giant model for accuracy, then distill it into a small one for serving. It's also used to compress ensembles (distill many models into one) and, increasingly, to transfer capabilities from huge language models into smaller, cheaper ones. When you use a fast on-device model that seems surprisingly smart, there's a good chance a much bigger teacher taught it.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Training the student on hard labels only. That throws away the dark knowledge — the whole point. Match the teacher's full distribution.
  • Using temperature 1. At \(T=1\) the teacher's output is often nearly one-hot, hiding the relational info. A higher \(T\) during distillation exposes it (then you deploy the student at \(T=1\)).
  • Expecting the student to exceed the teacher. Distillation transfers the teacher's knowledge; the student usually approaches but doesn't surpass it (though it can beat a same-size model trained from scratch).
  • Forgetting the student can also use the true labels. The best recipes combine matching the teacher's soft targets and fitting the real labels — soft for knowledge, hard for ground truth.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Distillation trains a small student to mimic a big teacher — capturing most of its accuracy cheaply.
  • Match the teacher's soft probabilities (the "dark knowledge" of class relationships), not just the hard label.
  • Temperature \(T\) softens the softmax (eq 0.1) to expose the small runner-up probabilities during distillation.
  • The soft targets carry more information than one-hot labels, so the student learns better from the same data.
  • Widely deployed (e.g., DistilBERT: ~97% of BERT's accuracy, 40% smaller, 60% faster).

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, raise the temperature and watch the probability bars. What information appears at high T that was invisible at T=1, and why does the student want it?

Show solution

At T=1 the output is nearly one-hot — the top class dominates and the others are almost zero, so the display looks like a hard label. As T rises, the runner-up classes grow to visibly nonzero values, revealing the teacher's relative sense of the classes: this "cat" is somewhat dog-like (moderate dog probability) but not at all car-like (near-zero). The student wants exactly this because it encodes the structure of the problem — which classes are neighbours and which are far apart — turning each training example into a rich lesson about the whole class space rather than a single yes/no. Matching those graded targets shapes a smoother, better-informed decision boundary, which is why distilled students beat same-size students trained on hard labels.

2. (transfer) Distillation, pruning, and quantization all shrink models but work differently. In one line each, how does distillation differ from the other two?

Show solution

Distillation trains a new, smaller model from scratch to imitate a big one — it changes the architecture entirely (a different, smaller network). Pruning (next lesson) removes weights from an existing trained model, making it sparse but keeping the same architecture skeleton. Quantization (Lesson 2) reduces the numerical precision of the existing weights (e.g., 32-bit → 8-bit) without changing the architecture or which weights exist. So: distillation = new small model that learns the teacher's behaviour; pruning = delete unimportant weights; quantization = store each weight in fewer bits. They're complementary and often combined — distill to a small model, then prune it, then quantize it — stacking their savings.

3. (stretch) Modern large language models are often improved by training on outputs generated by an even larger model. How is that a form of distillation, and what's one risk?

Show solution

It's distillation at the level of behaviour: instead of matching a probability vector over a few classes, the small (student) model is trained on the text a bigger (teacher) model generates — its answers, reasoning traces, or preferences — transferring the teacher's capabilities into a cheaper model. This is how many smaller open models acquire surprisingly strong abilities, and how techniques like reasoning-trace distillation work. The main risk is that the student inherits the teacher's errors and biases — including confidently-stated falsehoods (hallucinations) — because it's trained to imitate the teacher rather than to be correct against ground truth; if the teacher is wrong, the student faithfully learns to be wrong too, and errors can even amplify across generations of distillation ("model collapse" when models train on each other's synthetic output). So teacher quality is paramount, and mixing in real, verified data (ground-truth labels) guards against the student simply cloning the teacher's mistakes. It's the same caveat as the hard-vs-soft-label balance, scaled up to whole behaviours.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Distillation is a workhorse of practical AI deployment: it's how you take a state-of-the-art model and make it small and fast enough to run on a phone, in a browser, or cheaply at scale. DistilBERT, TinyBERT, and a whole family of distilled models power real products; distillation compresses ensembles and transfers skills between models. At the frontier, distilling from very large language models into smaller ones — including reasoning abilities and alignment behaviours — is an active and commercially important area, as is understanding why soft targets help (they act partly as a regularizer and partly as a richer signal). The broader lesson opens this track: the biggest model isn't the one you have to deploy. With distillation and the compression techniques that follow, you can keep most of the intelligence at a fraction of the cost — which is what makes powerful AI practical.

It's how models like DistilBERT keep ~97% of the accuracy at 40% of the size."
