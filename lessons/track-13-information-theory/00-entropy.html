Entropy: the measure of uncertainty

“Cross-entropy loss” and “KL divergence” turn up all over machine learning — in every classifier, in VAEs, in RLHF. This track builds the theory behind those names, and it all starts with one quantity: entropy, the amount of surprise in a distribution.

Track 3 · Lesson 0 Concept + code + interactive ~22 min Builds on: Track 1

Information theory, invented by Claude Shannon in 1948, is the mathematical language of uncertainty — and it quietly underpins most of machine learning. The loss functions you minimize, the regularizers in generative models, the objective in RLHF, even how models are evaluated (perplexity) are all information-theoretic. This short appendix track makes that foundation explicit, beginning with the atom of it all: entropy.

💡 Concept: surprise, and its average

Start with a single outcome. How surprising is it to observe something with probability \(p\)? A sure thing (\(p=1\)) is no surprise; a rare event (\(p\) tiny) is very surprising. Shannon’s measure of surprise, or information content, is

\[ \text{surprise}(x) = -\log_2 p(x) \quad\text{bits}. \] (0.1)

The logarithm makes surprise additive for independent events (two coin flips carry twice the information of one), and base 2 gives the unit of bits — the number of yes/no questions needed to identify the outcome. Entropy is just the average surprise of a distribution:

\[ H(X) = \mathbb{E}[-\log_2 p(x)] = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x) \quad\text{bits}. \] (0.2)

🛠️ From scratch: entropy in one line

def entropy(p):
    p = p[p > 0]                       # 0·log0 = 0 by convention
    return -(p * np.log2(p)).sum()     # average surprise, in bits

The numbers tell a story. A fair coin is exactly \(1\) bit; a biased coin \([0.9,0.1]\) is only \(0.47\) bits (more predictable, less uncertainty); a fair die is \(\log_2 6 = 2.585\) bits. And English text: if letters were uniform, \(\log_2 26 = 4.70\) bits each, but real letter frequencies give \(4.18\) bits — the structure of language reduces its entropy, which is exactly why text is compressible (Lesson 4). Entropy is maximal for the uniform distribution and shrinks as a distribution becomes peaked.

Entropy is everywhere in ML. A model’s uncertainty is the entropy of its output distribution — a confident classifier has low output entropy. Decision trees (Track 12) split to reduce entropy. “Perplexity,” the standard language-model metric, is just \(2^{H}\) — the effective number of choices the model is deciding between. And the loss you actually minimize, cross-entropy (next lesson), is entropy’s close cousin. Get entropy, and a dozen scattered ML ideas snap into one framework.

✍️ Worked example: entropy of coins and dice

Entropy is just the average surprise, so let's compute a few by hand.

1
Fair coin. Two outcomes at \(p=0.5\). \(H=-(0.5\log_2 0.5 + 0.5\log_2 0.5) = -(0.5\cdot(-1)+0.5\cdot(-1)) = 1\) bit. One yes/no question's worth of uncertainty.
2
Biased coin (\(p=0.9\)). \(H=-(0.9\log_2 0.9 + 0.1\log_2 0.1) \approx -(0.9\cdot(-0.152)+0.1\cdot(-3.32)) = 0.47\) bits. Less uncertain — you can usually guess "heads".
3
Fair 4-sided die. Four outcomes at \(0.25\). \(H=-4\cdot(0.25\log_2 0.25)=4\cdot 0.25\cdot 2 = 2\) bits — the maximum for 4 outcomes (two yes/no questions).
4
The pattern. Entropy is largest when outcomes are equally likely (\(\log_2 N\) bits for \(N\) equal outcomes) and zero when one outcome is certain. It's literally the average number of yes/no questions needed to pin down the outcome.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Surprise \(= -\log_2 p(x)\): rare events are informative; certain events carry zero information.
  • Entropy \(H(X) = -\sum_x p(x)\log_2 p(x)\): the average surprise, in bits — the irreducible uncertainty.
  • Maximal for uniform (\(\log_2 n\) for \(n\) equally-likely outcomes), zero for a certainty.
  • Structure lowers entropy — and lower entropy means more compressible (Lesson 4).
  • Base 2 → bits = average yes/no questions to identify the outcome; perplexity \(=2^H\).

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Confusing surprise with probability. Surprise is \(-\log p\), not \(p\). A rare event (small \(p\)) has large surprise; entropy averages that surprise, weighted by \(p\).
  • Forgetting the units. \(\log_2\) gives bits; the natural log \(\ln\) gives nats. ML code usually uses nats (natural log); the idea is identical, only the scale differs.
  • Thinking entropy measures a single outcome. The surprise of one outcome is \(-\log p(x)\); entropy is the average over the whole distribution — a property of the distribution, not any one event.
  • Assuming more outcomes always means more entropy. A 1000-outcome distribution with all mass on one value has entropy 0. It's the spread, not the count, that matters.

Q1. Which distribution over 4 outcomes has the highest entropy?

  • [1, 0, 0, 0]
    A certainty has zero entropy — no surprise at all.
  • [0.7, 0.2, 0.1, 0]
    Peaked distributions have low entropy; this is far from maximal.
  • [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]
    Correct. Entropy is maximized by the uniform distribution — all outcomes equally likely means maximum uncertainty, here \(\log_2 4 = 2\) bits.
  • [0.5, 0.5, 0, 0]
    This is 1 bit (like a fair coin over two of the four); the uniform over all four is higher (2 bits).

Q2. Why is surprise defined with a logarithm, \(-\log p\)?

  • So information from independent events adds (and certainty gives zero).
    Correct. For independent events \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\), and \(-\log p(x,y) = -\log p(x) - \log p(y)\): the log turns multiplying probabilities into adding information, matching the intuition that two independent flips carry twice the information.
  • To keep probabilities between 0 and 1.
    Probabilities are already in [0,1]; the log is about additivity, not bounding.
  • Because logs are faster to compute.
    Speed isn’t the reason; the additive property is.
  • To make entropy negative.
    Entropy is non-negative; the minus sign in \(-\log p\) keeps surprise positive.

Q3. English letters have entropy ~4.18 bits vs 4.70 for uniform. What does the gap mean?

  • English uses more than 26 letters.
    It’s the standard 26; the gap is about their frequencies, not the count.
  • The calculation is wrong.
    It’s correct — non-uniform frequencies always give entropy below the uniform maximum.
  • Letters are independent.
    They’re not (‘q’ is usually followed by ‘u’), but that’s a separate, further reduction; the 4.18 already comes just from unequal single-letter frequencies.
  • The uneven letter frequencies make English more predictable than random, hence more compressible.
    Correct. Because ‘e’ is common and ‘z’ rare, each letter carries less than the maximal information; that structure is exactly what compression exploits (Lesson 4).

Q4. A language model’s “perplexity” is \(2^{H}\) where \(H\) is its cross-entropy. Intuitively, perplexity is…

  • the number of parameters.
    Perplexity is about predictive uncertainty, not model size.
  • the effective number of equally-likely choices the model is deciding among at each step.
    Correct. If entropy is \(H\) bits, that’s equivalent to choosing uniformly among \(2^H\) options; lower perplexity means the model has narrowed the possibilities — a sharper predictive distribution.
  • the training time in hours.
    Unrelated to time; it’s a measure of prediction quality.
  • always equal to the vocabulary size.
    A random model has perplexity ≈ vocab size, but a good model’s is far lower — that’s the point of the metric.

✍️ Practice

1. Compute by hand the entropy of a biased coin with \(p(\text{heads})=0.25\). Then verify with entropy.py.

Show solution

\(H = -0.25\log_2 0.25 - 0.75\log_2 0.75\). Now \(-\log_2 0.25 = 2\) and \(-\log_2 0.75 \approx 0.415\), so \(H \approx 0.25(2) + 0.75(0.415) = 0.5 + 0.311 = 0.811\) bits. It’s below the fair-coin value of 1 bit (the coin is more predictable) but above the near-certain cases — matching the entropy curve, which is symmetric and peaks at \(p=0.5\). Doing one by hand cements that entropy is a weighted average of surprises, with each outcome contributing \(p\times(-\log_2 p)\).

2. A decision tree (Track 12) can split using entropy instead of Gini. Explain, in information terms, what a good split does.

Show solution

A good split reduces entropy: it partitions the data so that each child is more pure (lower label entropy) than the parent. The reduction in entropy from a split is called information gain — literally how many bits of uncertainty about the label the split removes. The tree greedily chooses the split with the highest information gain, which is the same idea as Gini reduction, just measured in bits. This reframes tree-building as an information-theoretic process: each question is chosen to be maximally informative about the class, exactly like an optimal game of twenty questions.

3. Conceptual: why can’t any lossless compressor represent English text in fewer than ~4.18 bits per letter (using single-letter statistics)?

Show solution

Shannon’s source coding theorem says entropy is a hard floor: you cannot losslessly encode a source in fewer bits per symbol than its entropy, on average, without losing information. If each letter is drawn with the given frequencies, ~4.18 bits/letter is the theoretical minimum for a code that treats letters independently — try to go lower and some messages become impossible to decode uniquely. (Real compressors like gzip beat 4.18 by exploiting dependencies between letters — the entropy of English drops well below 2 bits/letter once you condition on context, which is precisely what a language model learns to do.) This is the deep link between prediction and compression made in Lesson 4.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Entropy is everywhere in ML. A language model's quality is reported as perplexity, which is just \(2^{H}\) (or \(e^{H}\)) of its predictions — lower entropy over the next word means a more confident, better model. Decision trees split on the feature that reduces entropy the most (information gain). Maximum-entropy modelling picks the least-committal distribution consistent with your constraints. And the cross-entropy loss you've minimized since your first classifier (next lesson) is entropy's most famous descendant. Shannon's 1948 measure of "surprise" turned out to be the right currency for almost everything about learning from data.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Entropy is the average surprise in a distribution, measured in bits: \(H=-\sum p\log_2 p\). It's the number of yes/no questions you'd need to pin down the outcome. Uniform distributions have the most entropy (maximum uncertainty); a certain outcome has zero. It's the foundation of cross-entropy loss, KL divergence, and perplexity — the whole information-theoretic backbone of ML."
