KL divergence: distance between distributions

You met KL divergence twice already — as the VAE’s regularizer and RLHF’s penalty — without deriving it. Here it is: the number of extra bits you pay for believing the wrong distribution, and the true target behind cross-entropy training.

Track 13 · Lesson 2 Concept + code ~24 min Builds on: Lessons 0–1, Tracks 6, 7

Entropy measured surprise; cross-entropy measured the surprise of a wrong belief. Their difference is the Kullback–Leibler divergence — the excess surprise from using \(q\) when the truth is \(p\). It is the closest thing information theory has to a “distance” between distributions, and it appears throughout ML: the VAE objective (Track 6), the RLHF penalty (Track 7), variational inference, and the real target of every cross-entropy loss.

💡 Concept: the extra bits of a wrong code

The KL divergence from \(p\) to \(q\) is

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p \,\|\, q) = \sum_x p(x)\,\log_2\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \;=\; \underbrace{H(p,q)}_{\text{cross-entropy}} - \underbrace{H(p)}_{\text{entropy}}. \] (2.1)

Read the second form: KL is exactly cross-entropy minus entropy — the gap between the cost of your belief \(q\) and the best possible cost \(H(p)\). Operationally, it’s the number of extra bits per symbol you waste by compressing data from \(p\) with a code designed for \(q\). It has two defining properties: it is always \(\ge 0\), and zero only when \(p=q\) (Gibbs’ inequality — the information-theoretic reason cross-entropy is minimized at \(q=p\)).

📐 Why the asymmetry matters

Because \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\neq\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\), the two directions encourage different behaviours — a subtlety that shapes real algorithms. Forward KL \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) is “mode-covering”: it heavily penalizes \(q\) being small where \(p\) is large, so \(q\) spreads to cover all of \(p\)’s mass (this is what maximum-likelihood / cross-entropy training minimizes). Reverse KL \(\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\) is “mode-seeking”: it lets \(q\) concentrate on one mode of \(p\) and ignore the rest (this is what variational inference and some RL objectives minimize). Choosing a direction is a real modelling decision.

You’ve already optimized KL — twice. The VAE (Track 6) added \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,I))\) to pull the latent code toward the prior. RLHF (Track 7) subtracted \(\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\) to keep the tuned policy near the reference. And since \(H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) and \(H(p)\) is a constant, every cross-entropy loss you’ve minimized was, underneath, minimizing \(\mathrm{KL}\) from your model to the data. KL is the quiet objective behind an astonishing amount of machine learning.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • KL divergence \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\sum_x p\log_2\frac{p}{q}\): the extra bits from using \(q\) instead of the true \(p\).
  • Identity: \(H(p,q) = H(p) + \mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) — cross-entropy = entropy + KL.
  • \(\mathrm{KL}\ge 0\), zero iff \(p=q\) (Gibbs). It’s a divergence, not a symmetric distance.
  • Asymmetric: forward KL is mode-covering (spreads \(q\)); reverse KL is mode-seeking (concentrates \(q\)).
  • It’s everywhere: the VAE regularizer, the RLHF penalty, and the true target of cross-entropy training.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. How does KL divergence relate to entropy and cross-entropy?

  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = H(p) + H(p,q)\).
    It’s a difference, not a sum. Cross-entropy is the larger quantity.
  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = H(q) - H(p)\).
    It involves the cross-entropy \(H(p,q)\), not the entropy of \(q\).
  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q) = H(p,q) - H(p)\) — cross-entropy minus entropy.
    Correct. KL is the excess of cross-entropy over the entropy floor: the extra bits your wrong belief \(q\) costs beyond the unavoidable \(H(p)\).
  • They are unrelated.
    They’re tightly linked by the identity \(H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\).

Q2. Which statement about KL divergence is TRUE?

  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\ge 0\), and equals 0 only when \(p=q\).
    Correct. This is Gibbs’ inequality: you always pay a non-negative penalty for the wrong distribution, and pay nothing only when your belief matches reality exactly.
  • KL can be negative if \(q\) is closer to uniform.
    KL is never negative, regardless of \(q\).
  • \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)=\mathrm{KL}(q\|p)\) always.
    It’s asymmetric in general — that’s a defining feature.
  • KL is a true distance metric.
    It fails symmetry and the triangle inequality, so it’s a divergence, not a metric.

Q3. Since \(H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) and \(H(p)\) is fixed by the data, minimizing cross-entropy is equivalent to…

  • maximizing entropy.
    Entropy \(H(p)\) is a constant here; you’re not changing it.
  • minimizing \(H(p)\).
    You can’t change \(H(p)\) — it belongs to the data distribution, not the model.
  • maximizing KL.
    You minimize KL, not maximize it — you want \(q\) close to \(p\).
  • minimizing \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) — pushing the model \(q\) onto the data \(p\).
    Correct. With \(H(p)\) constant, lowering cross-entropy lowers KL, i.e. drives your model’s distribution toward the true one. Every cross-entropy loss is a KL minimization in disguise.

Q4. Forward KL \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) is “mode-covering.” What does that mean?

  • \(q\) collapses onto a single mode of \(p\).
    That’s reverse KL (mode-seeking). Forward KL does the opposite.
  • \(q\) is pushed to put mass everywhere \(p\) has mass, spreading to cover all modes.
    Correct. Forward KL blows up when \(q\) is near zero where \(p\) is large, so it forces \(q\) to cover \(p\)’s support — even if that means putting mass between modes. This is what maximum-likelihood training does.
  • It ignores \(p\) entirely.
    It’s an average weighted by \(p\), so it very much depends on \(p\).
  • It makes KL symmetric.
    Mode-covering vs mode-seeking is precisely a consequence of the asymmetry, not a fix for it.

✍️ Practice

1. Run kl_divergence.py and confirm the identity \(H(p,q)=H(p)+\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) numerically. Then find a \(q\) for which \(\mathrm{KL}(p\|q)\) is huge — what makes it blow up?

Show solution

The identity holds exactly (the script shows \(1.16+0.27=1.42=\) cross-entropy). KL blows up when \(q(x)\) is (near) zero at some \(x\) where \(p(x)>0\): the term \(p(x)\log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\) → ∞ as \(q(x)\to0\). Intuitively, if your model assigns almost no probability to something that actually happens, you’re infinitely surprised and pay unboundedly many extra bits. This is why models must keep some probability mass everywhere plausible (and why we add a tiny \(\epsilon\) in code), and it’s the mode-covering pressure of forward KL in action — it cannot tolerate \(q\) ignoring any of \(p\)’s outcomes.

2. In the VAE (Track 6), the loss was reconstruction + \(\mathrm{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0,I))\). Explain, in bits, what that KL term is buying.

Show solution

The KL term measures how far the encoder’s latent distribution \(q_\phi(z\mid x)\) is from the standard-normal prior — the extra bits needed to describe the encoded codes relative to the prior. Minimizing it pulls the latent codes to look like samples from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), which is exactly what lets you later sample \(z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\) and decode to realistic data. In information terms, it keeps the latent representation compressible under the prior — a regularizer that trades a little reconstruction fidelity for a well-organized, samplable latent space. Now the VAE’s objective reads as “reconstruct well while keeping the latent code cheap to encode under the prior,” a purely information-theoretic statement.

3. Conceptual: RLHF minimizes reward while penalizing \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\). Why is KL (rather than, say, parameter distance) the right leash?

Show solution

KL measures distance in behaviour (the distributions over outputs), not in parameters — which is what we actually care about: we want the tuned policy to keep generating like the trusted reference, regardless of how its weights moved. Two very different weight settings can produce nearly identical output distributions (small KL) or vice-versa, so parameter distance is a poor proxy for “how much has the model’s behaviour changed.” Penalizing KL directly bounds how far the output distribution drifts, which is exactly the guardrail against reward-hacking (Track 7): it keeps the policy’s language close to the reference while still letting it chase reward. It’s the same reason KL, not L2 on weights, is the natural leash throughout probabilistic ML.
