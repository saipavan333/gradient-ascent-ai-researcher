Mutual information: how much X tells you about Y

Correlation measures only straight-line relationships and is blind to everything else. Mutual information measures any dependence — the true amount, in bits, that one variable reveals about another. It’s the honest way to ask “are these two things related?”

Suppose you want to know whether a feature is useful for predicting a label, or whether two signals are related. Correlation is the usual tool — but it only detects linear association and can report exactly zero for a perfect nonlinear relationship. Mutual information fixes this: it quantifies, in bits, how much knowing one variable reduces your uncertainty about another, capturing dependence of any shape.

💡 Concept: shared information

Mutual information is the KL divergence between the joint distribution \(p(x,y)\) and the product of the marginals \(p(x)p(y)\) — that is, between “how \(X\) and \(Y\) actually co-occur” and “how they’d co-occur if they were independent”:

\[ I(X;Y) = \sum_{x,y} p(x,y)\,\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)\,p(y)} \;=\; H(Y) - H(Y\mid X). \] (3.1)

The second form is the clearest: MI is how much the uncertainty about \(Y\) drops once you know \(X\) — the entropy of \(Y\) minus its entropy conditioned on \(X\). Because it’s a KL divergence, \(I(X;Y)\ge 0\), and it is zero exactly when \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent (the joint equals the product of marginals). Any dependence — linear, quadratic, periodic, whatever — makes the joint differ from the product and drives MI above zero.

🛠️ From scratch: estimating MI by binning

With samples rather than known distributions, estimate MI from a joint histogram, from mutual_information.py: 

counts = histogram2d(x, y, bins)          # estimate the joint p(x,y)
pxy = counts / counts.sum()
px, py = pxy.sum(axis=1), pxy.sum(axis=0)  # marginals
MI = sum( pxy * log2( pxy / (px⊗py) ) )    # KL(joint || product of marginals)
MI is the ideal feature-relevance score. When selecting features, you want the ones that carry information about the target — and MI measures exactly that, regardless of whether the relationship is linear. It also underlies the information bottleneck view of deep learning (a network should keep the information in its representation that’s relevant to the label, \(I(\text{repr};Y)\), while discarding the rest, \(I(\text{repr};X)\)), and the InfoNCE objective behind modern self-supervised/contrastive learning maximizes a bound on MI. Correlation is a linear shadow; mutual information is the real thing.

✍️ Worked example: what does knowing X buy you?

Mutual information \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)\): the uncertainty in Y, minus the uncertainty left after you learn X.

1
Independent. Two fair coins with no link. \(H(Y)=1\) bit and \(H(Y\mid X)=1\) bit (X tells you nothing). \(I=1-1=0\). No shared information.
2
Identical. \(Y=X\). \(H(Y)=1\) bit, but \(H(Y\mid X)=0\) (knowing X pins down Y). \(I=1-0=1\) bit — they share everything.
3
In between. A noisy link where they agree 85% of the time: \(H(Y\mid X)=H(0.85)\approx 0.61\) bits, so \(I\approx 0.39\) bits. Partial dependence, partial information.
4
Symmetry. \(I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)\) — information is mutual: X tells you exactly as much about Y as Y tells you about X. Unlike KL, this one is symmetric.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • MI \(I(X;Y)=\sum p(x,y)\log_2\frac{p(x,y)}{p(x)p(y)} = H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)\): the reduction in uncertainty about \(Y\) from knowing \(X\).
  • \(I(X;Y)\ge 0\), zero iff \(X\perp Y\) (it’s the KL between joint and product of marginals).
  • Detects ANY dependence, linear or not — unlike correlation, which sees only straight lines.
  • Uses: feature selection, the information bottleneck, and contrastive learning (InfoNCE bounds MI).
  • Estimate from samples via a joint histogram (with care in high dimensions).

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Confusing MI with correlation. Correlation catches only linear relationships; mutual information catches any dependence, including nonlinear ones correlation would score as zero.
  • Thinking zero MI is rare. \(I(X;Y)=0\) exactly when X and Y are independent — a strong, precise condition, and the cleanest definition of independence there is.
  • Forgetting MI is symmetric and non-negative. \(I(X;Y)=I(Y;X)\ge 0\), unlike the asymmetric KL it's built from.
  • Estimating MI from little data. MI is notoriously hard to estimate for continuous or high-dimensional variables — naive histogram estimates are badly biased. It's an active research problem.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. Mutual information \(I(X;Y)\) is zero exactly when…

  • \(X\) and \(Y\) are perfectly correlated.
    Perfect correlation means strong dependence — MI is large there, not zero.
  • \(X\) and \(Y\) are independent.
    Correct. MI is the KL between the joint and the product of marginals; that’s zero precisely when \(p(x,y)=p(x)p(y)\), the definition of independence.
  • \(X\) equals \(Y\).
    If \(X=Y\), knowing one tells you the other completely — maximal MI, not zero.
  • the entropy of \(X\) is zero.
    That’s a property of \(X\) alone; MI is about the relationship between the two variables.

Q2. For \(Y=X^2\) with \(X\) symmetric around 0, correlation is ≈ 0 but MI is clearly positive. Why?

  • MI is miscalculated.
    It’s correct — the discrepancy is the whole point.
  • Correlation is always larger than MI.
    Here correlation is smaller (≈0); they measure different things.
  • They must always agree.
    They often disagree — correlation sees only linear structure.
  • Correlation only captures linear association, but knowing \(X\) still determines \(|Y|\), so real dependence exists — and MI captures it.
    Correct. The U-shape has no linear trend (positive and negative \(X\) both give large \(Y\)), so correlation cancels to ≈ 0; but \(X\) clearly informs \(Y\), and MI — sensitive to any dependence — reports it.

Q3. The form \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)\) says MI is…

  • the reduction in uncertainty about \(Y\) once you know \(X\).
    Correct. \(H(Y)\) is your uncertainty about \(Y\); \(H(Y\mid X)\) is what remains after learning \(X\); their difference is how many bits \(X\) told you about \(Y\).
  • the total entropy of \(X\) and \(Y\).
    That would be the joint entropy \(H(X,Y)\), a different quantity.
  • always equal to \(H(Y)\).
    Only if \(X\) fully determines \(Y\) (then \(H(Y\mid X)=0\)); in general MI is smaller.
  • the correlation coefficient in bits.
    MI is not a rescaled correlation; it captures nonlinear dependence correlation can’t.

Q4. Why is MI a better feature-selection score than correlation?

  • It’s faster to compute.
    MI is usually more expensive (needs joint density estimation); its advantage is what it detects.
  • It only keeps linear features.
    The opposite — correlation is the linear-only one; MI keeps features with any relationship.
  • It detects a feature’s relevance even when the relationship is nonlinear, which correlation would rate as useless.
    Correct. A feature related to the target through a curve or interaction can have ~0 correlation but high MI; ranking by MI keeps such genuinely informative features that a correlation filter would wrongly discard.
  • It ignores the target.
    MI is computed between feature and target; it centrally uses the target.

✍️ Practice

1. Run mutual_information.py. Then reduce the number of bins to 2 and raise it to 50. How does the MI estimate change, and why is bin count tricky?

Show solution

Too few bins (2) blur the relationship and under-estimate MI (you can’t see structure finer than the bins). Too many bins (50) with limited data over-estimate MI, because each cell has few samples and random coincidences look like dependence — even independent variables show spurious MI. This bias is the central difficulty of MI estimation from samples: the histogram approach is sensitive to binning and to dimensionality. Practical estimators (k-NN based, like KSG) reduce this bias, but the lesson stands — MI is powerful but must be estimated carefully, and a single number should be sanity-checked against the independent baseline (which should read ≈ 0).

2. Show that if \(X\) fully determines \(Y\) (a deterministic function), then \(I(X;Y) = H(Y)\).

Show solution

Use \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y\mid X)\). If \(Y=f(X)\) is a deterministic function of \(X\), then once you know \(X\) there is no remaining uncertainty about \(Y\): \(H(Y\mid X)=0\). Therefore \(I(X;Y)=H(Y)\) — knowing \(X\) tells you everything about \(Y\), so the information shared equals all of \(Y\)’s entropy. This is the upper end of MI: it’s bounded above by \(\min(H(X),H(Y))\), reaching \(H(Y)\) exactly when \(X\) determines \(Y\). It also shows MI’s natural scale — it can’t exceed the information content of the variables involved.

3. Conceptual: the “information bottleneck” says a good representation \(Z\) of input \(X\) for predicting \(Y\) should maximize \(I(Z;Y)\) while minimizing \(I(Z;X)\). Interpret this.

Show solution

It formalizes “keep what matters, throw away the rest.” Maximizing \(I(Z;Y)\) means the representation should retain all information relevant to the label \(Y\) (be predictive). Minimizing \(I(Z;X)\) means it should not carry unnecessary detail about the raw input \(X\) (be compressed) — forgetting nuisances like background pixels or exact phrasing. A good representation is thus a minimal sufficient statistic for the task: maximally informative about the answer, minimally cluttered by the input. This information-theoretic lens explains why compression and generalization are linked, and connects directly to why learned embeddings (Track 11) discard surface form while keeping meaning — the same principle running from Shannon to modern deep learning.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Mutual information is a deep idea with wide reach. Feature selection keeps the inputs with the highest MI to the target. The "information bottleneck" theory frames learning as squeezing inputs through a representation that keeps MI with the label while dropping MI with the noise. Self-supervised methods like contrastive learning (Track 13) can be read as maximising a lower bound on mutual information between two views of the data. And estimating MI between high-dimensional variables with neural networks (MINE and successors) is an active frontier — because "how much does one thing tell you about another" is one of the most fundamental questions you can ask of data.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Mutual information \(I(X;Y)\) is how many bits of uncertainty about Y disappear once you know X — the shared information between two variables. It's zero exactly when they're independent, maximal when one determines the other, and symmetric. Unlike correlation it catches nonlinear dependence too, which is why it underlies feature selection, the information bottleneck, and contrastive self-supervised learning."
