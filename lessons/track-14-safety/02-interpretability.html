Mechanistic interpretability

A trained network is a black box of millions of numbers. Interpretability is the science of prying it open — and the simplest tool, a linear probe, already reveals something profound: you can read a concept straight out of a hidden layer.

If a model is going to be trusted with real decisions, “it usually works” isn’t enough — we want to know how and why it works, and whether it’s reasoning for the right reasons. Mechanistic interpretability aims to reverse-engineer the computations inside a network into human-understandable parts. It’s a young, fast-moving field, and you can run its foundational tool — the linear probe — from scratch today.

💡 Concept: read the representation with a probe

A network transforms its input through hidden layers into representations — vectors of activations. The core question of interpretability is: what do those representations encode? A linear probe answers a sharp version of it. Freeze the trained network, take the activations of some hidden layer, and train a simple linear classifier to predict a concept from them. If the probe succeeds, that concept is linearly decodable — the layer represents it in a directly readable way. If the probe does no better than chance, the layer doesn’t encode it.

🛠️ From scratch: probe a hidden layer

Train the network on its task, freeze it, extract hidden activations, then fit a linear probe for each candidate concept, from probing.py: 

net = train_mlp(X, y)                 # train on the XOR-of-signs task, then FREEZE
h = hidden(net, X)                    # the hidden-layer activations

linear_probe(h, sign_of_x1)  # -> 0.99   the layer linearly encodes this (task needs it)
linear_probe(h, sign_of_x2)  # -> 0.99   ... and this
linear_probe(h, random_label)# -> 0.51   ... but not noise (chance)

The contrast is the whole point: the probe finds the concepts the task actually required and not spurious ones. Scaled up, this is how interpretability researchers discover that language models have directions for sentiment, truthfulness, or a specific syntactic role; that vision models build up edges → textures → object parts; and that some neurons act as recognizable “feature detectors.” The frontier goes further — identifying circuits (groups of neurons implementing an algorithm) and untangling superposition (many features packed into few neurons) — but it all starts with “what is linearly readable here?”

Why interpretability is a safety tool. If you can read what a model represents, you can catch failures that behaviour alone hides: a model that gives right answers for wrong reasons, a hidden “deception” or “I’m being tested” feature, or a spurious shortcut (predicting “wolf” from snow in the background). Probes and their descendants let you audit a model’s internals, not just its outputs — complementing the black-box evaluation of the next lesson. Understanding the machine is part of trusting it.

✍️ Worked example: from neurons to circuits

Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers the algorithm a network learned, one component at a time.

1
Probe a component. Feed many inputs and record when a neuron or attention head activates. A head that fires on "the word before" is a previous-token head.
2
Find circuits. Components compose. "Induction heads" pair a previous-token head with a copy head to continue repeated patterns (…A B … A → B) — a concrete mechanism behind in-context learning.
3
Test by intervention. Don't just correlate — ablate the component (zero it out) or patch in a different activation and see if the behaviour breaks. Causal tests separate "involved" from "merely present."
4
The polysemantic snag. Real neurons often fire for several unrelated things ("superposition"), so clean one-neuron-one-concept stories are rare — which is why sparse autoencoders are used to pull apart overlapping features.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers a network’s internal computations into human-understandable parts.
  • Linear probe: freeze the model, train a linear classifier on a hidden layer’s activations to test if a concept is linearly decodable.
  • High probe accuracy = the layer represents that concept; chance = it doesn’t.
  • Frontier ideas: feature detectors, circuits (neuron-groups implementing algorithms), and superposition (many features, few neurons).
  • A safety tool: auditing internals catches right-answer-wrong-reason failures and hidden features that behaviour alone conceals.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Trusting attention as explanation. Where a model attends isn't the same as why it decided — attention weights are suggestive, not a full account. Confirm with interventions.
  • Assuming one neuron = one concept. Superposition means neurons are usually polysemantic. Reading a single neuron's "meaning" off a few examples is a classic trap.
  • Correlation without causation. A component lighting up during a behaviour doesn't prove it causes it. Ablation and activation patching are what turn a story into evidence.
  • Cherry-picking inputs. A neat pattern on ten hand-picked examples can vanish on the full distribution. Test broadly before believing an explanation.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. A linear probe on a hidden layer achieves high accuracy for concept C. This means…

  • the network was trained to output C.
    Not necessarily — the probe can find concepts the network represents internally even if C wasn’t the training target.
  • C causes the network’s predictions.
    Decodability shows C is represented, not that it’s causally used — a subtle but important distinction (correlation in the representation).
  • C is linearly decodable from that layer — the layer represents C in a readable way.
    Correct. A successful linear probe means the concept is present as a linear direction in the activations; the layer encodes it accessibly.
  • the network is overfitting.
    Probe success is about representation content, not about generalization/overfitting of the base model.

Q2. Why train the probe as a linear classifier rather than another deep network?

  • So that success means the concept is directly (linearly) accessible, not that the probe itself computed it.
    Correct. A powerful probe could learn to extract almost anything, confounding the test. A linear probe can only read what’s already laid out linearly, so its success is evidence about the representation, not the probe.
  • Because linear models are more accurate.
    They’re usually less accurate; simplicity is the point, not raw accuracy.
  • To train the base network faster.
    The base network is frozen; the probe doesn’t affect its training.
  • Because deep probes can’t be trained.
    They can be — but a strong probe would defeat the purpose by extracting concepts the layer doesn’t plainly represent.

Q3. In the demo, a random label probes at ~50%. Why is that the desired control result?

  • It proves the network is broken.
    It proves the opposite — the probe correctly finds nothing where there’s nothing.
  • It shows the layer encodes everything.
    If it encoded everything, even random labels would probe high; ~50% shows it doesn’t.
  • Random labels are easy.
    They’re impossible to predict (by construction), which is exactly why the probe hits chance.
  • It confirms the probe reports chance for concepts that aren’t represented, so its high scores elsewhere are meaningful.
    Correct. A good control (unrelated concept → chance) validates the method: the probe isn’t spuriously succeeding, so its ~99% on the real concepts genuinely reflects the representation.

Q4. How does interpretability complement behavioural evaluation for safety?

  • It replaces the need for any testing.
    It complements, not replaces, output testing; both are needed.
  • It can reveal internal failures — right answers for wrong reasons, or hidden features — that outputs alone don’t show.
    Correct. A model can produce correct outputs on your tests while relying on a spurious shortcut or harboring a concerning internal feature; reading its representations can surface what behaviour conceals.
  • It measures accuracy more precisely.
    Interpretability inspects internals; it isn’t an accuracy metric.
  • It makes the model faster.
    Interpretability is about understanding, not performance.

✍️ Practice

1. In probing.py, probe the raw input \(x\) instead of the hidden layer for sign(x1). Then probe a network trained on a task that doesn’t need x2 for sign(x2). What do you expect?

Show solution

Probing the raw input for sign(x1) trivially succeeds — the information is right there — so a probe on inputs is a baseline, not a discovery. The interesting case is the hidden layer: it succeeds only if the network chose to preserve that concept. If you train a network on a task that ignores x2, its hidden layer may discard x2 (an information bottleneck, Track 13), and the sign(x2) probe drops toward chance — showing the representation keeps task-relevant information and forgets the rest. This is exactly what makes probing informative: it reveals what a network decided to represent, which reflects what it learned to care about.

2. A probe finding a concept is decodable does not prove the model uses it. Design a follow-up experiment (in words) to test causal use.

Show solution

Move from correlation to intervention: edit the representation and see if behaviour changes. Concretely, find the probe’s direction for the concept, then add or subtract that direction from the hidden activations (an “activation patch” or “steering”) and check whether the model’s output flips accordingly. If pushing along the “sentiment” direction reliably makes outputs more positive, the model is causally using that feature, not merely storing it. This intervention-based approach — activation patching, ablating specific neurons/directions, causal tracing — is how modern mechanistic interpretability establishes that a representation is actually part of the computation, echoing the ablation logic of Track 9 applied inside the network.

3. Conceptual: why is “superposition” (a network packing more features than it has neurons) both fascinating and a challenge for interpretability?

Show solution

Networks appear to represent far more distinct features than they have neurons by encoding them in overlapping combinations of neurons (superposition), exploiting the fact that real features are sparse (few active at once). It’s fascinating because it means a single neuron may participate in many unrelated concepts (“polysemantic” neurons), which is efficient. It’s a challenge because you can no longer read features off individual neurons — the clean “this neuron = this concept” story breaks, and you must disentangle features from mixtures (e.g., with sparse autoencoders that find an over-complete, more monosemantic feature basis). Superposition is a central obstacle current interpretability research is working to overcome, and it’s why simple probing is a first step, not the whole story.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Interpretability is the effort to make models auditable rather than trusted on faith — and it's advancing fast. Mechanistic interpretability has reverse-engineered real circuits (induction heads, indirect-object identification) and, with sparse autoencoders, extracted millions of human-interpretable features from frontier models, even steering behaviour by turning features up or down. Why it matters for safety: outputs alone can't tell you whether a model is being honest or just sounds honest — you have to look inside. The open frontier is scaling these methods to the largest models, handling superposition, and turning feature dictionaries into reliable safety audits.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Interpretability opens the black box to read what the parts compute. Attention heads specialise — previous-token heads, induction heads — and neurons can be probed, but you confirm a story by intervening (ablating or patching), not just correlating. The big complication is superposition: neurons are polysemantic, so sparse autoencoders help pull overlapping features apart. It matters because you can't tell an honest model from a plausible-sounding one by outputs alone."
