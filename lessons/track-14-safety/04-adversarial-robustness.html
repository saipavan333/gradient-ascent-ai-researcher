Adversarial robustness

A perturbation too small for a human to notice can turn a confident classifier’s answer into nonsense. Adversarial examples reveal that “high accuracy” and “robust understanding” are not the same thing — and you can craft one with the very gradients you used to train.

We close the curriculum with a humbling result. A model can reach 99% accuracy and still be brittle in a way no human is: add a tiny, carefully-chosen perturbation to an input — invisible or trivial to us — and the model confidently gives the wrong answer. These adversarial examples expose a gap between statistical accuracy and genuine robustness, and they’re built with the same gradients that trained the model. It’s the perfect final lesson: the tools of learning are also the tools of attack.

💡 Concept: attack along the gradient

Training moves the weights to reduce the loss. An adversarial attack instead moves the input to increase the loss — finding the smallest change to the input that pushes it across the decision boundary. The Fast Gradient Sign Method (FGSM) does this in one step: nudge each input feature by a small amount \(\varepsilon\) in the direction that increases the loss, i.e. the sign of the input gradient:

\[ x_{\text{adv}} = x + \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}\!\big(\nabla_x \,\text{loss}(x)\big). \] (4.1)

Why is a tiny \(\varepsilon\) enough? In high dimensions, a small nudge to every feature — each aligned with the gradient — adds up to a large change in the model’s score, even though no single feature moved much. The perturbation is small in magnitude but perfectly coordinated to fool the model.

🛠️ From scratch: fool a classifier

# trained logistic classifier (w, b); x is a confidently-correct point of class 1
# to lower P(class 1), step the input AGAINST the gradient direction:
x_adv = x - eps * np.sign(w)          # w is the input gradient of the logit
# small eps per feature -> P(class 1) collapses from 0.92 to below 0.5 (flips!)

The result is unsettling: \(\|\delta\|_2 \approx 1.3\) against \(\|x\|_2\approx 5.7\) — a small, structured push — flips a 92%-confident prediction to the wrong class. And these attacks have two properties that make them a real safety concern: they often transfer (an example crafted for one model fools another, even a different architecture), and they exist for essentially all standard models, including state-of-the-art image and text systems.

Defense is hard — and the arms race is instructive. The natural fix, adversarial training (add adversarial examples to the training set), helps but costs accuracy and can be beaten by stronger attacks. Many proposed defenses have fallen to adaptive attacks that target the specific defense. The deeper lesson is that our models learn decision boundaries that are accurate on natural data but have strange, exploitable geometry off the data manifold — a sign that statistical accuracy is not the same as human-like robustness. Closing that gap is an open problem, and a fitting note to end on: you now understand these systems deeply enough to see clearly what they still get wrong.

✍️ Worked example: building an adversarial example

The attack is simple calculus: move the input in the direction that most increases the loss.

1
Get the gradient. Compute \(\nabla_x L\) — how the loss changes with each input pixel. (Same backprop you use to train, but w.r.t. the input, not the weights.)
2
Take the sign. The Fast Gradient Sign Method nudges every pixel by \(\pm\epsilon\): \(x' = x + \epsilon\,\mathrm{sign}(\nabla_x L)\). Tiny per-pixel change, but aligned to do maximum damage.
3
The result. With \(\epsilon\) far below what the eye can see, a "panda" becomes a "gibbon" at 99% confidence. The image looks identical; the prediction flips.
4
Why it happens. High-dimensional decision boundaries pass surprisingly close to normal inputs, so a small step in the right direction crosses them. It's a property of the geometry, not a rare bug.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Adversarial example: a tiny, structured perturbation that flips a confident prediction to the wrong class.
  • FGSM: \(x_{\text{adv}} = x + \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(\nabla_x\text{loss})\) — attack by moving the input to increase loss.
  • High dimensions amplify it: many tiny, gradient-aligned nudges sum to a decisive change in the score.
  • Attacks transfer across models and exist for nearly all standard networks — a genuine safety/robustness concern.
  • Defense is hard: adversarial training helps at a cost; accuracy ≠ robustness, and closing the gap is open research.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking high accuracy means robust. A model can be 99% accurate on clean data and near 0% under attack. Standard accuracy says nothing about worst-case inputs.
  • Trusting "security by obscurity." Hiding the model doesn't help — attacks transfer between models, so an adversary can craft them on a surrogate and hit yours.
  • Believing a defence too soon. Many published defences broke under stronger, adaptive attacks. Evaluate against an attacker who knows your defence, not a fixed one.
  • Assuming it's only an image problem. Text, audio, and malware classifiers all have adversarial examples; jailbreak prompts are the LLM version of the same phenomenon.

Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. How does an FGSM adversarial attack differ from training?

  • It uses a different loss function.
    It uses the same loss; what changes is what is moved.
  • It has no gradients.
    It centrally uses gradients — of the loss with respect to the input.
  • Training moves the weights to lower the loss; the attack moves the input to raise it.
    Correct. Both use the loss gradient, but training descends it in weight space to fit; the attack ascends it in input space to break the model on a specific example.
  • It requires a bigger model.
    Model size is irrelevant to the attack mechanism.

Q2. Why can a tiny per-feature perturbation flip a confident prediction?

  • In high dimensions, small aligned nudges to many features sum to a large change in the model’s score.
    Correct. The logit is a dot product over many features; moving each slightly in the gradient direction accumulates into a big shift, even though every individual change is small.
  • Because the model has too few parameters.
    Adversarial examples afflict large models too; it’s about input dimensionality and boundary geometry, not parameter count.
  • Because the learning rate was too high.
    The attack happens after training; the learning rate isn’t involved.
  • Because the perturbation is actually large.
    It’s small per feature (and in norm relative to the input) — that’s exactly what makes it alarming.

Q3. That adversarial examples often “transfer” between models means…

  • they only work on the exact model they were made for.
    Transfer is the opposite — they generalize across models.
  • they require access to the model’s weights.
    Transfer is precisely what enables black-box attacks without the target’s weights.
  • they stop working after retraining.
    They frequently survive retraining and fool independently-trained models.
  • an example crafted for one model can fool another, even a different architecture — enabling black-box attacks.
    Correct. Transferability suggests models learn similar exploitable boundary geometry, and it lets an attacker fool a target model without ever seeing it — a serious practical concern.

Q4. What does the existence of adversarial examples tell us about “99% accurate” models?

  • The accuracy number is fake.
    The accuracy on natural data is real; robustness is a different property.
  • Statistical accuracy on natural data is not the same as robust, human-like understanding.
    Correct. A model can be highly accurate on typical inputs yet have brittle, exploitable decision boundaries off the data manifold — accuracy and robustness are distinct, and high accuracy doesn’t guarantee robustness.
  • The model is broken and useless.
    It’s useful on natural inputs; the concern is adversarial or shifted inputs, not everyday use.
  • Adversarial examples don’t affect real systems.
    They affect essentially all standard models, including deployed ones — hence the active research.

✍️ Practice

1. In adversarial.py, increase the feature dimension \(d\) from 40 to 200 (keep the data scale). Does a smaller \(\varepsilon\) now suffice to flip the prediction? Why?

Show solution

Yes — higher dimension makes the attack easier: a smaller per-feature \(\varepsilon\) flips the prediction, because there are more features to nudge and their aligned contributions to the logit add up over more terms. The change in the model’s score from FGSM scales roughly with \(\varepsilon\times\|w\|_1\), which grows with dimension, so the same disruption needs a smaller step per feature. This is a core reason adversarial examples are so potent for real inputs (images have hundreds of thousands of pixels): high dimensionality gives the attacker enormous, cheap leverage — an unintuitive consequence of the geometry of high-dimensional spaces.

2. Sketch how adversarial training would defend this classifier, and name its downside.

Show solution

Adversarial training augments the training set with adversarial examples: at each step, craft \(x_{\text{adv}}\) for the current model and train on it with the correct label, so the model learns to classify perturbed inputs correctly — effectively smoothing its decision boundary. It’s the most reliable known defense. The downsides: it’s more expensive (you generate attacks every step), it typically reduces clean accuracy (a robustness–accuracy trade-off), and it defends mainly against the attack type you trained on — a stronger or different attack can still break it. This cat-and-mouse dynamic, where defenses are repeatedly overcome by adaptive attacks, is why robustness remains unsolved and why evaluating a defense requires trying hard to break it (Lesson 3’s red-teaming mindset).

3. Capstone reflection: you’ve now built, from scratch, the mathematics, the models, and the safety failures of modern AI. In a few sentences, what does “understanding a model deeply” let you do that using one never could?

Show solution

Understanding lets you reason about why a system behaves as it does, predict how it will fail, and decide whether to trust it — none of which follow from calling an API. Because you derived attention, backprop, diffusion, RL, and these safety failures yourself, you can debug a training run from first principles, judge when a model is being over-optimized or gamed, design honest evaluations, craft and anticipate adversarial inputs, and read a new paper critically rather than taking it on faith. That is the difference between a user and a researcher: not access to bigger models, but the ability to explain, extend, and question them. You built the whole stack; now you can see clearly both what these systems can do and what they still get wrong — which is exactly where new research begins.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Adversarial examples (Szegedy et al. 2013; Goodfellow's FGSM 2014) exposed a deep fragility: models that match humans on average can fail catastrophically on tiny, targeted perturbations. A decade of attack-defence escalation later, adversarial training (train on adversarial examples) and certified defences give partial robustness, but a fully robust model on hard benchmarks remains open. The stakes are practical — self-driving perception, malware detection, content filters — and the idea generalises to today's frontier as jailbreaks: prompts engineered to bypass an LLM's safety training. Robustness is now a central safety concern, because a system is only as safe as its worst-case input, not its average one.
