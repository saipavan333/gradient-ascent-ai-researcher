Evaluations & red-teaming

A single accuracy number is a comforting lie. A model can score 90% overall and fail completely for a group of people who matter. Safety evaluation means refusing the average — disaggregating, stress-testing, and actively hunting for the failures a benchmark hides.

Track 23 · Lesson 3 Concept + code ~22 min Builds on: Track 22, Lessons 0–2

You know from Track 9 how to evaluate honestly on average. Safety demands more: an average can be excellent while the model is dangerous in exactly the cases that matter. Responsible AI development therefore treats evaluation as an adversarial search for failure — red-teaming — not a single leaderboard score. This lesson shows how badly averages can mislead, and what to do instead.

💡 Concept: the average hides the worst case

Aggregate metrics are weighted by frequency, so a large, easy majority can drown out a small, critical minority. A model that is perfect on 90% of inputs and completely wrong on the other 10% still reports 90% accuracy — a number that looks great and conceals a total failure. In safety-relevant settings (medical, legal, a demographic subgroup, a rare but dangerous input), it’s precisely the minority slice you cannot afford to get wrong.

🛠️ From scratch: disaggregate, then red-team

The demo builds data where a 90% majority follows one rule and a 10% minority follows the opposite; a single classifier nails the majority and fails the minority. The one-line fix in reporting changes everything: 

overall = accuracy(pred, y)                       # 0.90  -- the comforting headline
for g in groups:                                  # disaggregate by subgroup
    print(group_name[g], accuracy(pred[g], y[g])) # A: 1.00  |  B: 0.01  -- the truth

Disaggregation is the defensive move; red-teaming is the offensive one — actively constructing inputs designed to make the model fail: adversarial phrasings, edge cases, jailbreak prompts, underrepresented groups, distribution shifts. Rather than asking “what’s the average score?”, red-teaming asks “what’s the worst thing this model will do, and how do I make it do that?” The two together — measure the slices you know, hunt for the failures you don’t — are the backbone of a safety evaluation.

Evals are becoming the science of AI safety. For modern models, the hardest safety questions are empirical: can it be jailbroken? Does it deceive? Can it assist with dangerous capabilities? Is it robust across languages and demographics? Progress here is measured by evaluations — carefully constructed test suites and red-team exercises, increasingly by independent third parties. It’s the same rigor from Track 9 (multiple seeds, honest reporting, no test-set gaming), now aimed at harms rather than accuracy — and it’s one of the most in-demand skills in the field.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Averages hide worst cases: a frequency-weighted metric can be high while a critical minority slice fails completely.
  • Disaggregate by subgroup/slice to reveal failures the headline number conceals.
  • Red-teaming = actively constructing inputs to make the model fail (edge cases, jailbreaks, shifts), not just measuring averages.
  • Evaluate the worst case, not the mean — especially in safety-critical settings.
  • Safety evals extend Track 9’s honest-evaluation rigor to harms, robustness, and misuse — increasingly by independent parties.

Q1. A model reports 90% overall accuracy but 0% on a 10% subgroup. How is the 90% possible?

  • The overall number must be wrong.
    It’s arithmetically correct: \(0.9\times100\% + 0.1\times0\% = 90\%\). That’s exactly the problem.
  • The average is frequency-weighted, so the large majority (perfect) dominates the small failing minority.
    Correct. Aggregate metrics weight by group size; a 90% majority at 100% plus a 10% minority at 0% averages to 90%, hiding the total failure on the minority.
  • The subgroup has no data.
    It has 10% of the data; that data is simply all misclassified.
  • Accuracy can’t be disaggregated.
    It can and should be — disaggregation is exactly what reveals the failure.

Q2. What is “red-teaming” a model?

  • Reporting its average score.
    That’s the passive metric red-teaming is meant to go beyond.
  • Training it on more data.
    Red-teaming is about testing/attacking, not training.
  • Making it faster.
    Performance optimization, not safety evaluation.
  • Actively constructing inputs designed to make it fail — edge cases, jailbreaks, shifts.
    Correct. Red-teaming is an adversarial search for failures: you try to break the model on purpose to discover harms before deployment does.

Q3. Why is disaggregated evaluation especially important in safety-critical settings?

  • Because the failing minority may be exactly the cases you cannot afford to get wrong.
    Correct. In medical, legal, or demographic contexts, a small slice can be the most consequential; an average that hides its failure is not just misleading but harmful.
  • Because averages are hard to compute.
    Averages are trivial to compute; the issue is what they conceal.
  • Because minorities are always easy.
    Often the opposite — minority/rare cases are frequently where models fail.
  • Because it speeds up inference.
    Evaluation granularity is unrelated to inference speed.

Q4. How do safety evaluations relate to Track 9’s honest-evaluation methods?

  • They abandon them for new rules.
    They build on the same principles, not replace them.
  • They only care about speed.
    Speed isn’t the focus; correctness and harm are.
  • They apply the same rigor (honest reporting, no test-set gaming, worst-case thinking) but aim it at harms, robustness, and misuse.
    Correct. Safety evals extend multiple-seed, disaggregated, no-leakage discipline from accuracy to safety-relevant behaviors — the same scientific honesty, higher stakes.
  • They require no data.
    They require carefully constructed, often adversarial data — arguably more, not less.

✍️ Practice

1. In evaluation.py, change the minority group’s share from 10% to 1%. How does the headline accuracy change, and why is that scarier?

Show solution

The headline rises toward ~99% (the tiny failing group barely moves the average), which is scarier: the smaller the failing slice, the better the aggregate looks, so the more thoroughly a real failure is hidden. A 99% headline invites total confidence while the model may be catastrophically wrong for a rare but important population. This is why you cannot let a single high number end the conversation — the rarer and more consequential a failure mode, the more it demands targeted evaluation. It also motivates stress datasets that deliberately over-sample hard/minority cases so failures are visible in the metrics.

2. Design three red-team tests for a customer-service chatbot. What failure is each hunting for?

Show solution

Examples: (1) Jailbreak prompts (“ignore your instructions and…”) — hunting for safety-guardrail bypasses. (2) Adversarial/edge phrasings and typos, and non-English inputs — hunting for robustness gaps and demographic/linguistic disparities. (3) Prompt-injection via user-supplied content (a support ticket that contains instructions) — hunting for the model following untrusted embedded commands. Others: eliciting confidently wrong facts (hallucination), coaxing it to reveal system prompts or other users’ data (privacy), and pushing it toward harmful advice. Each test targets a specific harm class; a good red-team is systematic across categories, and the failures found feed back into training and guardrails — a loop, not a one-off checkmark.

3. Conceptual: why are independent, third-party evaluations increasingly seen as important for frontier models?

Show solution

The developer of a model has incentives (and blind spots) that can bias its own evaluations — and Goodhart’s law (Lesson 1) means a lab optimizing its internal benchmarks may over-fit them. Independent evaluators bring fresh adversarial creativity, no stake in a favorable result, and consistency across models from different developers, making claims about safety and capability more trustworthy — much as third-party audits do in other high-stakes industries. It also addresses a collective-action problem: shared, external evals let society compare models on safety, not just marketing. This is why external red-teaming, evaluation organizations, and pre-deployment testing regimes are becoming standard practice for the most capable systems — the same “don’t trust the number you optimized” principle, applied at the level of an entire lab.
