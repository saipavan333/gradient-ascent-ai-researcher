How to read a paper (the three-pass method)

Reading a research paper front-to-back like a novel is the slowest, most painful way to do it. Skilled researchers read in three quick passes, each with one job — decide if it's worth your time, grasp the main idea, then rebuild it in full — so you never spend four hours on a paper you should have dropped in five minutes.

Track 21 · Lesson 0 Concept + code + interactive ~20 min Builds on: Tracks 1, 12

A paper is the standard unit of research writing: a short document (usually 8–12 pages) where scientists report one new result and the evidence for it. The hard truth is that there are far more papers than you can ever read closely, and most of any given paper is detail you don't need yet. The fix, from S. Keshav's famous one-page guide, is the three-pass method: instead of one slow linear read, you make three fast passes over the paper, each with a single clear goal. Pass 1 decides whether to keep reading. Pass 2 gets you the gist. Pass 3 — only for the few papers you truly need — rebuilds the work in your head. The whole point is to spend your attention where it pays off.

💡 Intuition: scouting a hiking trail

Imagine you're picking a trail to hike. You wouldn't measure every pebble before deciding whether to go. First you glance at the map — where does this trail lead, how long, how steep? That's enough to decide if it's even the right trail. If it looks promising, you walk it once at a steady pace, noticing the big features: the river crossing, the lookout, the tricky fork. Only if you're going to lead a group up it next week do you go back and study every rock, every turn, every hidden root, until you could walk it blindfolded.

Reading a paper is exactly this. The map is Pass 1: title, abstract, headings, conclusion — a five-minute glance to decide "is this even the right trail?" The steady walk is Pass 2: read the whole thing for the shape of the argument, but don't stop to inspect every stone (the proofs, the fiddly derivations). The blindfold-level study is Pass 3: the deep read you do only for the handful of papers your own work will stand on. Matching effort to purpose is the entire skill.

Before the passes, a two-minute anatomy lesson. Almost every paper has the same skeleton, and knowing each part's job lets you find what you need fast:

  • Abstract — the whole paper in one paragraph: the problem, what they did, the key result.
  • Introduction — the problem, why it's hard, and a bulleted list of the paper's contributions (its new claims).
  • Related work — how this differs from what came before.
  • Method — what they actually did, in enough detail to (in principle) redo it.
  • Experiments / Results — the evidence: tables, plots, comparisons.
  • Conclusion — a recap of the claim and what's next.
  • References — the map of which earlier papers this one builds on.

Here is what each pass is for, in plain terms:

Pass 1 — the skim (5–10 minutes). Read only the title, abstract, section headings, and conclusion, and glance at the figures. Skip all the math. Your one job is to answer five questions Keshav calls the "five C's": what Category of paper is it (a new method? an analysis? a survey?), what's the Context (what problem, which prior work), does it seem Correct at a glance, what are its Contributions, and is it Clear? Then make one decision: read further, or stop. Dropping a paper here is a success, not a failure — you just saved four hours.

Pass 2 — the grasp (about an hour). Now read the whole paper, but still skip the heavy proofs. Look carefully at the figures and tables — are the axes labeled? Are there error bars (little marks showing how much the number wobbles run to run)? What exactly are they comparing against? Mark references you don't know for later. Your goal: be able to explain the main claim and the evidence for it to a friend, without notes.

Pass 3 — the rebuild (4–5 hours). Reserved for papers you'll build on. Here you virtually re-implement the paper: mentally (or on paper) recreate every step, making the same assumptions, and check that you'd arrive at their results. This is where you find the hidden assumptions and the weak spots. When you're done you could reconstruct the paper from memory and defend or attack it.

✍️ Worked example: Pass 1 on "Attention Is All You Need"

Let's run a real Pass 1 on the 2017 paper behind Track 5 — the one that introduced the Transformer. Time yourself; this should take under ten minutes.

1
Title → category. "Attention Is All You Need." The provocative title signals a new architecture paper, and hints at the claim: you can drop the usual machinery and keep only "attention." Category logged: new method for sequence modeling.
2
Abstract → the main claim. It proposes the Transformer, a model based solely on attention, with no recurrence and no convolutions. Headline evidence: 28.4 BLEU on WMT 2014 English→German (a new best at the time) while training far faster because the computation parallelizes. That one sentence is the claim you'd defend.
3
Headings → the shape. Skimming the section titles — Model Architecture, Attention, Positional Encoding, Why Self-Attention, Training, Results — tells you the argument: here's the design, here's why attention is enough, here's the payoff. You haven't read a single equation yet.
4
Conclusion + figures → confirm. The conclusion restates that an attention-only model is both stronger and faster to train. A glance at Figure 1 shows the encoder–decoder stack. Nothing contradicts the abstract. Decision after ~8 minutes: this is foundational to Track 5, so it earns a Pass 2 (and eventually a Pass 3). Notice you made a confident keep/drop call without touching the math — that's Pass 1 working exactly as designed.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Reading linearly, start to finish. Marching word-by-word from the first page is the slowest path and the easiest way to burn out on a paper you should have dropped. Always skim first.
  • Getting stuck on the math in Pass 1. If you hit an equation in the skim and try to understand it, you've broken the pass. Skip it — the math is a Pass 2 or Pass 3 problem.
  • Never doing Pass 3 on the paper you're building on. For the one or two papers your project depends on, a deep rebuild is not optional — that's where the hidden assumptions live.
  • Ignoring the reference graph. The references are a map. If three papers you're reading all cite the same earlier work, that earlier work is probably a key node — read it.
  • Not taking notes. If you don't capture the claim and the key number as you go, you'll re-read the whole thing next week. A summary card pays for itself immediately.

🔑 Key ideas to remember

  • Three passes, three jobs: Pass 1 = should I read this? Pass 2 = what's the idea? Pass 3 = could I rebuild it?
  • Pass 1 is a keep/drop filter (5–10 min): title, abstract, headings, conclusion, figures — skip the math. Dropping is a win.
  • Pass 2 gets the gist (~1 hr): read it all except proofs; study figures and tables; note what they compare against.
  • Pass 3 rebuilds it (4–5 hr): re-implement in your head, surface hidden assumptions — only for papers you'll build on.
  • Match effort to purpose, capture notes as you go, and follow the reference graph to find the key papers.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What is the single job of Pass 1?

  • Understand every equation.
    Equations are skipped in Pass 1; that's Pass 2/3 work.
  • Reproduce the results.
    Reproduction is a deep Pass 3 (and the next lesson) activity.
  • Decide whether the paper is worth reading further.
    Correct. Pass 1 is a fast keep/drop filter using the title, abstract, headings, and conclusion.
  • Write your own related-work section.
    That comes much later; Pass 1 is just triage.

Q2. You skim a paper's title, abstract, and conclusion and it's clearly not relevant to your work. What should you do?

  • Force yourself to read the whole thing anyway.
    That wastes the hours the three-pass method is designed to save.
  • Stop reading — dropping it here is the intended outcome.
    Correct. A confident drop after Pass 1 is a success; it protects your time for the papers that matter.
  • Immediately start a full Pass 3 rebuild.
    Pass 3 is reserved for papers you'll build on, not ones you're dropping.
  • Read only the math sections.
    If the paper is irrelevant, the math won't change that — and math is skipped early anyway.

Q3. In Pass 2, why do you look carefully at figures and tables?

  • To admire the color scheme.
    Presentation isn't the point; the evidence is.
  • Because figures replace the need to read the text.
    Pass 2 reads the whole paper; figures complement the text, not replace it.
  • To copy them into your slides.
    Maybe later — but that's not why you scrutinize them in Pass 2.
  • They carry the core evidence — check axes, baselines, and error bars.
    Correct. Figures and tables are where the claim is supported or exposed; unlabeled axes or missing error bars are red flags.

Q4. When is a full Pass 3 (rebuild) worth the 4–5 hours?

  • For the few papers your own work will directly build on.
    Correct. A deep rebuild surfaces hidden assumptions and weak spots — essential for foundations, wasteful for everything else.
  • For every paper you ever open.
    There are far too many papers; a Pass 3 on all of them is impossible and unnecessary.
  • Only for papers you plan to reject.
    You reject after Pass 1; a rebuild is for papers you're relying on.
  • Never — Pass 2 is always enough.
    Pass 2 gives the gist, but rebuilding is what lets you truly trust and extend a result.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) Using the interactive, list the four parts of a paper you read in Pass 1 and the one decision you make at the end. Why is the math deliberately excluded?

Show solution

Pass 1 reads the title, abstract, section headings, and conclusion (plus a glance at figures), and ends with one decision: keep reading or drop it. The math is excluded because it's slow and detailed, and you don't yet know whether the paper is even worth that investment — the skim's only purpose is triage, and equations don't help you decide relevance faster.

2. (transfer) A colleague says: "I spent all afternoon carefully reading a paper, and only at the end did I realize it wasn't relevant to my project." Which part of the method did they skip, and what would you advise?

Show solution

They skipped Pass 1 — the five-minute skim that answers "is this even the right trail?" A quick read of the abstract, headings, and conclusion would have flagged the mismatch in minutes and saved the afternoon. Advice: always triage first; only invest a full read after a paper survives Pass 1. Reading linearly without triage is the single most common time sink.

3. (stretch) You're starting a project and have collected 30 candidate papers. Sketch a plan, in terms of the three passes, for how many of each pass you'd realistically do — and explain how the reference graph helps.

Show solution

A realistic budget: Pass 1 on all 30 (a couple of hours total) to triage down to maybe 8–10 relevant ones; Pass 2 on those 8–10 to understand the landscape; and Pass 3 on the 1–3 your project will directly build on. The reference graph accelerates this: if many of your Pass-2 papers cite the same earlier work, that shared citation is almost certainly a foundational node worth promoting to Pass 3 — the citations tell you where the field's weight actually sits, so you're not reading at random.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

The volume problem has only grown — thousands of machine-learning papers now appear on arXiv every month, far more than existed when Keshav wrote his guide in 2007. Modern researchers lean on tools to widen Pass 1: Semantic Scholar and Connected Papers visualize the reference graph, "Papers with Code" links results to runnable repositories, and large language models can draft a first-pass summary in seconds. But none of these replace the discipline — an LLM summary is itself a claim you must verify, and the reproducibility questions you'll meet in the next lessons can only be answered by a real Pass 3. The tools make triage faster; they make careful reading more valuable, not less. Reading well is still the highest-leverage skill in research.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Don't read a paper like a novel. Skim it in five minutes to decide if it's worth your time (Pass 1), read it in an hour for the main idea and evidence (Pass 2), and only rebuild it in full for the few papers you'll actually build on (Pass 3)."
