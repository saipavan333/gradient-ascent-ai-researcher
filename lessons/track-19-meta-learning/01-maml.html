MAML: learning an initialization that adapts fast

What if, instead of learning to solve one task, a model learned to learn any new task quickly? MAML meta-learns a single set of starting weights so good that a few gradient steps adapt it to a brand-new task. It's "learning to learn," and it works.

Few-shot learning (Lesson 0) leaned on a good representation and hoped it was suited to the comparison. Meta-learning — "learning to learn" — is more ambitious: it explicitly trains a model to be good at learning new tasks. The most famous method, MAML (Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning), has a beautifully simple goal: find a single network initialization — one set of starting weights — from which any new task from your family can be mastered in just a few gradient steps. Rather than a set of weights that solves one problem, MAML seeks a launch point that's a short hop away from the solution to every problem. You train it by simulating the whole "adapt to a new task" process thousands of times — take a task, adapt from the current init, measure how well the adapted model does, and nudge the init so that adaptation would have gone better. The result is weights that aren't good at any one task out of the box, but are superbly positioned to become good at a new one almost instantly. This lesson builds MAML on the classic sine-wave benchmark and shows the meta-init adapting three times faster than a random start.

💡 Intuition: base camp near every summit

Imagine you're a mountaineer who will be asked to climb one of several nearby peaks, but you won't know which until the morning. Where should you pitch base camp? Not on any single summit — you'd be far from all the others. The smart move is to camp in the middle, at the spot that's a short climb from every peak. Then whichever mountain you're assigned, you're only a few hours away. MAML pitches exactly this base camp in weight space. Each "peak" is the ideal weights for one task; the meta-learned initialization is the central launch point that's a few gradient steps from all of them. When a new task arrives, a handful of steps carries you up its slope.

Formally, MAML has two nested loops. The inner loop adapts the current init \(\theta\) to a task with a few gradient steps: \(\theta'_t = \theta - \alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}_t(\theta)\). The outer loop improves \(\theta\) so those adapted weights do well:

\[ \theta \;\leftarrow\; \theta - \beta\,\nabla_\theta \sum_{\text{tasks } t} \mathcal{L}_t\big(\underbrace{\theta - \alpha\nabla\mathcal{L}_t(\theta)}_{\text{adapt to task } t}\big) \] (1.1)

Read it inside-out: for each task \(t\), adapt the init by one (or a few) gradient steps with inner learning rate \(\alpha\) — that's the base-camp-to-summit climb. Then evaluate the adapted weights' loss \(\mathcal{L}_t(\theta')\), and take an outer gradient step (rate \(\beta\)) on the original \(\theta\) to make that post-adaptation loss small, summed over tasks. In words: "adjust the starting point so that adapting from it works well." The simpler first-order cousin, Reptile — which we implement — skips the second-derivative bookkeeping: it just adapts to each task, then moves the init a little toward the adapted weights. Same base-camp intuition, cheaper math.

✍️ Worked example: MAML on sine waves

From maml.py: the classic benchmark — each task is a sine \(A\sin(x+\phi)\) with random amplitude and phase.

1
The task family. Thousands of sine waves, each with its own amplitude \(A\) and phase \(\phi\). A model must fit a new sine from just 10 sample points.
2
Meta-train (Reptile). Repeatedly: pick a sine, take a few gradient steps fitting it, then nudge the init 5% of the way toward those fitted weights. Over thousands of tasks, the init drifts to the "central" weights of the whole sine family.
3
Test on a new sine. From the meta-init, 5 gradient steps on 10 points gives query loss 0.57. From a random init, the same 5 steps give 1.54 — nearly 3× worse. The meta-init started already "shaped like a sine" and just needed to dial in \(A\) and \(\phi\).
4
What was learned. Not a sine — an initialization. The meta-init encodes "functions in this family look like sines," so adaptation only has to fit the few remaining degrees of freedom. That's learning to learn.

🛠️ From scratch: Reptile (first-order MAML)

P = net_init()
for it in range(3000):                       # meta-training over many tasks
    A, ph = task()                           # a random sine (amplitude, phase)
    x, y = sample(A, ph, 20)
    Pk = sgd_steps(P, x, y, steps=8, lr=0.02) # inner loop: adapt to this task
    for i in range(len(P)):
        P[i] += 0.05 * (Pk[i] - P[i])         # outer loop: move init TOWARD adapted weights

# test: adapt the meta-init to a NEW task in 5 steps  -> loss 0.57
#       adapt a RANDOM init to the same task in 5 steps -> loss 1.54  (~3x worse)

The single line P[i] += 0.05*(Pk[i]-P[i]) is the whole meta-update: after adapting to a task, step the initialization a little toward where adaptation landed. Do it across enough tasks and the init settles at the point that's a short adaptation from all of them. (Full MAML instead differentiates through the inner steps — the outer gradient of an inner gradient — which is more powerful but needs second derivatives; Reptile captures most of the benefit with none of that machinery.)

"Model-agnostic" is the whole point. MAML doesn't care what the model is — any network trained with gradient descent works, because MAML only touches the initialization and the update rule, not the architecture. The same recipe meta-learns fast adaptation for classifiers, regressors, and reinforcement-learning policies. It reframes learning itself as something you can optimize: don't just learn a solution, learn a starting point for learning solutions. That shift — treating the learning process as the object of optimization — is the core idea of meta-learning.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Expecting the meta-init to solve tasks out of the box. It's deliberately not good at any single task — it's positioned to become good fast. Judge it after adaptation, not before.
  • Confusing the inner and outer learning rates. The inner rate \(\alpha\) adapts to a task; the outer rate \(\beta\) improves the init. They do different jobs and are tuned separately.
  • Forgetting tasks must share structure. MAML helps when new tasks resemble the meta-training tasks (all sines, all similar classification problems). A truly unrelated task has no shared "base camp" to exploit.
  • Reaching for full second-order MAML by default. It's expensive and often barely beats first-order MAML or Reptile. Start simple; add second-order only if it clearly helps.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • MAML meta-learns an initialization from which any new task in the family is a few gradient steps away.
  • Two loops: inner adapts to a task; outer improves the init so adaptation works well (eq 1.1).
  • Reptile = first-order version: adapt to a task, then move the init toward the adapted weights.
  • Judge after adaptation: meta-init isn't good at any task alone, but adapts ~3× faster than random (sine benchmark).
  • Model-agnostic: works for any gradient-trained model — classification, regression, RL.

Q1. What does MAML actually learn?

  • A classifier for one specific task.
    It learns something reusable across tasks, not one solution.
  • A dataset.
    It learns weights, not data.
  • An initialization from which new tasks can be learned in a few steps.
    Correct. The meta-init is a launch point close to every task's solution.
  • The learning rate only.
    It learns the initial weights (some variants also learn rates, but the core is the init).

Q2. Why is the meta-init not good at any single task before adaptation?

  • It's positioned to be a short adaptation from all tasks, not to solve any one.
    Correct. Like a base camp central to every peak — close to all, on top of none.
  • Because it's randomly initialized.
    It's carefully meta-trained, not random.
  • Because it has too few parameters.
    Capacity isn't the reason; positioning is.
  • Because MAML never trains it.
    MAML trains it extensively via the outer loop.

Q3. In Reptile, what is the meta-update?

  • Replace the init with the adapted weights.
    It moves only partway toward them, not all the way.
  • Average all task datasets.
    It works on weights, not by pooling data.
  • Differentiate through the inner loop.
    That's full MAML; Reptile avoids second derivatives.
  • Move the init a fraction of the way toward the task-adapted weights.
    Correct. Adapt to a task, then nudge the init toward where adaptation landed.

Q4. When will MAML help on a new task?

  • Whenever the task is large.
    Size isn't the criterion; shared structure is.
  • When the new task shares structure with the meta-training tasks.
    Correct. A shared "base camp" only exists if tasks resemble each other.
  • Only for image tasks.
    MAML is model- and domain-agnostic.
  • Only with second-order gradients.
    First-order variants work well too.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, drag the init onto one of the task ★s. What happens to the total distance, and why is that a bad initialization for MAML?

Show solution

Sitting on one task's ★ makes the distance to that task zero but leaves you far from the others, so the total distance is large. That's a bad meta-init: it's specialized to one task and slow to adapt to the rest — exactly the trap MAML avoids. The meta-optimal init sits in the middle (near the centroid of the tasks), trading a little distance to any single task for being close to all of them. This is why the meta-init deliberately isn't good at any one task on its own: being central beats being specialized when you don't know which task is coming.

2. (transfer) How is MAML different from ordinary pre-training + fine-tuning (which also gives you a starting point to adapt from)?

Show solution

Ordinary pre-training optimizes the weights to perform well on the pre-training objective, and you hope those weights also fine-tune nicely — adaptation is an afterthought. MAML instead optimizes the initialization directly for adaptability: its objective is "after a few gradient steps on a new task, how good is the result?" — it simulates fine-tuning during training and shapes the init to make that fine-tuning succeed. So MAML's init can be worse at the pre-training tasks yet better at rapidly learning new ones, because fast adaptation is the explicit goal rather than a hoped-for side effect. In practice, for large models plain pre-training + fine-tuning often wins on cost and simplicity, but MAML's framing — optimize for the ability to adapt — is the conceptual advance, and it shines when you must adapt from very few examples very quickly.

3. (stretch) Large language models do "in-context learning" — adapt to a task from a few examples in the prompt, with no weight updates at all. How is that both like and unlike MAML?

Show solution

Like MAML: both produce a system that masters a new task from a few examples, and both get there by training over a huge distribution of tasks so that fast adaptation emerges — MAML meta-trains an init across task episodes; an LLM pre-trains across an enormous variety of text that implicitly contains countless tasks. Unlike MAML: MAML adapts by actually running gradient steps on the new task's examples (updating weights), whereas in-context learning adapts entirely in the forward pass — the examples go in the prompt and the frozen network's activations do the "adapting," no gradients, no weight change. A striking research thread argues that in-context learning may implement a form of gradient-based adaptation internally within the Transformer's computation, blurring the line: the LLM may have learned to simulate an MAML-like inner loop in its activations. Either way, both are "learning to learn" — MAML in weight space with real gradient steps, LLMs in activation space within a single forward pass.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

MAML sparked a wave of meta-learning research and is used for fast adaptation in robotics (a robot adapting its gait to a new terrain in a few trials), personalization (adapting a model to a new user quickly), drug and materials discovery, and few-shot vision. Its bigger legacy is conceptual: the idea that the learning procedure itself is something to optimize. That framing echoes through in-context learning in large language models, learned optimizers, and "learning to learn" agents. Meta-learning asks the deep question of what it means to be a good learner rather than a good solver — and MAML's answer, "be positioned to adapt," is one of the most elegant in the field.
