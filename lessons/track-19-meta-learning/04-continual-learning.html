Continual learning: learning without forgetting

Catastrophic forgetting has fixes. The simplest and most effective is replay — rehearse a few old examples while learning the new task, and the old knowledge survives. This lesson builds it, sketches its main rival (EWC), and closes the track on lifelong learning.

Track 16 · Lesson 4 Concept + code + interactive ~18 min Builds on: Lesson 3

Lesson 3 showed the disease — a network forgets task A the moment it learns task B — because B's gradients overwrite the shared weights with nothing protecting A. Continual learning (also called lifelong or incremental learning) is the cure: methods that let a network accumulate knowledge across a stream of tasks without erasing what came before, using bounded memory and compute (no cheating by retraining on all past data every time). There are two big families, and they mirror the biological tricks from last lesson. Replay (rehearsal) keeps a small buffer of old examples and mixes them into training on the new task, so the gradients still "see" the old task and preserve its weights — the artificial version of the brain replaying memories. Regularization methods like EWC instead identify which weights were important for old tasks and penalize changing them — the artificial version of protecting consolidated memories. This lesson builds replay from scratch and watches it keep task A alive at 93% where naive training crashed it to 7%, then explains EWC and where the field is headed. It's the satisfying resolution to the track: from "networks can't learn continually" to "here's how they can."

💡 Intuition: study old flashcards while learning new ones

How do you learn a second language without forgetting your first? You keep using the first — a bit of practice keeps it fresh. Replay does exactly this for a network. Instead of training on task B alone, you keep a small stack of "flashcards" from task A and shuffle a few into every study session on B. Now each training batch contains both B's new material and a reminder of A, so the gradient can't drift away from A's solution — it's pulled back toward A on every step that includes a replayed example. A surprisingly tiny buffer (a few dozen examples) is enough, because those reminders keep the shared weights anchored where both tasks need them.

Concretely, replay changes the training data for task B from "B only" to "B plus a sample from the memory buffer":

\[ \theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\nabla_\theta\,\mathcal{L}\big(\underbrace{B \;\cup\; \mathcal{M}_A}_{\text{new task + replayed old examples}}\big), \qquad |\mathcal{M}_A| \ll |A| \] (4.1)

The only change from the forgetting equation (3.1) is what's inside the loss: instead of B alone, the gradient is computed on B together with a small memory buffer \(\mathcal{M}_A\) of old task-A examples (much smaller than all of A). Because A now appears in the objective, its weights are protected — the update can't sacrifice A to fit B. That one change turns a forgetting network into one that remembers.

✍️ Worked example: replay keeps task A alive

From continual.py: the same two forgetting-prone tasks, learned with and without replay.

1
Naive baseline. Train A, then B alone. Task-A accuracy after B: 0.07 — forgotten (from Lesson 3).
2
Build a buffer. Keep just 40 of task A's examples in memory \(\mathcal{M}_A\) (a small fraction).
3
Train B with replay. Each step trains on B's data plus the 40 buffered A examples. Task-B still reaches 0.98 — the new task is learned fine.
4
Re-check A. Task-A accuracy is now 0.93 — retained! Forty remembered examples were enough to anchor A's weights while B was learned. Naive 0.07 → replay 0.93: the fix works.

🛠️ From scratch: experience replay

idx = rng.choice(len(XA), 40, replace=False)      # remember 40 of task A's examples
bufX, bufY = XA[idx], yA[idx]

net.train(XA, yA)                                  # learn A
for _ in range(250):                               # learn B, WITH replay
    net.step(np.vstack([XB, bufX]),                # each batch = task B + buffered A
             np.concatenate([yB, bufY]))

# task A after B:  naive 0.07  ->  replay 0.93     (task B still 0.98)

The entire fix is np.vstack([XB, bufX]) — appending the memory buffer to each training batch. That's it. Replay is the workhorse of continual learning precisely because it's this simple and this effective; most sophisticated methods are refinements of "which examples to keep and how to replay them."

The other big family: EWC. Replay stores data; Elastic Weight Consolidation stores importance. After learning task A, EWC estimates how important each weight was for A (via the Fisher information — roughly, how much A's loss would suffer if that weight changed). Then, while learning B, it adds a penalty \(\sum_i F_i(\theta_i - \theta_{A,i})^2\) that pulls important weights back toward their task-A values (springs of stiffness \(F_i\)), while unimportant weights are free to move. No old data needed — just the importances. Replay vs EWC is the classic trade-off: keep data, or keep a summary of what mattered.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking you need to store all old data. A tiny buffer (dozens of examples) often recovers most of the lost accuracy — the power of replay is how little it needs.
  • Ignoring the memory/privacy cost of replay. Storing raw old examples can be infeasible or violate privacy. That's exactly when EWC (store importances, not data) or generative replay (learn to regenerate old examples) is preferred.
  • Assuming one method wins everywhere. Replay, EWC, and architectural methods each have regimes where they shine. The right choice depends on memory budget, privacy, and how similar the tasks are.
  • Forgetting the stability–plasticity trade-off. Protect old knowledge too hard and you can't learn new tasks (too stable); protect too little and you forget (too plastic). Every method is tuning this balance.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Continual learning = accumulate knowledge across a task stream without forgetting, on a bounded budget.
  • Replay: keep a small buffer of old examples and mix them into new-task training (eq 4.1) — simple and very effective (A: 0.07 → 0.93).
  • EWC (regularization): penalize changing weights that were important for old tasks — stores importances, not data.
  • A tiny buffer suffices; replay's strength is how little memory it needs.
  • Stability–plasticity trade-off: every method balances keeping the old against learning the new.

Q1. How does replay prevent catastrophic forgetting?

  • By using a bigger network.
    Replay is about the data, not capacity.
  • By mixing a few old-task examples into new-task training, so gradients keep protecting old weights.
    Correct. The old task stays in the objective, anchoring its weights.
  • By lowering the learning rate to zero.
    That would stop learning B too; replay keeps learning while remembering.
  • By deleting task B.
    The goal is to learn B and keep A.

Q2. How does EWC differ from replay?

  • EWC needs more old data.
    EWC needs no old data — that's its appeal.
  • EWC deletes the new task.
    It learns the new task while protecting old weights.
  • They are identical.
    They take opposite approaches (data vs importances).
  • EWC stores which weights mattered and penalizes changing them, instead of storing old examples.
    Correct. Replay stores data; EWC stores importance and adds a penalty.

Q3. Roughly how big a replay buffer is needed to recover most of task A?

  • A small one — dozens of examples often suffice.
    Correct. Replay's strength is how little memory it needs (40 examples took A from 0.07 to 0.93).
  • All of task A's data.
    That would defeat the point; a tiny buffer works.
  • Ten times task B's data.
    No such requirement; small buffers are effective.
  • None — replay uses no data.
    Replay is defined by keeping some old data.

Q4. What is the stability–plasticity trade-off?

  • The trade-off between speed and accuracy.
    That's a different trade-off.
  • The trade-off between model size and compute.
    Unrelated to continual learning's core tension.
  • Protecting old knowledge (stability) vs being able to learn new tasks (plasticity).
    Correct. Too stable can't learn new; too plastic forgets old — every method balances these.
  • The trade-off between train and test error.
    That's generalization, not continual learning.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, run "Train B — naive" and note the task-A bar, then reset, train A again, and run "Train B — with replay." How much does the task-A bar differ, and why?

Show solution

Naive leaves task A near the floor (it's overwritten), while replay keeps it high — a dramatic gap, mirroring the 0.07 → 0.93 result. The reason is exactly equation (4.1): with replay, every training batch includes a few remembered A examples, so the gradient always has a component pulling the weights back toward A's solution, and they never drift far. Without replay, nothing counteracts B's pull. The small ringed buffer is doing all the work — a vivid demonstration that you don't need to store much of the past to preserve it, just enough to keep reminding the network.

2. (transfer) Storing raw old data isn't always allowed (privacy, storage). Name a replay variant and a non-replay method that avoid keeping real old examples.

Show solution

Generative replay avoids storing real data by training a generative model (Track 8 — a VAE or GAN) on old tasks and, when learning a new task, sampling synthetic old examples from it to replay instead of real ones — you keep a generator, not a dataset. EWC (and related regularization methods like Synaptic Intelligence) avoid data entirely: they store only per-weight importances and add a penalty against changing important weights. Both sidestep the privacy/storage cost of a raw buffer, trading it for either the cost of a generative model (which itself must not forget) or the approximation error of the importance estimate. This is a real deployment consideration: when you literally cannot retain user data, importance-based or generative approaches are the way to do continual learning.

3. (stretch) Modern large language models face a continual-learning problem: fine-tuning on new data degrades old abilities. Relate the fixes here to how that's handled in practice.

Show solution

The same two families reappear, scaled up. Replay-like: when fine-tuning an LLM on new data, practitioners mix in a sample of the original pre-training/instruction data (rehearsal) so the model doesn't forget general abilities — this is experience replay by another name. Regularization/architectural: rather than update all weights (which risks overwriting broad knowledge), methods like LoRA and adapters freeze the base model and train small add-on modules per task, so old abilities in the frozen weights are protected by construction — an architectural cousin of EWC's "don't disturb important weights." There's also KL-penalty fine-tuning (stay close to the original model, echoing EWC's anchor). So the humble replay buffer and weight-protection ideas you built here are directly reflected in how billion-parameter models are kept from forgetting — the problem doesn't go away with scale, and the same principles solve it.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Continual learning is essential for any AI that must keep up with a changing world: recommenders and fraud models updated on fresh data without losing old patterns, robots acquiring new skills, on-device models that personalize over time, and large models fine-tuned repeatedly without eroding their core abilities. The frontier explores generative replay (rehearse from a learned generator instead of stored data), better importance estimates, task-free continual learning (no clean task boundaries — the realistic streaming setting), and the deep stability–plasticity question of how a system can stay flexible yet retain knowledge indefinitely. This closes the track: you've seen how to learn new things fast (few-shot, MAML, protonets) and how to keep learning without forgetting (replay, EWC) — the two halves of building AI that learns like a lifelong learner rather than a one-shot, frozen model.
