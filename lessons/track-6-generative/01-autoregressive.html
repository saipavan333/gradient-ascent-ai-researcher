Autoregressive models

The most direct way to model \(p(x)\): break it into a chain of one-step predictions, each conditioned on everything generated so far. It is exact, it is simple, and you have already trained one — GPT.

Track 11 · Lesson 1 Concept + code + interactive ~26 min Builds on: Lesson 0, Track 9

Some generative models approximate. This one doesn't. Autoregressive models write the data distribution \(p(x)\) exactly, using a rule of probability you already know, and then generate by producing one piece at a time — each piece chosen in light of everything produced before it. The GPT you built in Track 5 is precisely this idea applied to text. Here we strip it back to its bones so the entire mechanism fits on one screen.

💡 Intuition: writing a sentence one word at a time

Think about how your phone's predictive text works, or how you'd finish the sentence "The cat sat on the ___." You don't produce the whole sentence in one burst — you choose the next word based on the words already there, then the next, then the next. Each choice is easy because it only has to answer a small question: "given what I've written so far, what comes next?" String enough of those small, easy choices together and you've written something coherent. That is the entire idea of an autoregressive model: turn one impossibly hard question ("what is a whole valid sentence?") into a chain of easy ones ("what's the next token?").

🧠 The concept, one step at a time

Modelling a joint distribution over many variables at once, \(p(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)\), is daunting — there are astronomically many combinations. But the chain rule of probability — which is always true, for any distribution, with no assumptions whatsoever — lets us factor that joint into a product of one-variable conditionals:

\[ p(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n) \;=\; p(x_1)\,\prod_{i=2}^{n} p\!\left(x_i \mid x_1,\dots,x_{i-1}\right). \] (1.1)

In words: the probability of the whole thing equals the probability of the first piece, times the probability of the second given the first, times the third given the first two, and so on. Each factor \(p(x_i\mid x_1,\dots,x_{i-1})\) is read "the probability of piece \(i\) given all the pieces before it." The subscript \({<}i\) is shorthand for "everything before position \(i\)," so we often write the conditional as \(p(x_i\mid x_{only modelling choice we make is how to represent each conditional — a lookup table, a small network, or a giant Transformer.

To generate, we run those conditionals forward, feeding each choice back in. This is called ancestral sampling:

\[ x_1\sim p(x_1),\quad x_2\sim p(x_2\mid x_1),\quad x_3\sim p(x_3\mid x_1,x_2),\ \dots \] (1.2)

Draw the first piece, look at it, use it to draw the second, and keep going. Because each step conditions on the real values chosen so far, the pieces come out coherent rather than independent.

How do we train it? We maximize the probability the model assigns to real data — equivalently, we minimize the negative log-likelihood, which by Equation (1.1) splits into a sum over positions:

\[ -\log p(x) \;=\; -\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log p\!\left(x_i \mid x_{ (1.3)

That is exactly next-token prediction — the cross-entropy loss you trained GPT with. Nothing new: an autoregressive model is trained by asking it, at every position, to predict the next piece, and penalizing it by how surprised it is at the true one.

One last dial for generation. At sampling time we can reshape each conditional with a temperature \(\tau\) (Greek "tau"): replace each probability \(p_i\) by \(p_i^{1/\tau}\) and renormalize so they sum to 1.

\[ \tilde{p}_i \;=\; \frac{p_i^{\,1/\tau}}{\sum_j p_j^{\,1/\tau}}. \] (1.4)

Low \(\tau\) (below 1) sharpens the distribution toward its most likely option — safer, more repetitive text. High \(\tau\) (above 1) flattens it — more surprising, more varied, more mistakes. Crucially, temperature changes only sampling, not the trained model. On our 2-D toy data, a tiny table for each conditional is enough to reproduce shapes a single Gaussian never could.

✍️ Worked example: score a sequence, then sample the next token

Use the widget's model. Start-day probabilities \(\pi=[0.5,\,0.3,\,0.2]\) for (Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy), and transition rows \(P[\text{Sunny}]=[0.7,0.2,0.1]\), \(P[\text{Cloudy}]=[0.3,0.4,0.3]\), \(P[\text{Rainy}]=[0.2,0.3,0.5]\). This is a first-order model — each token depends only on the previous one — but the machinery is identical to conditioning on the full history.

1
Score the sequence "Sunny, Cloudy, Rainy" with the chain rule. \(p=\pi_{\text{Sun}}\cdot P[\text{Sun}\!\to\!\text{Cloud}]\cdot P[\text{Cloud}\!\to\!\text{Rain}] = 0.5\times0.2\times0.3 = 0.03\). That single number is Equation (1.1) applied end to end.
2
Its training loss. The negative log-likelihood is \(-\log 0.5-\log 0.2-\log 0.3 = 0.69+1.61+1.20 = 3.50\) nats. Training nudges the model to make real sequences cheaper (smaller loss).
3
Sample the next day given "Rainy" at \(\tau=1\). The conditional is \([0.2,0.3,0.5]\), so the cumulative thresholds are 0.2, 0.5, 1.0. Draw \(u=0.65\): it lands in the third bucket → Rainy again. Append it, and the last day is now Rainy for the next step.
4
Turn the temperature down to \(\tau=0.5\). Raise each probability to the power \(1/0.5=2\): \([0.04,0.09,0.25]\), which normalizes to \([0.11,0.24,0.66]\). The model is now even more sure it will keep raining. Same trained model, different boldness — that is all temperature does.

🛠️ From scratch: ancestral sampling in a loop

import numpy as np

pi = np.array([0.5, 0.3, 0.2])                 # p(x_1): start-day distribution
P  = np.array([[0.7,0.2,0.1],                  # P[i,j] = p(next=j | today=i)
               [0.3,0.4,0.3],
               [0.2,0.3,0.5]])

def temper(p, tau):                            # eq (1.4): reshape then renormalize
    q = p**(1.0/tau)
    return q / q.sum()

def sample_sequence(n, tau=1.0):
    x = [np.random.choice(3, p=temper(pi, tau))]        # x_1 ~ p(x_1)
    for _ in range(n-1):
        x.append(np.random.choice(3, p=temper(P[x[-1]], tau)))  # x_t ~ p(x_t | x_{

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking the chain-rule factorization is an approximation. Equation (1.1) is exact for every distribution — no independence assumed. The only approximation is in how well your model represents each conditional.
  • Forgetting to feed samples back. At training time the model sees the real previous tokens (teacher forcing); at generation it must consume its own previous outputs. This gap is "exposure bias," and it's why a small early mistake can snowball.
  • Believing temperature changes the model. It doesn't touch a single weight. \(\tau\) only reshapes the sampling distribution: low = cautious/repetitive, high = wild/varied.
  • Ignoring the sequential cost. Because token \(i\) needs token \(i-1\), generation can't be parallelized over positions — producing \(n\) tokens takes \(n\) sequential steps. Exact likelihood is bought with slow sampling.

Q1. The chain-rule factorization \(p(x)=p(x_1)\prod_i p(x_i\mid x_{

  • Only when the variables are independent.
    Independence would let you drop the conditioning entirely — that's a special case, not the rule.
  • For any distribution, with no assumptions.
    Correct. It's an identity of probability; the modelling happens inside each conditional.
  • Only for text.
    It applies to any ordered set of variables — pixels, audio, anything.
  • Only approximately, as a bound.
    It's exact equality, not a bound.

Q2. How does an autoregressive model generate a new sample?

  • It outputs all coordinates at once from noise.
    That's more like a GAN or a single decoder pass; autoregressive models go one step at a time.
  • It optimizes the whole sequence by gradient descent at test time.
    No optimization at generation — it samples.
  • Ancestral sampling: draw each token from its conditional, feeding results back in.
    Correct. Each new token conditions on the tokens already drawn.
  • It retrieves the closest training sequence.
    That's retrieval, not sampling from the factorized model.

Q3. Training an autoregressive model by minimizing \(-\sum_i\log p(x_i\mid x_{

  • Next-token prediction with cross-entropy (the GPT loss).
    Correct. The negative log-likelihood splits into a per-position prediction loss.
  • Minimizing reconstruction error like an autoencoder.
    No bottleneck/reconstruction here — it's likelihood of the next token.
  • A minimax game against a discriminator.
    That's a GAN; autoregressive training is plain maximum likelihood.
  • Matching noise like a diffusion model.
    Different family; this is direct likelihood.

Q4. You lower the sampling temperature from \(\tau=1.0\) to \(\tau=0.4\). What happens?

  • The model's weights change.
    Temperature never touches the trained weights.
  • Every token becomes equally likely.
    That's what a very high temperature approaches, not a low one.
  • Likelihoods on real data improve.
    Temperature is a sampling choice; it doesn't change how the model scores data.
  • Sampling concentrates on the most likely tokens — safer, more repetitive output.
    Correct. \(p_i^{1/\tau}\) with small \(\tau\) sharpens toward the top choices.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, drag the temperature to its minimum (0.2) and click "sample next day" many times from a Rainy day. What kind of sequence do you get, and why?

Show solution

You get long runs of the single most likely next token — from Rainy, that's usually more Rainy (its conditional peaks at 0.5, and low temperature sharpens that peak toward ~1). Low \(\tau\) makes sampling nearly greedy, so the model almost always picks the argmax and the sequence becomes monotonous. This is the safe-but-boring end of the temperature dial; it explains why very low-temperature LLM output feels repetitive.

2. (transfer) Run autoregressive.py. It learns \(p(x_1)\) and a \(24\times24\) table for \(p(x_2\mid x_1)\) on two-moons data, then samples. Why can this tiny model carve the crescent gap that a single Gaussian (Lesson 0) could not?

Show solution

Because it never assumes a shape. The table stores a separate distribution over \(x_2\) for each bin of \(x_1\), so it can say "when \(x_1\) is here, \(x_2\) lives only in these two bands, never in the middle." That per-column freedom lets it place probability exactly on the crescents and zero in the gap — something a Gaussian, forced into a single elliptical blob, can't express. It's the chain rule buying expressiveness: model each conditional flexibly and the joint follows.

3. (stretch) Autoregressive generation is inherently sequential: token \(i\) waits for token \(i-1\). Name one consequence for large language models and one research idea that softens it.

Show solution

Consequence: generating long outputs is slow and hard to parallelize — latency grows with length, and serving costs are dominated by the sequential decode loop (unlike training, which parallelizes over positions with teacher forcing). Softeners include speculative decoding (a small draft model proposes several tokens that the big model verifies in one pass), KV caching (reuse past attention computations so each step is cheap), and non-autoregressive or diffusion-style text models that emit tokens in parallel. All trade some exactness or simplicity for speed — the same tension this lesson opened with.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Autoregressive modelling is the dominant paradigm for language: every GPT-family model, and most open LLMs, are autoregressive models of \(p(x)\) over tokens, trained with exactly the next-token loss above. The same recipe generates raw audio (WaveNet) and, historically, images pixel by pixel (PixelRNN/PixelCNN). Its strengths — exact likelihood, stable training, a clean scaling story — are why it powers the largest models on Earth. Its weakness — slow, strictly sequential sampling — is the target of a whole research frontier (speculative decoding, KV caching, parallel decoders). When you built GPT in Track 5, you built the state of the art of this lesson; here you can see the probability skeleton underneath it.

"An autoregressive model factors \(p(x)\) exactly into a chain of next-token predictions, trains by asking 'what comes next?' at every position, and generates by sampling one token at a time and feeding each back in — which is exactly what GPT does."
