Why RNNs forget: vanishing & exploding gradients

The plain RNN has a fatal flaw on long sequences. It isn't a coding bug — it's arithmetic. Multiply many similar numbers together and the result races to zero or to infinity. That product is exactly what training an RNN computes.

In the last lesson you watched a memory fade a little each step. Here's why that fade is fatal — and it's one of the most important diagnoses in all of deep learning. For the network to learn that something at step 0 matters for a decision at step 30, the learning signal (the gradient) has to travel back 30 steps. On the way it gets multiplied by the same number again and again — and repeated multiplication is an exponential. If that number is a little less than 1, the signal vanishes to nothing; if it's a little more than 1, it explodes to infinity. Either way, learning long-range connections breaks.

💡 Intuition: the compound-interest trap

You already know how repeated multiplication behaves — it's compound interest. Multiply by 0.8 each year and after 30 years you have \(0.8^{30} \approx 0.001\), almost nothing. Multiply by 1.2 each year and after 30 years you have \(1.2^{30} \approx 237\), a fortune. Small changes to the per-step factor produce wildly different long-run results, because the effect compounds. Training an RNN does the same multiplication, backward through the steps. Each step multiplies the gradient by roughly the same factor (set by the size of the recurrent weights and the slope of \(\tanh\)). Below 1, the gradient from far-back steps shrinks to zero — the network gets no signal telling it those early inputs mattered. Above 1, it blows up into garbage.

Written out, the gradient connecting step \(k\) to a later step \(T\) is a product of one factor per step in between:

\[ \frac{\partial h_T}{\partial h_k} = \prod_{t=k+1}^{T} \frac{\partial h_t}{\partial h_{t-1}} \;\;\approx\;\; (\text{factor})^{\,T-k}. \] (1.1)

The big \(\prod\) symbol just means "multiply all these together." Because they're all similar, the product behaves like a single factor raised to the power \(T-k\) — the number of steps in between. That exponent is the whole story: it's why the trouble grows so fast with distance.

✍️ Worked example: put numbers on it

Suppose the per-step factor is \(0.8\) (a typical "slightly-less-than-1" value once you include the \(\tanh\) slope). Let's see how the learning signal from step 0 fares as it travels back to later and later steps.

1
After 5 steps: \(0.8^5 \approx 0.33\). Still a third of its strength — the network can learn dependencies this short.
2
After 20 steps: \(0.8^{20} \approx 0.012\). Down to about 1% — weak, and easily drowned out by the stronger signals from recent steps.
3
After 40 steps: \(0.8^{40} \approx 0.00013\). Essentially zero. The network receives almost no signal that step 0 mattered, so it cannot learn a 40-step dependency.
4
Flip the factor to 1.2 and it explodes: \(1.2^{40} \approx 1470\). Now a single update is enormous and training destabilizes. Only a factor of exactly 1 stays steady — and real training almost never balances there. That knife-edge is the whole problem.

🛠️ From scratch: watch it happen

# send a unit gradient back through T steps, record its size each step
dh = unit_vector(H)
for t in reversed(range(T)):
    da = (1 - h[t]**2) * dh      # the tanh slope factor (always <= 1)
    dh = Whh.T @ da              # multiply by the recurrent weights, again
    norms.append(np.linalg.norm(dh))
# small ||Whh|| -> norms shrink to ~0 (vanish); large ||Whh|| -> norms blow up

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking it's a bug you can code around. It's a mathematical property of repeated multiplication. You fix it by changing the architecture (next lesson), not by debugging.
  • Believing clipping fixes vanishing. Gradient clipping only tames the exploding case (it caps large gradients). You can't clip a signal back to life once it has shrunk to numerical zero.
  • Confusing "small gradient" with "small learning rate." The learning rate scales all updates equally. Vanishing specifically starves the far-back steps while recent steps still learn fine — that's what makes long-range learning fail.
  • Assuming deeper always means better. Very deep plain networks (in layers or in time) suffer the same compounding. The cure — an additive shortcut for the gradient — is the same idea in both cases (ResNets in depth, LSTMs in time).

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Training multiplies the same factor once per step — a product that's exponential in the distance between two events.
  • Factor < 1 → vanishing: far-back steps get no learning signal, so long-range dependencies can't be learned.
  • Factor > 1 → exploding: updates blow up to "NaN".
  • Gradient clipping cures exploding (cap the size) but cannot cure vanishing.
  • The real fix is architectural — a memory path that updates by addition, so the factor stays near 1 (the LSTM, next).

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. Fundamentally, why do gradients vanish or explode in a long unrolled RNN?

  • Because tanh is discontinuous.
    tanh is smooth; the issue is the repeated multiplication, not a jump.
  • The gradient is a product of many similar factors, which is exponential in length.
    Correct. Repeated multiplication by roughly the same factor grows or shrinks geometrically with the number of steps.
  • Because the learning rate is too small.
    The learning rate scales all updates uniformly; it doesn't create the length-dependence.
  • Because the data is noisy.
    It happens even on clean data — it's structural.

Q2. Which problem can gradient clipping solve?

  • Vanishing gradients.
    Clipping only shrinks large gradients; it can't restore a signal that decayed to ~0.
  • Overfitting.
    Clipping is about gradient size, not generalization.
  • Exploding gradients.
    Correct. Clipping caps gradients whose size exceeds a threshold, taming the explosion while keeping direction.
  • Slow data loading.
    Unrelated to input pipelines.

Q3. On the log-scale plot, why are the vanishing and exploding curves straight lines?

  • Because the axes are linear.
    The vertical axis is logarithmic — that's exactly why exponentials look straight.
  • Because the gradient is constant.
    It's changing fast; only the balanced case is roughly flat.
  • Coincidence of the random seed.
    The shape is structural, not a seed artefact.
  • An exponential plotted on a log axis becomes a straight line.
    Correct. \(\log(c^{T}) = T\log c\) is a straight line in \(T\), so geometric decay/growth appears straight.

Q4. What should a memory path do to avoid vanishing gradients?

  • Update by addition, so the gradient passes through with a factor near 1.
    Correct. An additive carry (like the LSTM cell state) gives the gradient a near-constant highway instead of a repeated multiply.
  • Stack many nonlinear squashes in a row.
    More squashing makes vanishing worse, not better.
  • Use a larger recurrent weight everywhere.
    That risks exploding; the goal is a factor near 1, not simply bigger.
  • Just add more layers.
    Depth alone doesn't create a protected gradient path; the update rule must.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) Using the interactive, find (roughly) the weight size where the signal stays about the same over 40 steps. Why is that value a knife-edge?

Show solution

It's near a factor of 1 (weight size around 1). Just below, the signal vanishes; just above, it explodes — both exponentially, so even a small step off the edge compounds into a huge difference over 40 steps. Real training almost never sits exactly there for long, which is why plain RNNs are so hard to train on long sequences. Gated cells replace this fragile balancing act with a built-in factor-≈1 path, so you never have to walk the knife-edge.

2. (transfer) The tanh slope is \(1-h^2\), which is at most 1 and near 0 when the unit "saturates" (\(|h|\) close to 1). How does saturation make vanishing worse?

Show solution

When a unit saturates, its slope \(1-h^2\) is nearly 0, so that step multiplies the backward signal by almost nothing — an extra source of vanishing on top of the weight factor. Saturated units are effectively "dead" for learning. This is why keeping activations in their responsive middle range (through careful initialization and normalization) matters. Gated cells help here too, because the carried memory doesn't get squashed through a nonlinearity every single step.

3. (stretch) Very deep feed-forward networks (Track 4) also suffer vanishing gradients. What's the shared cure, and how does a "residual connection" relate to the LSTM's memory?

Show solution

Both are cured by giving the gradient an additive shortcut. A residual connection computes \(y = x + F(x)\): the input flows through the "+x" path untouched (factor 1), skipping the squashing. That's exactly the trick the LSTM's memory uses across time (its cell state is carried by addition). ResNets do it in depth, LSTMs do it in time — the same idea in two settings: an additive highway that keeps the per-step factor near 1, so the signal survives. Spotting that these are one idea is the kind of insight that makes later architectures feel inevitable.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

This diagnosis, from Bengio and colleagues in 1994, is one of the most consequential in deep learning — it explained why early neural networks stalled and pointed the way to nearly everything that followed. The additive-shortcut cure shows up everywhere: LSTMs and GRUs (next two lessons) in time; residual connections in ResNets and, crucially, inside every Transformer block (the "residual stream" that GPT-style models are built around). Even today, training enormous models is in large part a fight to keep gradients well-behaved across hundreds of layers — normalization schemes, careful initialization, and residual paths are all descendants of this single insight about repeated multiplication.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Training an RNN multiplies the same factor once per step, so the learning signal from far-back inputs either shrinks to nothing or blows up — like compound interest — which is why plain RNNs can't connect events that are far apart."
