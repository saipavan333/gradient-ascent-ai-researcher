Probability foundations & Bayes’ rule

Machine learning is reasoning under uncertainty, and probability is its grammar. We build it from three simple axioms up to Bayes’ rule — the formula for updating beliefs when new evidence arrives, and the backbone of countless ML models.

A machine learning model almost never knows the answer — it holds a belief and refines it as data arrives. The mathematics of how much to believe, and exactly how far to move when new evidence lands, is probability. In this lesson we build it from three plain-common-sense rules up to Bayes’ rule, the single formula that tells you how to update a belief in light of evidence. Bayes’ rule is the quiet engine behind spam filters, medical diagnosis, Naive Bayes classifiers, and the whole field of Bayesian machine learning — and it delivers one of the most useful shocks in all of statistics: a “99% accurate” test for a rare disease can still mean a positive result is probably a false alarm.

💡 Intuition: a detective updating a hunch

Think of yourself as a detective. Before any clue, you have a hunch about how likely a suspect is guilty — call that your prior (your belief before the evidence). A clue arrives. You don’t throw away the hunch and you don’t ignore the clue; you combine them into a sharpened belief — the posterior (your belief after the evidence). Bayes’ rule is the exact recipe for that combination. The crucial subtlety it captures: a clue’s weight depends not just on how strongly it points at the suspect, but on how rare the suspect was to begin with. A dramatic clue against a very unlikely suspect still leaves them probably innocent — because there were so many other people the clue could have implicated by chance.

Everything rests on three simple rules (the axioms of probability). A probability is a number \(P\) between 0 and 1: \(0\le P(A)\le 1\), where \(A\) is an event (a thing that might happen). The certain event has \(P=1\). And for two events that can’t both happen (mutually exclusive), the chance of “one or the other” is the sum: \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)\). From these three, all of probability follows.

The key new idea is a conditional probability — the chance of \(A\) given that \(B\) already happened, written \(P(A\mid B)\) (read “\(P\) of \(A\) given \(B\)”; the \(\mid\) bar means “given”). It is defined as the fraction of \(B\)-world in which \(A\) also happens:

\[ P(A\mid B) \;=\; \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)} \] (9.1)

Here \(P(A\text{ and }B)\) is the chance both happen, and dividing by \(P(B)\) “zooms in” to only the worlds where \(B\) is true. Rearranging (9.1) both ways (once for \(P(A\text{ and }B)\), once for \(P(B\text{ and }A)\)) and setting them equal gives Bayes’ rule.

🧭 Bayes’ rule: flipping the condition

Often you know one direction — \(P(\text{evidence}\mid\text{hypothesis})\), how likely the clue is if the hypothesis holds — but you want the other direction — \(P(\text{hypothesis}\mid\text{evidence})\), how likely the hypothesis is now that the clue arrived. Bayes’ rule flips one into the other:

\[ \underbrace{P(H\mid E)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{P(E\mid H)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{P(H)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{P(E)}_{\text{evidence}}} \] (9.2)

Symbol by symbol: \(H\) is the hypothesis (e.g. “this patient has the disease”); \(E\) is the evidence (“the test came back positive”); \(P(H)\) is the prior (how common the disease is before testing); \(P(E\mid H)\) is the likelihood (how often a sick person tests positive); and \(P(H\mid E)\) is the posterior we want (given a positive test, how likely is disease). The denominator \(P(E)\) is the total chance of seeing the evidence at all, from anyone. You compute it with the law of total probability — add up the evidence coming from each possible world:

\[ P(E) \;=\; P(E\mid H)\,P(H) \;+\; P(E\mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H) \] (9.3)

Here \(\neg H\) (“not \(H\)”) is the opposite hypothesis (“the patient is healthy”), and \(P(\neg H)=1-P(H)\). The two terms are the two ways to get a positive test: a true positive from a sick person, plus a false positive from a healthy person. That second term is exactly where rare diseases ambush our intuition — there are so many healthy people that even a small false-positive rate produces a flood of false alarms.

✍️ Worked example: the 99%-accurate test that’s usually wrong

A disease affects 1 in 100 people. The test catches 99% of sick people (sensitivity \(P(+\mid\text{sick})=0.99\)) and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people (false-positive rate \(P(+\mid\text{healthy})=0.05\)). You test positive. What’s the chance you’re actually sick? The cleanest way is natural frequencies — imagine a concrete crowd.

1
Take 10,000 people. With prevalence 1%, that’s 100 sick and 9,900 healthy.
2
True positives. Of the 100 sick, 99% test positive: \(0.99\times100 = \mathbf{99}\) true positives.
3
False positives. Of the 9,900 healthy, 5% test positive: \(0.05\times9900 = \mathbf{495}\) false positives. Notice: far more than the true positives — because “healthy” is a huge crowd.
4
Everyone who tests positive: \(99 + 495 = 594\) people. The fraction who are truly sick is \(P(\text{sick}\mid +) = \dfrac{99}{594} = 0.167\).
5
The punchline. A positive result on this “99% accurate” test means only about a 16.7% chance of actually being sick — a positive is more likely a false alarm than real. Plug the same numbers into Bayes’ rule (9.2) directly: \(P(\text{sick}\mid+)=\frac{0.99\cdot0.01}{0.99\cdot0.01+0.05\cdot0.99}=\frac{0.0099}{0.0594}=0.167\). Identical. This is why doctors retest before alarming a patient, and why base rates matter as much as accuracy.

🛠️ In code: Bayes in four lines

The whole calculation is a direct transcription of (9.2) and (9.3):

def posterior(prior, sensitivity, false_pos_rate):
    true_pos  = sensitivity   * prior          # sick people who test +
    false_pos = false_pos_rate * (1 - prior)   # healthy people who test +
    return true_pos / (true_pos + false_pos)   # P(sick | +)

print(posterior(0.01, 0.99, 0.05))   # 0.1667...  -> only ~17%!

# Retest: feed yesterday's posterior back in as today's prior.
p = posterior(0.01, 0.99, 0.05)      # 0.167 after first positive
print(posterior(p, 0.99, 0.05))      # 0.798  -> a second positive is far more convincing
Why this matters for ML. That last trick — yesterday’s posterior becomes today’s prior — is the beating heart of Bayesian learning. A model starts with a prior over its parameters, sees data, and updates to a posterior; the next batch updates it again. Naive Bayes spam filters, Kalman filters, Bayesian neural networks, and Thompson sampling in reinforcement learning are all just Bayes’ rule applied over and over. Learn it once here and you’ll recognize it everywhere.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Confusing \(P(E\mid H)\) with \(P(H\mid E)\). “99% of sick people test positive” is not “99% of positives are sick.” Flipping the bar without Bayes’ rule is the single most common error in all of applied statistics (the “prosecutor’s fallacy”).
  • Ignoring the base rate (the prior). Accuracy alone is meaningless without prevalence. For a rare condition, the huge healthy population manufactures false positives faster than the sick population makes true ones.
  • Thinking probabilities of related events add. \(P(A\text{ or }B)=P(A)+P(B)\) only when they can’t both happen. Otherwise you double-count the overlap and must subtract \(P(A\text{ and }B)\).
  • Reading a density or likelihood as a probability. \(P(E\mid H)\) is a probability here, but likelihoods in continuous models (next lessons) can exceed 1 — a likelihood is not a probability.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Conditional probability: \(P(A\mid B)=P(A\text{ and }B)/P(B)\) — zoom into the \(B\)-worlds.
  • Bayes’ rule: posterior \(=\) likelihood \(\times\) prior \(/\) evidence, \(P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E)}\).
  • Evidence (denominator): \(P(E)=P(E\mid H)P(H)+P(E\mid\neg H)P(\neg H)\) — true positives plus false positives.
  • Base rates dominate: for rare \(H\), even an accurate test yields mostly false positives.
  • Belief updating chains: today’s posterior becomes tomorrow’s prior — the core of Bayesian ML.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. In Bayes’ rule \(P(H\mid E)=\frac{P(E\mid H)P(H)}{P(E)}\), which piece is the prior?

  • \(P(H\mid E)\).
    That’s the posterior — the belief after seeing the evidence.
  • \(P(E\mid H)\).
    That’s the likelihood — how probable the evidence is if \(H\) holds.
  • \(P(H)\).
    Correct. \(P(H)\) is the prior: the probability of the hypothesis before any evidence.
  • \(P(E)\).
    That’s the evidence (marginal probability of \(E\)), the normalizing denominator.

Q2. A test is 99% sensitive with a 5% false-positive rate, and you test positive. Which fact makes the chance of actually being sick low?

  • The test is not sensitive enough.
    99% sensitivity is high; that’s not the problem.
  • The false-positive rate is astronomically large.
    5% is fairly small per person; the issue is how many people it’s applied to.
  • Positive tests are impossible to trust ever.
    Too strong — a second positive raised the posterior to ~80%. Trust depends on the base rate.
  • The disease is rare, so the huge healthy group produces many false positives.
    Correct. A low prior means false positives from the large healthy pool outnumber true positives from the tiny sick pool.

Q3. “90% of people with the flu have a fever” and “90% of people with a fever have the flu” are…

  • Different statements — they swap what’s given, so they generally differ.
    Correct. \(P(\text{fever}\mid\text{flu})\neq P(\text{flu}\mid\text{fever})\) in general; converting one to the other needs Bayes’ rule and the base rates.
  • Always exactly equal.
    Only if flu and fever were equally common, which they aren’t. Conditionals don’t flip freely.
  • Both meaningless.
    Both are perfectly meaningful conditional probabilities; they’re just not the same one.
  • The same by the law of total probability.
    Total probability builds the denominator \(P(E)\); it doesn’t make \(P(A\mid B)=P(B\mid A)\).

Q4. You test positive twice on the same test (5% false-positive rate, 99% sensitive, 1% prevalence). Why does the second positive convince you far more?

  • The test becomes more accurate on the second try.
    The test’s accuracy is unchanged; what changes is your belief going in.
  • The first positive raised your prior from 1% to ~17%, so Bayes updates from a much stronger starting point.
    Correct. Posterior becomes the new prior; starting at 17% instead of 1% pushes the second posterior to ~80%.
  • Two positives are mutually exclusive events.
    They’re not exclusive — both happened. And exclusivity wouldn’t explain the update.
  • Because \(P(E)\) becomes 1.
    The evidence probability doesn’t collapse to 1; the prior rising is what drives the jump.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, keep sensitivity 99% and false-positive rate 5%, but drag the prevalence up to 50% (a common condition). What happens to \(P(\text{sick}\mid+)\), and why?

Show solution

It jumps to about 95%. With prevalence 50% the sick and healthy groups are the same size, so true positives (\(0.99\times0.5=0.495\)) vastly outnumber false positives (\(0.05\times0.5=0.025\)): \(P(\text{sick}\mid+)=0.495/0.520\approx0.952\). The test didn’t change — only the base rate did. The lesson: the same test is trustworthy for common conditions and untrustworthy for rare ones. Accuracy is a property of the test and the population together.

2. (transfer) A spam filter flags 98% of spam (sensitivity) and 3% of real mail (false-positive rate). If 40% of incoming mail is spam, what’s the chance a flagged message really is spam?

Show solution

Prior \(P(\text{spam})=0.40\). True positives: \(0.98\times0.40=0.392\). False positives: \(0.03\times0.60=0.018\). Posterior \(=0.392/(0.392+0.018)=0.392/0.410\approx0.956\), about 95.6%. Because spam is common (40%), a flag is highly trustworthy — the opposite regime from the rare disease. This is exactly why filters can afford to be aggressive on high-spam inboxes but must be gentler where legitimate mail dominates.

3. (stretch) Show algebraically that when the prior \(P(H)\) is very small, the posterior \(P(H\mid+)\) is approximately \(\dfrac{\text{sensitivity}}{\text{false-positive rate}}\times P(H)\). What does that ratio tell you?

Show solution

Start from \(P(H\mid+)=\dfrac{s\,p}{s\,p + f\,(1-p)}\) with \(s\)=sensitivity, \(f\)=false-positive rate, \(p\)=prior. When \(p\) is tiny, \(1-p\approx1\) and \(s\,p\) is much smaller than \(f\), so the denominator \(\approx f\). Then \(P(H\mid+)\approx \dfrac{s\,p}{f} = \dfrac{s}{f}\,p\). The factor \(s/f\) is the test’s likelihood ratio: how many times more likely a positive is under disease than under health. A positive multiplies your odds by roughly \(s/f\). For the disease test, \(s/f=0.99/0.05\approx20\), so a positive makes disease about 20× more likely than the base rate — which, starting from 1%, lands near 17%. This “multiply the odds by the likelihood ratio” view is the elegant odds-form of Bayes’ rule, and it scales beautifully to chaining many pieces of evidence.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Bayes’ rule is not a historical curiosity — it is a live design principle. Spam filters were the first mass-market ML success, and they were Naive Bayes. Modern probabilistic programming languages (Stan, PyMC, NumPyro) let researchers write down priors and likelihoods and have the machine compute posteriors over millions of parameters. Reinforcement-learning agents use Bayesian “Thompson sampling” to balance exploration and exploitation. Even large language models are, under one lens, computing \(P(\text{next token}\mid\text{context})\) — a conditional probability. And the base-rate lesson keeps mattering: every time someone touts a classifier’s “99% accuracy” on a rare event, a Bayesian quietly asks about the false-positive flood. Calibrated uncertainty — knowing what you don’t know — is one of the frontier problems in trustworthy AI, and it is Bayes’ rule all the way down.

Recap — say it to a friend

“Bayes’ rule updates a belief with evidence: posterior equals likelihood times prior, divided by the total chance of the evidence. Its most useful surprise is that a very accurate test for a rare thing mostly rings false alarms — because base rates matter as much as accuracy.”
