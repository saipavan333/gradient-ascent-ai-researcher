Vector operations & geometry

There are only two things you can ever do to vectors: add them, and scale them. Astonishingly, all of linear algebra — and the entire training loop of a neural network — is built from just these two moves.

Track 1 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~22 min Builds on: Lesson 1

Last lesson a vector just sat there as an arrow. Now we make it move. It turns out there are exactly two operations that respect a vector's arrow-ness: addition (combine two arrows into one) and scalar multiplication (stretch or shrink an arrow). That's it — no vector "multiplication" or "division" here. What's remarkable is how far these two tiny moves carry you: a neuron computing a weighted sum, an optimizer nudging weights downhill, an average of embeddings — every one of them is just adding and scaling vectors. Learn to see those two operations geometrically and a huge amount of ML stops looking like magic.

💡 Intuition: two trips, one destination

Back to walking directions. Suppose you follow one instruction, \(\mathbf a =\) "3 east, 1 north," and then, from wherever you land, immediately follow a second, \(\mathbf b =\) "1 east, 2 north." Where do you end up relative to where you started? Four east and three north — you just added the trips. Adding vectors means doing one movement after another, and the sum is the single arrow from your start to your finish. It doesn't matter which trip you take first (east/north amounts just pile up), so \(\mathbf a + \mathbf b = \mathbf b + \mathbf a\) — the two ways trace out a parallelogram, and the sum is its diagonal.

Scaling is even simpler: \(2\mathbf a\) means "take trip \(\mathbf a\), but go twice as far in the same direction." A negative scalar like \(-1\) reverses the arrow (turn around and walk back); a fraction like \(0.5\) shrinks it. The direction is fixed by the sign; the length is fixed by the size. Play with both moves below before we write down the rules.

📐 The concept, built up in small steps

Step 1 — addition is componentwise. To add two vectors, add the matching components: east-with-east, north-with-north. Nothing crosses over.

\[ \mathbf a + \mathbf b = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + b_1 \\ a_2 + b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \](2.1)

Here \(a_1, a_2\) are the components of \(\mathbf a\) and \(b_1, b_2\) those of \(\mathbf b\). Geometrically this is the tip-to-tail picture: slide \(\mathbf b\) so its tail sits on \(\mathbf a\)'s tip, and the sum runs from the original tail to the final tip. Because addition just piles up matching components, order can't matter — that's the parallelogram in equation form.

Step 2 — scaling multiplies every component. To scale a vector by a number \(c\) (a "scalar" — that's all "scalar" means, an ordinary number that scales), multiply every component by \(c\).

\[ c\,\mathbf a = c\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c\,a_1 \\ c\,a_2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad 2\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad -1\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} \](2.2)

The length scales by \(|c|\) (the size of \(c\), ignoring its sign) and the direction stays put when \(c>0\), flips when \(c<0\), and collapses to the origin when \(c=0\). Subtraction is not a new operation — \(\mathbf a - \mathbf b\) just means \(\mathbf a + (-1)\mathbf b\), the arrow that points from \(\mathbf b\)'s tip to \(\mathbf a\)'s tip.

Step 3 — combine them: the linear combination. Chain the two operations and you get the single most important expression in the subject: scale some vectors and add the results.

\[ c_1\,\mathbf a + c_2\,\mathbf b \qquad(\text{a } \textbf{linear combination} \text{ of } \mathbf a \text{ and } \mathbf b) \](2.3)

This one pattern — "scale-and-add" — is everywhere in ML. A single artificial neuron computes \(w_1 x_1 + w_2 x_2 + \dots\), a linear combination of its inputs. And one step of gradient descent, the algorithm that trains essentially every model, is literally a linear combination of the current weights and the gradient:

\[ \mathbf w_{\text{new}} = \mathbf w - \eta\,\mathbf g = 1\cdot\mathbf w + (-\eta)\cdot \mathbf g \](2.4)

Here \(\mathbf w\) is the vector of weights, \(\mathbf g\) is the gradient (a vector pointing uphill in error), and \(\eta\) ("eta") is the small learning rate. Reading it as a linear combination: keep all the current weights, then add a small backward step along the gradient. Scale, and add. That's training.

✍️ Worked example: scale, add, and take a gradient step

Let \(\mathbf a = (2, 1)\) and \(\mathbf b = (1, 3)\). We'll run every operation by hand, then do one real training step.

1
Add. \(\mathbf a + \mathbf b = (2+1,\ 1+3) = (3, 4)\). Its length is \(\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5\) — the diagonal of the parallelogram.
2
Scale. \(3\mathbf a = (6, 3)\) — same direction as \(\mathbf a\), three times as long. And \(-\mathbf b = (-1,-3)\) — same length as \(\mathbf b\), opposite direction.
3
Subtract. \(\mathbf a - \mathbf b = \mathbf a + (-\mathbf b) = (2-1,\ 1-3) = (1, -2)\), the arrow pointing from \(\mathbf b\)'s tip to \(\mathbf a\)'s tip.
4
A gradient-descent step. Say weights \(\mathbf w = (0.5, 0.5)\), gradient \(\mathbf g = (2, -1)\), learning rate \(\eta = 0.1\). Then \(\mathbf w_{\text{new}} = \mathbf w - \eta\mathbf g = (0.5 - 0.1\cdot 2,\ 0.5 - 0.1\cdot(-1)) = (0.3,\ 0.6)\). That's it — the whole update is one scale (\(-\eta\) times the gradient) followed by one add. Every parameter in every neural network is trained by repeating exactly this two-move combination millions of times.

🛠️ The same thing in code

NumPy overloads + and * to work componentwise on arrays, so the code reads exactly like the math.

import numpy as np
a = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
b = np.array([1.0, 3.0])

print(a + b)        # add        -> [3. 4.]
print(3 * a)        # scale      -> [6. 3.]
print(a - b)        # subtract   -> [ 1. -2.]

# one step of gradient descent is just "scale and add":
w    = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
grad = np.array([2.0, -1.0])
eta  = 0.1
w = w - eta * grad
print(w)            # -> [0.3 0.6]
Where you'll meet this again. Momentum optimizers keep a running linear combination of past gradients; exponential moving averages (used in Adam, in batch-norm statistics, in EMA model weights) are \(v \leftarrow \beta v + (1-\beta)g\) — a scale-and-add; even "mixup" data augmentation blends two images as \(\lambda\mathbf x_1 + (1-\lambda)\mathbf x_2\). Once you notice the pattern, you'll see it on nearly every page of a deep-learning paper.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Trying to add vectors of different lengths. \((1,2) + (1,2,3)\) is undefined — addition needs the same number of components, because it pairs them up one-to-one.
  • Adding the lengths. \(\lVert \mathbf a + \mathbf b\rVert \ne \lVert\mathbf a\rVert + \lVert\mathbf b\rVert\) unless the arrows point the same way. Here \(\lVert(3,4)\rVert = 5\), but \(\lVert(2,1)\rVert + \lVert(1,3)\rVert \approx 2.24 + 3.16 = 5.4\). Add the vectors first, measure after.
  • Thinking a negative scalar changes the length's sign. Length is never negative. \(-2\mathbf a\) has length \(2\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\); the minus only flips the direction.
  • Expecting "vector times vector." There's no componentwise product that behaves like real multiplication here. The meaningful products are the dot product (next lesson) and matrix–vector products (Lesson 5) — both different beasts.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Only two operations: add (componentwise, tip-to-tail) and scale (multiply every component, stretch/flip).
  • Addition commutes: \(\mathbf a + \mathbf b = \mathbf b + \mathbf a\); the sum is the parallelogram's diagonal.
  • Scaling by \(c\) multiplies length by \(|c|\), keeps direction if \(c>0\), flips it if \(c<0\).
  • Linear combination \(c_1\mathbf a + c_2\mathbf b\) — scale-and-add — is the workhorse pattern of all of linear algebra and ML.
  • Gradient descent \(\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\mathbf g\) is a linear combination: training is repeated scale-and-add.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What is \((2, 1) + (1, 3)\)?

  • \((2, 3)\), multiplying the matching components.
    Addition adds matching components; it doesn't multiply them.
  • \((3, 3)\).
    Check the second component: \(1 + 3 = 4\), not 3.
  • \((3, 4)\).
    Correct. \((2+1,\ 1+3) = (3,4)\) — east-with-east, north-with-north.
  • \(5\), the length of the sum.
    That's the length of the sum, but the sum itself is a vector, \((3,4)\).

Q2. How does \(-2\mathbf a\) compare to \(\mathbf a\)?

  • Same direction, twice as long.
    The negative sign flips the direction; only the magnitude 2 controls length.
  • Opposite direction, twice as long.
    Correct. \(|-2| = 2\) doubles the length, and the minus sign reverses the arrow.
  • Opposite direction, negative length.
    Lengths are never negative. The arrow reverses, but its length is \(2\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\).
  • Same arrow, since scaling doesn't change vectors.
    Scaling very much changes the vector — here it flips and stretches it.

Q3. In the parallelogram picture of \(\mathbf a + \mathbf b\), what is the sum arrow?

  • One of the two sides.
    The sides are \(\mathbf a\) and \(\mathbf b\) themselves; the sum is not a side.
  • The shorter diagonal, always.
    The sum is a specific diagonal, but "shorter" isn't guaranteed — it depends on the angle between the vectors.
  • The perimeter.
    A perimeter is a length, not a vector; the sum is a single arrow.
  • The diagonal from the shared tail to the opposite corner.
    Correct. Both routes (a-then-b, b-then-a) end at that far corner, so the sum runs straight to it.

Q4. Why is the gradient-descent update \(\mathbf w \leftarrow \mathbf w - \eta\mathbf g\) called a linear combination?

  • Because it scales two vectors (\(\mathbf w\) by 1, \(\mathbf g\) by \(-\eta\)) and adds them.
    Correct. \(1\cdot\mathbf w + (-\eta)\cdot\mathbf g\) is scale-and-add — exactly a linear combination.
  • Because \(\eta\) is a straight line.
    \(\eta\) is just a small number (the learning rate), not a line.
  • Because it multiplies \(\mathbf w\) and \(\mathbf g\) together.
    There's no vector-times-vector product here; the vectors are scaled and added, not multiplied together.
  • Because it only works in a straight line of descent.
    The name refers to the algebraic form (scale-and-add), not the shape of the descent path.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) With \(\mathbf a = (2,1)\) and \(\mathbf b = (1,3)\), compute \(2\mathbf a - \mathbf b\).

Show solution

Scale first: \(2\mathbf a = (4, 2)\). Then subtract: \(2\mathbf a - \mathbf b = (4-1,\ 2-3) = (3, -1)\). (Scale-and-add, with the "add" being a negative-scaled \(\mathbf b\).)

2. (transfer) A model averages two word embeddings to represent a phrase: \(\mathbf p = \tfrac12\mathbf u + \tfrac12\mathbf v\). If \(\mathbf u = (4, 0)\) and \(\mathbf v = (0, 2)\), find \(\mathbf p\). Where does it sit relative to \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\), and what kind of combination is this?

Show solution

\(\mathbf p = (\tfrac12\cdot 4 + \tfrac12\cdot 0,\ \tfrac12\cdot 0 + \tfrac12\cdot 2) = (2, 1)\), the exact midpoint between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\mathbf v\). Because the coefficients are non-negative and sum to 1, this is a convex combination (a weighted average) — the result always lands on the line segment between the two vectors. Averaging embeddings is a real, if crude, way to combine meanings.

3. (stretch) Prove the "distributive law" \(c(\mathbf a + \mathbf b) = c\mathbf a + c\mathbf b\) from the componentwise definitions, and say in one sentence why it matters for scaling a whole gradient at once.

Show solution

Work componentwise: \(c(\mathbf a + \mathbf b) = c\,(a_1+b_1,\ a_2+b_2) = (c(a_1+b_1),\ c(a_2+b_2))\). Ordinary real-number distributivity gives \(c(a_i+b_i) = ca_i + cb_i\), so this equals \((ca_1 + cb_1,\ ca_2 + cb_2) = c\mathbf a + c\mathbf b\). It matters because it means you can multiply an entire gradient vector by the learning rate in one stroke and get the same result as scaling each component separately — the identity that makes vectorized (whole-array) computation valid and fast.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

It is genuinely surprising how much of frontier AI is "just" adding and scaling vectors. A Transformer's attention output is a weighted sum (linear combination) of value vectors. Residual connections — the trick that makes 100-layer networks trainable — add a layer's input back to its output: \(\mathbf x + f(\mathbf x)\), pure vector addition. Diffusion models generate images by repeatedly scaling and adding noise vectors. And the celebrated "king − man + woman ≈ queen" analogy in word embeddings is nothing but three vector additions. The two humble operations you practiced here are the load-bearing walls of the whole edifice.

Recap — say it to a friend

"You can only do two things to vectors: add them tip-to-tail (the parallelogram diagonal) and scale them (stretch or flip). Chaining those gives a linear combination — scale-and-add — and that single pattern is a neuron's weighted sum, a gradient-descent step, and half of every deep-learning paper."
