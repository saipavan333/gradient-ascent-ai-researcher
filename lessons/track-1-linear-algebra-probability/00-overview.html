Welcome — and how to learn this

Every model you will ever build — a spam filter, a face recognizer, ChatGPT — runs on two ideas from this track: lists of numbers (vectors) and grids of numbers (matrices). This lesson shows you the objects you are about to master and, just as important, the way to learn them so they actually stick.

Here is a secret that makes the rest of this course easier: modern AI is not a pile of a thousand unrelated tricks. Underneath, almost everything is linear algebra — the mathematics of lists of numbers and the operations that transform them. A word becomes a list of numbers. An image becomes a grid of numbers. A neural network is mostly one operation, multiply a grid of numbers by a list of numbers, repeated millions of times. Learn that language well, and the fancy stuff later stops looking like magic and starts looking like sentences you can read.

💡 Intuition: learn like a cook, not like a photocopier

Imagine two people trying to learn to cook. The first memorizes 200 recipes word for word. Change one ingredient and they are lost. The second learns a handful of techniques — how heat, salt, acid, and fat behave — and can then invent dishes they have never seen. This course is built for the second kind of learner. The "techniques" here are a small set of ideas — vectors, the dot product, matrices as transformations — and once you own them, the hundreds of ML "recipes" become variations you can reason about instead of spells you have to memorize.

The way to get there is a loop, and every lesson follows it. First build an intuition — a mental picture or everyday analogy. Then pin that picture down with a little math — precise symbols so there is no hand-waving. Then turn the math into code you can run. Then experiment — poke it, break it, watch what changes. Around the loop again for the next idea. Reading alone feels productive but fades fast; it is the doing — recalling, coding, testing yourself — that carves the knowledge in.

📐 A one-minute preview of the whole track

A vector is just an ordered list of numbers — like \((3, 2)\) — that we can also draw as an arrow pointing from the origin (the point \((0,0)\)) to that spot on a grid. The first number says how far to go across; the second says how far up. That is the entire idea, and it is Lesson 1.

\[ \mathbf{v} = (3, 2) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \text{"3 across, 2 up"} \] (0.1)

Because a vector is an arrow, it has a length — the straight-line distance from its start to its tip. We get it with the Pythagorean theorem you already know: square each component (each number in the list), add them, take the square root. The double bars \(\lVert\,\cdot\,\rVert\) are just notation for "length of."

\[ \lVert \mathbf{v} \rVert = \sqrt{3^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{13} \approx 3.61 \] (0.2)

From these arrows we will build everything: adding and scaling them (Lesson 2), measuring the angle between two of them with the dot product (Lesson 3), combining them to reach anywhere in space (Lesson 4), and finally matrices — grids of numbers that move every arrow at once, which is exactly what a layer of a neural network does (Lessons 5–6). Try the arrow below; it is a live preview of Lesson 1.

✍️ Worked example: the learning loop on one idea

Let us run the four-step loop on a single question — "how long is the arrow \((3,2)\)?" — so you can see the rhythm every lesson will use.

1
Intuition. Walk 3 steps east, then 2 steps north. The length is the straight-line distance back to where you started — the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs 3 and 2.
2
Math. Pythagoras: \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=\sqrt{3^2+2^2}=\sqrt{9+4}=\sqrt{13}\). No hand-waving — every step is forced.
3
Compute. \(\sqrt{13}=3.6055\ldots\approx 3.61\). In code this is one line: np.sqrt(3**2 + 2**2).
4
Experiment. Drag the widget to \((4,3)\): the length reads exactly \(5\), because \(\sqrt{4^2+3^2}=\sqrt{25}=5\). You just tested the formula instead of trusting it. Do this on every lesson and the ideas will stop being things you "saw once" and become things you know.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Reading as if it were a novel. Passive reading feels smooth but leaks out within days. After each section, close the page and try to say the idea out loud. If you cannot, you have not learned it yet — and that is useful to know now, not on a later lesson that depends on it.
  • Skipping the math because "I get the idea." The picture and the symbols are two views of the same thing. The symbols are what let you be exact and what the code is written in. Skipping them is where "I sort of understand" quietly becomes "I am totally lost" three lessons later.
  • Never running the code. Ten minutes of typing and breaking a snippet teaches more than an hour of reading it. The whole course is built from-scratch precisely so you can open the hood.
  • Cramming instead of spacing. One lesson a day, revisited, beats seven lessons in one sitting. Sleep is part of learning, not a break from it.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • A vector is an ordered list of numbers — and also an arrow from the origin. Both views are the same object.
  • Its length is Pythagoras: \(\lVert\mathbf{v}\rVert=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+\cdots}\), one term per component.
  • Almost all of ML is vectors, matrices, and operations on them — this track is the alphabet for everything after.
  • The learning loop: intuition → math → code → experiment, repeated. Doing beats reading.
  • Test yourself and space it out. Retrieval and spacing are the two most reliable ways to make knowledge last.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory first, then read why.

Q1. What is a vector, in the simplest terms?

  • A single number.
    A single number is a scalar. A vector is a whole ordered list of numbers.
  • An ordered list of numbers, which we can also picture as an arrow.
    Correct. The two views — a list like \((3,2)\) and an arrow to that point — describe the same object.
  • A grid (rows and columns) of numbers.
    That is a matrix, which comes in Lesson 5. A vector is a single list.
  • A kind of computer program.
    No — it is a mathematical object. Programs will operate on vectors, but a vector is just the data.

Q2. The vector \((6,8)\) has what length?

  • 14, because \(6+8=14\).
    Length is not the sum of components — you must square, add, then square-root.
  • 48, because \(6\times 8=48\).
    That multiplies the components; length uses squares and a square root, not a product.
  • 10, because \(\sqrt{6^2+8^2}=\sqrt{100}\).
    Correct. \(36+64=100\) and \(\sqrt{100}=10\) — another Pythagorean triple.
  • You cannot tell without more information.
    You can — the two components are all the length formula needs.

Q3. Why does this course build everything "from scratch" in code?

  • Because libraries like NumPy are not allowed.
    We use NumPy freely; "from scratch" means we also show what is happening underneath, not that we avoid tools.
  • To make the lessons longer.
    Length is not the goal — understanding is. Running code is the fastest route to it.
  • Because typing and breaking code teaches the idea far faster than only reading it.
    Correct. Active experimentation is one of the most reliable ways to actually learn a concept.
  • Because real ML never uses libraries.
    Real ML uses libraries constantly. We open the hood so that when you use them, you know what they do.

Q4. According to the learning loop, what should you do right after reading a section?

  • Look away and try to restate the idea in your own words.
    Correct. Retrieval — pulling the idea out of your own head — is what makes it stick, far more than re-reading.
  • Immediately read the next three sections.
    Rushing ahead skips the step that actually cements the idea and sets up confusion later.
  • Highlight it in a bright color and move on.
    Highlighting feels productive but is passive; it does little for memory on its own.
  • Nothing — understanding it once is enough.
    Understanding once is not remembering. Without retrieval and spacing, most of it fades within days.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) By hand, find the length of the vector \((5,12)\). (Hint: it is another famous Pythagorean triple.)

Show solution

\(\lVert(5,12)\rVert=\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=\sqrt{25+144}=\sqrt{169}=13\). Square each component, add, square-root. The exact same three-step recipe works no matter how many components a vector has.

2. (transfer) A house is described by its size in square feet, its number of bedrooms, and its age in years: 1500, 3, 30. Write this house as a vector and say what its "dimension" (number of components) is. Why is turning objects into vectors useful?

Show solution

The house is the vector \((1500,\,3,\,30)\), a 3-dimensional vector (three components). Turning a real thing into a list of numbers — a feature vector — is what lets a computer compare, add, and learn from it. Every ML model starts by turning its input (a house, a photo, a sentence) into a vector; the rest of the course is what we do with those vectors once we have them.

3. (stretch) Design your own study plan for this track using the learning loop. For one upcoming idea, write down what you would do at each of the four steps (intuition, math, code, experiment).

Show solution

There is no single right answer, but a strong plan for, say, "the dot product" might read: intuition — sketch two arrows and ask "how much do they point the same way?"; math — write \(\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=a_1b_1+a_2b_2\) and \(=\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\lVert\mathbf b\rVert\cos\theta\); code — implement it as a loop and check it against np.dot; experiment — rotate one arrow and watch the number go positive, zero, then negative. The habit matters more than the exact words: pass every idea through all four steps and space your review across days.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

The reason this humble arrow deserves a whole track: at the largest scale of AI, it is still the whole game. A large language model turns every word into a vector of thousands of numbers (an embedding), and its core operation — attention — is built from dot products between those vectors, the exact tool you meet in Lesson 3. The reason such models need GPUs is that GPUs are specialized hardware for one thing: multiplying big grids of numbers (matrices) by big lists of numbers (vectors), fast — the operation of Lessons 5–6. Master this track and you are not learning a warm-up; you are learning the machinery that the frontier runs on.
