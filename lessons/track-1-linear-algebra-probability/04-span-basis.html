Linear combinations, span & basis

Given a handful of vectors, which points can you reach by scaling and adding them? The answer — and the idea of a “smallest set that reaches everything” — is the backbone of dimension, coordinates, and later PCA.

Track 1 · Lesson 4 Concept + code + interactive ~26 min Builds on: Lessons 1–3

In Lesson 2 we learned the "scale-and-add" move — the linear combination. Now we ask the natural follow-up: if you're handed a few vectors and allowed to scale each by any amount and add them up, what set of points can you hit? That reachable set is called the span, and thinking about it carefully leads straight to the three ideas this lesson is named for: span (everything you can reach), linear independence (whether any vector is redundant), and basis (a smallest, non-wasteful set that reaches everything). These are the ideas that let us say a space is "2-dimensional," give every point coordinates, and — later — compress data by keeping only the directions that matter.

💡 Intuition: how far can two ingredients take you?

Think of two vectors as two "ingredients" and a linear combination as a recipe: use \(c_1\) of the first and \(c_2\) of the second. If your ingredients are "1 step east" and "1 step north," then by choosing the amounts you can reach any point on the plane — every location is some amount east plus some amount north. Those two ingredients span the whole plane. But if your two ingredients both point east (say "1 step east" and "2 steps east"), no recipe will ever move you north; you're stuck on a single line. The second ingredient was redundant — it added nothing the first couldn't already do.

That contrast is the whole lesson in miniature. Two vectors that genuinely point in different directions reach a plane; two that lie along the same line reach only that line. "Genuinely different directions" is what linear independence means, and a set that is both independent and reaches everything is a basis. 📐 The concept, built up in small steps

Step 1 — the span is the set of all linear combinations. Given vectors \(\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2\), their span is every point you can write as a scale-and-add of them, letting the coefficients range over all real numbers:

\[ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2\} = \left\{\, c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 \;:\; c_1, c_2 \in \mathbb{R} \,\right\} \](4.1)

The curly braces with a colon read "the set of all things of the form \(c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2\), where \(c_1\) and \(c_2\) are any real numbers." For two vectors pointing different ways in 2-D, that set is the entire plane. For a single vector, it's the line through it. For two vectors on the same line, it's still just that line.

Step 2 — linear independence: is anyone redundant? A set of vectors is linearly independent if the only way to combine them into the zero vector is to use zero of each. If some nonzero recipe also lands on zero, they're dependent — one of them can be built from the others, so it adds nothing to the span.

\[ c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 = \mathbf 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad c_1 = c_2 = 0 \qquad(\text{independent}) \](4.2)

In 2-D there's a one-line test: two vectors \(\mathbf v_1 = (a, b)\) and \(\mathbf v_2 = (c, d)\) are independent exactly when the number \(ad - bc\) — the determinant, coming in Lesson 7 — is nonzero. If \(ad - bc = 0\), they lie on the same line and are dependent.

\[ ad - bc \ne 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{independent (span the plane)}; \qquad ad - bc = 0 \;\Leftrightarrow\; \text{dependent (span a line)} \](4.3)

Step 3 — a basis: independent AND spanning. A basis for a space is a set of vectors that is (a) linearly independent — no waste — and (b) spans the space — reaches everything. For the 2-D plane, any two independent vectors form a basis; you always need exactly two (one is too few to reach the plane, three must contain a redundancy). That fixed count is the dimension of the space.

Step 4 — a basis gives coordinates. The real payoff: once you fix a basis, every point in the span is a unique recipe \(c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2\), and those coefficients \((c_1, c_2)\) are the point's coordinates in that basis. The familiar \((x, y)\) you've used all along are just the coordinates in the standard basis \(\mathbf e_1 = (1,0)\), \(\mathbf e_2 = (0,1)\). Choosing a smarter basis — one aligned with the structure of your data — is exactly what PCA does.

\[ \mathbf p = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mathbf p \text{ has coordinates } (c_1, c_2) \text{ in the basis } \{\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2\} \](4.4)

✍️ Worked example: are these a basis, and what are the coordinates of (5, 5)?

Take \(\mathbf v_1 = (2, 1)\) and \(\mathbf v_2 = (1, 3)\). We'll test independence, then find the recipe for the target \(\mathbf p = (5, 5)\).

1
Independence test. \(ad - bc = (2)(3) - (1)(1) = 6 - 1 = 5 \ne 0\). Nonzero, so \(\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2\) are independent — they form a basis for the plane.
2
Set up the recipe. We want \(c_1(2,1) + c_2(1,3) = (5,5)\), which is two equations: \(2c_1 + c_2 = 5\) (first components) and \(c_1 + 3c_2 = 5\) (second components).
3
Solve. From the first equation \(c_2 = 5 - 2c_1\). Substitute into the second: \(c_1 + 3(5 - 2c_1) = 5 \Rightarrow c_1 + 15 - 6c_1 = 5 \Rightarrow -5c_1 = -10 \Rightarrow c_1 = 2\), then \(c_2 = 5 - 4 = 1\).
4
Check and interpret. \(2(2,1) + 1(1,3) = (4,2) + (1,3) = (5,5)\) ✓. So in the basis \(\{\mathbf v_1, \mathbf v_2\}\), the point \((5,5)\) has coordinates \((2, 1)\). The same physical point wears different coordinates in different bases — \((5,5)\) in the standard basis, \((2,1)\) in this one. Changing basis is just relabeling the same space, and it's the heart of PCA, Fourier analysis, and every "change of representation" trick in ML.

🛠️ The same thing in code

Finding the recipe means solving a small linear system — one np.linalg.solve call. Checking independence is one determinant.

import numpy as np
v1 = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
v2 = np.array([1.0, 3.0])
B  = np.column_stack([v1, v2])      # basis as matrix columns: [[2,1],[1,3]]

print(np.linalg.det(B))             # 5.0  -> nonzero, so independent (a basis)

p = np.array([5.0, 5.0])
coords = np.linalg.solve(B, p)      # solve B @ coords = p
print(coords)                       # -> [2. 1.]   the coordinates of p in this basis
print(B @ coords)                   # -> [5. 5.]   reconstruct p, confirming the recipe

dep = np.column_stack([[1,2],[2,4]]) # second column is 2x the first
print(np.linalg.det(dep))            # 0.0  -> dependent, spans only a line
Why "dimension" is the quantity ML obsesses over. The number of vectors in a basis — the dimension — is the number of independent "knobs" a space has. Embeddings live in a few hundred dimensions; images in tens of thousands. PCA finds a new basis ordered so the first few directions capture most of the data's variation, letting you throw the rest away — dimensionality reduction is literally "pick a better, smaller basis." Rank, degrees of freedom, and the "intrinsic dimension" of a dataset are all this same idea.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking more vectors always means a bigger span. Adding a vector that's already in the span (a redundant one) grows the set of vectors but not the span. Three vectors in a plane still only span the plane.
  • Confusing "independent" with "perpendicular." Independent just means "not on the same line" — the arrows can meet at any angle except \(0^\circ\) or \(180^\circ\). Perpendicular is a special, extra-nice case, not the requirement.
  • Believing coordinates are absolute. A point's coordinates depend entirely on the chosen basis. \((5,5)\) and \((2,1)\) named the same point above. Always ask "coordinates in which basis?"
  • Forgetting you need exactly \(n\) independent vectors for \(\mathbb{R}^n\). Too few can't reach everything; too many must contain a dependency. A basis is the Goldilocks count.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Span = all linear combinations \(c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2\); it's a line, a plane, etc.
  • Independent = the only combination giving \(\mathbf 0\) is the all-zero one; in 2-D, test \(ad - bc \ne 0\).
  • Basis = independent + spanning; a smallest set that reaches everything.
  • Dimension = the number of vectors in any basis (always the same count).
  • Coordinates = the unique coefficients \((c_1, c_2)\) for a point in a chosen basis; changing basis relabels the same space.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What is the span of \(\{(1, 0),\ (0, 1)\}\)?

  • Just the two points \((1,0)\) and \((0,1)\).
    Span includes all scale-and-adds, not only the original vectors — far more than two points.
  • The line \(y = x\).
    These two independent vectors reach much more than one line; they cover the whole plane.
  • A single line through the origin.
    One line would be the span of a single vector; two independent ones open up a plane.
  • All of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (the whole plane).
    Correct. Any \((x,y) = x(1,0) + y(0,1)\), so every point is reachable — this is the standard basis.

Q2. Are \(\mathbf v_1 = (1, 2)\) and \(\mathbf v_2 = (2, 4)\) linearly independent?

  • Yes, because they have different components.
    Different-looking components can still be scalar multiples; check the determinant.
  • No — \(\mathbf v_2 = 2\mathbf v_1\), and \(ad - bc = (1)(4) - (2)(2) = 0\).
    Correct. One is twice the other, the determinant is 0, and they span only a line.
  • Yes, because neither is the zero vector.
    Being nonzero isn't enough; two nonzero vectors on the same line are still dependent.
  • It's impossible to tell without more information.
    Two 2-D vectors are always testable: compute \(ad - bc\). Here it's 0, so: dependent.

Q3. How many vectors are in a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^2\)?

  • As many as you like, as long as they span.
    A basis must be independent too, so it can't have extras. Spanning alone isn't the whole definition.
  • Exactly 1.
    One vector spans only a line; you can't reach the whole plane with it.
  • Exactly 2 (independent) vectors.
    Correct. Two independent vectors span the plane with none to spare — that's the dimension, 2.
  • Exactly 3.
    Any 3 vectors in the plane must contain a redundancy, so they can't all be part of a basis.

Q4. A point has coordinates \((2, 1)\) in the basis \(\{(2,1), (1,3)\}\). What point is it in the standard basis?

  • \((5, 5)\), from \(2(2,1) + 1(1,3)\).
    Correct. Coordinates are the recipe: \(2(2,1) + 1(1,3) = (4,2)+(1,3) = (5,5)\).
  • \((2, 1)\); coordinates never change.
    Coordinates absolutely change with the basis — that's the whole point of this lesson.
  • \((3, 4)\), by adding the two basis vectors.
    That would be \(1(2,1) + 1(1,3)\), but the recipe here uses \(c_1 = 2\), not 1.
  • \((4, 2)\), using only the first basis vector.
    That's just \(2\mathbf v_1\); you dropped the \(+1\mathbf v_2 = (1,3)\) term.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) Do \(\mathbf v_1 = (3, 1)\) and \(\mathbf v_2 = (-1, 2)\) form a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^2\)? Justify with the determinant.

Show solution

\(ad - bc = (3)(2) - (1)(-1) = 6 + 1 = 7 \ne 0\). Nonzero, so the two vectors are independent and therefore span the plane — yes, they're a basis. (Any nonzero determinant would do; the exact value 7 also happens to be the area of the parallelogram they form, a preview of Lesson 7.)

2. (transfer) A dataset of 2-D points happens to lie exactly along the line spanned by \((2, 1)\). If you run PCA and keep just one direction, which vector should it pick, and how many numbers now describe each point instead of two?

Show solution

All the data lies in \(\operatorname{span}\{(2,1)\}\) — a 1-dimensional subspace — so PCA's top direction is (a unit vector along) \((2, 1)\). Each point \(\mathbf p = c\,(2,1)\) is now fully described by the single coordinate \(c\) along that direction; the second coordinate is always 0 and carries no information. So you've gone from 2 numbers to 1 per point with zero loss. That's the ideal case of dimensionality reduction: the data's intrinsic dimension (1) was smaller than its ambient dimension (2).

3. (stretch) Prove that two vectors lying on the same line through the origin can never be a basis for \(\mathbb{R}^2\), using the definition of the span.

Show solution

Suppose \(\mathbf v_2 = k\mathbf v_1\) for some scalar \(k\) (that's what "same line" means). Then any combination \(c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2\mathbf v_2 = c_1\mathbf v_1 + c_2 k\mathbf v_1 = (c_1 + c_2 k)\mathbf v_1\) is itself just a multiple of \(\mathbf v_1\). So the entire span is \(\operatorname{span}\{\mathbf v_1\}\) — the single line through \(\mathbf v_1\) — no matter what \(c_1, c_2\) you choose. Since a line is not all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\) (it misses every point off the line), the set fails to span the plane, so it cannot be a basis. Equivalently, \(\mathbf v_2 - k\mathbf v_1 = \mathbf 0\) is a nonzero recipe hitting \(\mathbf 0\), the direct definition of dependence.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

"How many independent directions does this data really need?" is one of the deepest questions in ML, and it's a span-and-basis question. PCA picks a basis aligned with a dataset's variation and keeps only the top directions — the engine behind eigenfaces, gene-expression analysis, and countless compression schemes. The "rank" of a weight matrix — the dimension of its span — is what LoRA exploits to fine-tune giant language models by editing only a handful of directions instead of billions of parameters. And the surprising observation that high-dimensional data (images, text embeddings) often lives near a low-dimensional surface — the "manifold hypothesis" — is why deep learning works at all. Every one of these rests on the idea you just dragged into place: a few good basis vectors can reach a whole world.

Recap — say it to a friend

"The span is everything you can reach by scaling and adding your vectors. If none of them is redundant they're independent; an independent set that reaches everything is a basis, and the number you need is the dimension. Fix a basis and every point gets unique coordinates — and choosing a smarter basis is exactly what PCA does."
