Matrix multiplication = doing one transformation after another

The rule for multiplying matrices looks arbitrary when you first meet it. It isn't. It's forced on us by one simple demand: the product of two matrices must be the single matrix that does what both transformations do, one after the other.

In the last lesson you learned that a matrix is a transformation — it takes a vector and moves it (rotates, stretches, shears). So a natural question follows: what if you do two transformations in a row — first rotate, then stretch? The answer is another transformation, and the matrix that represents it is exactly the matrix product of the two. That single idea explains the whole multiplication rule, tells you why order matters, and is the reason a deep neural network — a stack of matrix transformations — works at all.

💡 Intuition: chaining two machines

Think of a matrix as a machine that reshapes space. Matrix \(A\) is one machine, matrix \(B\) is another. If you feed a vector \(v\) into \(B\) first and then feed the result into \(A\), you've applied \(A\) after \(B\): the output is \(A(Bv)\). Now here's the point — that whole two-step process is itself a single linear transformation, so there must be one matrix, call it \(C\), that does it in one shot: \(Cv = A(Bv)\) for every \(v\). We define the product \(AB\) to be exactly that matrix \(C\). Multiplication of matrices is nothing more than "bottle up two transformations into one."

Written with the composition symbol, \((AB)v = A(Bv)\). Read right to left: \(B\) acts first, then \(A\). That right-to-left order feels backwards but it's just function notation — like \(f(g(x))\), where \(g\) runs first. Working out what \(C\)'s entries must be (so that \(Cv\) always equals \(A(Bv)\)) gives the familiar "row times column" rule: entry \((i,j)\) of \(AB\) is the dot product of row \(i\) of \(A\) with column \(j\) of \(B\). The rule isn't a definition to memorize — it's the consequence of wanting composition.

\[ (AB)_{ij} \;=\; \sum_k A_{ik}\,B_{kj} \qquad\text{(row } i \text{ of } A \cdot \text{column } j \text{ of } B) \] (6.1)

This also explains the shape rule — why an \(m\times n\) matrix can only multiply an \(n\times p\) matrix. For "row of \(A\) dot column of \(B\)" to make sense, \(A\)'s rows and \(B\)'s columns must be the same length; the inner dimensions must match, and they vanish, leaving an \(m\times p\) result. It's the transformations lining up: \(B\) outputs vectors that \(A\) must be able to accept as input.

✍️ Worked example: multiply two 2×2 matrices

Let \(A=\begin{bmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\) (a shear) and \(B=\begin{bmatrix}2&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) (a stretch). Compute \(AB\) using "row of A dot column of B."

1
Top-left = row 1 of \(A\) · column 1 of \(B\) = \((1,2)\cdot(2,0) = 1\cdot2 + 2\cdot0 = 2\).
2
Top-right = row 1 of \(A\) · column 2 of \(B\) = \((1,2)\cdot(0,3) = 1\cdot0 + 2\cdot3 = 6\).
3
Bottom row = row 2 of \(A\) · each column = \((0,1)\cdot(2,0)=0\) and \((0,1)\cdot(0,3)=3\). So \(AB=\begin{bmatrix}2&6\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\).
4
Now flip the order. \(BA = \begin{bmatrix}2&4\\0&3\end{bmatrix}\) — different! \(AB\ne BA\): stretching then shearing is not the same as shearing then stretching. Order is not a technicality; it changes the geometry. This is the single most important thing to remember about matrix multiplication.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Assuming \(AB = BA\). Matrix multiplication is not commutative — order changes the result, because "do A then B" is a different process than "do B then A." This trips up almost everyone at first.
  • Reading \(ABv\) left to right. The rightmost matrix acts first. \(ABv\) means "apply \(B\) to \(v\), then \(A\)" — exactly like \(f(g(x))\).
  • Multiplying mismatched shapes. The inner dimensions must match (\(m\times n\) times \(n\times p\)). If they don't, the product simply doesn't exist — the second transformation can't accept the first one's output.
  • Confusing matrix product with element-wise product. Matrix multiplication is row-dot-column composition, not "multiply matching entries." (Element-wise, the Hadamard product, is a different, rarer operation.)

🔑 Key results to remember

  • \(AB\) is the single matrix that does \(B\) then \(A\): \((AB)v = A(Bv)\). Multiplication = composition.
  • Entry rule: \((AB)_{ij}\) = row \(i\) of \(A\) · column \(j\) of \(B\) — a direct consequence of composition, not an arbitrary rule.
  • Order matters: \(AB \ne BA\) in general.
  • Shapes must line up: \((m\times n)(n\times p) = (m\times p)\); inner dimensions match and cancel.
  • Deep nets are matrix products: stacking layers = composing transformations (with nonlinearities between them, Track 6).

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What does the product \(AB\) represent?

  • Multiplying matching entries of \(A\) and \(B\).
    That's the element-wise (Hadamard) product, a different operation.
  • The single transformation that applies \(B\) first, then \(A\).
    Correct. \((AB)v = A(Bv)\) — composition of the two transformations into one.
  • The average of the two matrices.
    No averaging is involved in a matrix product.
  • \(A\) plus \(B\).
    That's matrix addition, which is element-wise and unrelated.

Q2. In \(ABv\), which transformation acts on \(v\) first?

  • \(A\).
    \(A\) is leftmost, so it acts last.
  • Both at once.
    They act in sequence, not simultaneously.
  • Neither — order doesn't matter.
    Order matters a great deal.
  • \(B\) (the rightmost matrix).
    Correct. Like \(f(g(x))\), the innermost/rightmost acts first.

Q3. Is \(AB = BA\) in general?

  • No — order changes the result.
    Correct. "Do A then B" is a different process from "do B then A," so the products differ.
  • Yes, always.
    Only in special cases; generally they differ.
  • Only for 2×2 matrices.
    Non-commutativity isn't limited by size.
  • Only if they're square.
    Even square matrices generally don't commute.

Q4. You can multiply an \(m\times n\) matrix by a \(p\times q\) matrix only if…

  • \(m=p\).
    The outer dimensions don't need to match.
  • \(m=q\).
    Not the constraint; the inner dimensions are what must agree.
  • \(n=p\) (the inner dimensions match).
    Correct. Row-of-A · column-of-B needs equal lengths, so \(A\)'s columns must equal \(B\)'s rows; the result is \(m\times q\).
  • All four numbers are equal.
    Far too strict; only the inner dimensions must match.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) Compute \(\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\3&7\end{bmatrix}\). What's special about the first matrix?

Show solution

The result is \(\begin{bmatrix}5&2\\3&7\end{bmatrix}\) — unchanged. The first matrix is the identity \(I\): the "do nothing" transformation, the matrix equivalent of the number 1. \(IM = MI = M\) for any \(M\). It's your reference point for everything else (and the thing an inverse matrix multiplies back to).

2. (transfer) Using the interactive, apply rotate-then-stretch, then stretch-then-rotate on the same square. Describe how the two results differ, and connect it to \(AB\ne BA\).

Show solution

Rotate-then-stretch stretches along the original axes after the shape has turned, so the stretch cuts across the rotated square diagonally; stretch-then-rotate stretches first (along the axes) and then spins the already-stretched shape. The final parallelograms point different ways and have different proportions. That visible difference is \(AB\ne BA\): the matrix products for the two orders are genuinely different matrices, producing different geometry.

3. (stretch) A neural network layer computes \(a = f(Wx)\). A two-layer network (ignoring the nonlinearity \(f\)) computes \(W_2(W_1 x)\). Why is the nonlinearity essential for depth to add power?

Show solution

Without \(f\), \(W_2(W_1 x) = (W_2 W_1)x\) — by composition, the two layers collapse into a single matrix \(W_2 W_1\), so two layers do exactly what one layer could. Depth would be pointless. The nonlinearity between them breaks the composition (you can't absorb \(f\) into a matrix), so each layer genuinely adds expressive power. This is the exact same "linear layers collapse" fact you'll prove again in Track 6 — and it all rests on the composition meaning of matrix multiplication you learned here.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Matrix multiplication is, quite literally, what modern AI spends most of its time doing. Every layer of every neural network is a matrix product; training a large model is trillions of them. That's why GPUs and TPUs exist — they're machines built to multiply matrices at staggering speed, and the whole hardware race (and much of AI's cost and energy use) is a race to do this one operation faster. The "composition" view you learned also underlies why a deep network can build complex functions from simple parts: each layer composes onto the last. A humble row-times-column rule turns out to be the engine of the entire field.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Multiplying two matrices gives the one matrix that does both transformations in sequence — that's why the row-times-column rule exists, why order matters (AB ≠ BA), and why stacking neural-network layers is just composing matrices."
