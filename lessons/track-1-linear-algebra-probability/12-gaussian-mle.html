The Gaussian, maximum likelihood, and the bridge to ML

One bell-shaped curve shows up everywhere in nature and machine learning. Pair it with one principle — pick the parameters that make your data most probable — and you've derived, from first principles, why we minimise squared error.

This lesson ties the whole probability half of Track 1 into machine learning. First, the Gaussian (the bell curve, or normal distribution) — the most important distribution in all of statistics, because sums of many small random effects tend toward it (the Central Limit Theorem). Second, maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) — the master principle for fitting any model: choose the parameters that make the observed data as probable as possible. Put them together and something beautiful falls out: fitting a model under a Gaussian noise assumption is exactly minimising mean squared error. Every regression you'll ever run is secretly maximum likelihood with a bell curve.

💡 Intuition: which bell curve best explains the data?

A Gaussian is described by two numbers: its mean \(\mu\) (mu — where the peak sits) and its standard deviation \(\sigma\) (sigma — how wide it is). Its formula gives the "height" (probability density) at any point \(x\):

\[ p(x) \;=\; \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right) \] (12.1)

Don't panic at the formula — read its shape. The important part is \(\exp(-(x-\mu)^2/\dots)\): the height falls off as \(x\) moves away from the mean \(\mu\), and it falls off squared, so points far from the mean become very unlikely very fast. The stuff in front is just a scaling factor that makes the whole curve's area equal 1 (as any probability distribution must). Now the key question of fitting: given a handful of data points, which \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) make those exact points most likely to have occurred? That's the maximum-likelihood question.

The likelihood of a whole dataset is the probability of seeing all of it: multiply the density at each data point together. We want the \(\mu, \sigma\) that maximise this product. Multiplying many small numbers is awkward, so we take the logarithm (which turns products into sums and doesn't change where the maximum is) and maximise the log-likelihood. Do the calculus, and the answer is wonderfully intuitive: the best \(\mu\) is just the average of the data, and the best \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the data (Lesson 11). The principle hands you back exactly the summary statistics you'd have guessed.

✍️ Worked example: MLE recovers "minimise squared error"

Here's the bridge to all of machine learning, in four steps. Suppose your data is a true value plus Gaussian noise: \(y = \text{model}(x) + \varepsilon\), where \(\varepsilon\) is bell-curve noise.

1
Likelihood of one point. The probability of observing \(y\) is the Gaussian height at the error \((y-\text{model}(x))\): proportional to \(\exp(-(y-\text{model})^2/2\sigma^2)\).
2
Log it. Taking the log turns the exponential into \(-(y-\text{model})^2/2\sigma^2\) plus a constant. The constant doesn't depend on the model, so ignore it.
3
Sum over all data and maximise. Maximising \(\sum -(y_i-\text{model}(x_i))^2\) is the same as minimising \(\sum (y_i-\text{model}(x_i))^2\) — the sum of squared errors.
4
The punchline. "Fit by maximum likelihood, assuming Gaussian noise" is "minimise mean squared error." Every time you train a regression on MSE (Track 5), you are secretly doing maximum likelihood with a bell curve. The loss functions of ML aren't arbitrary — they drop out of probability.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Reading the Gaussian's height as a probability. For a continuous variable it's a density, not a probability — the height can exceed 1. Probability is the area under a stretch of the curve.
  • Thinking MLE is a special trick. It's the universal recipe: write the probability of your data given the parameters, then maximise it. Cross-entropy loss (Track 3) is MLE for a classifier, exactly as MSE is MLE for a Gaussian.
  • Assuming everything is Gaussian. The bell curve is common because of the Central Limit Theorem, but plenty of real data (incomes, word counts, waiting times) is skewed or heavy-tailed. Choosing the right noise model is a modelling decision, not a given.
  • Maximising the product instead of the log. Multiplying thousands of tiny densities underflows to zero on a computer. Always work in log-likelihood — it's numerically stable and turns products into sums.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • The Gaussian is set by mean \(\mu\) and standard deviation \(\sigma\); it's everywhere because sums of small effects converge to it (CLT).
  • Maximum likelihood: pick the parameters that make the observed data most probable — the master recipe for fitting models.
  • Work in log-likelihood (products → sums, numerically stable); the maximum is in the same place.
  • MLE for a Gaussian mean/variance gives exactly the data's average and variance.
  • The bridge: MLE with Gaussian noise = minimising squared error; MLE for classes = cross-entropy. ML's losses come from probability.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What does maximum likelihood estimation choose?

  • The parameters with the smallest values.
    Size of the parameters isn't the criterion.
  • The parameters that make training fastest.
    Speed isn't what MLE optimises.
  • The parameters that make the observed data most probable.
    Correct. MLE maximises the probability of the data you actually saw.
  • Random parameters.
    MLE is a precise optimisation, not random.

Q2. The maximum-likelihood estimate of a Gaussian's mean is…

  • the average of the data.
    Correct. The calculus works out so the best \(\mu\) is exactly the sample mean.
  • the largest data point.
    The maximum value isn't the mean estimate.
  • always zero.
    Only if the data happened to average zero.
  • the median.
    Close in spirit, but for a Gaussian the MLE is specifically the mean.

Q3. Fitting a model by maximum likelihood under Gaussian noise is equivalent to…

  • maximising accuracy.
    Accuracy is a classification metric, not what this yields.
  • minimising the number of parameters.
    MLE doesn't minimise parameter count.
  • maximising entropy.
    A different principle; not what falls out here.
  • minimising mean squared error.
    Correct. The log-likelihood of Gaussian noise is the negative squared error, so maximising one minimises the other.

Q4. Why do we maximise the log-likelihood instead of the likelihood?

  • The log changes where the maximum is.
    It doesn't — log is increasing, so the maximiser is the same.
  • Products of many tiny probabilities underflow; the log turns them into a stable sum.
    Correct. Logs convert products to sums and avoid numerical underflow, without moving the optimum.
  • The likelihood has no maximum.
    It does; the log is just easier to work with.
  • Logs are required by probability.
    They're a convenience, not a requirement.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, drag the mean far from the data, then press Solve. Where does it land, and what data statistic is that?

Show solution

It snaps to the centre of the data cloud — the sample mean (average). The maximum-likelihood mean of a Gaussian is exactly the average of the observations, and the width snaps to their standard deviation. Watching the log-likelihood peak precisely there makes the abstract principle concrete: "most probable" and "average" coincide for the bell curve.

2. (transfer) Add one wild outlier far from the rest. What happens to the maximum-likelihood mean and width, and what does that reveal?

Show solution

Both get dragged toward the outlier — the mean shifts and the width balloons. This exposes a real weakness: the Gaussian's squared term punishes far-away points so heavily that a single outlier distorts the whole fit. It's the same reason squared-error regression is sensitive to outliers. When data has outliers, people switch to heavier-tailed noise models (or robust losses like the Huber loss), which is really just choosing a different likelihood.

3. (stretch) Cross-entropy loss (Track 3) is the standard loss for classifiers. In what sense is it also maximum likelihood?

Show solution

For a classifier that outputs class probabilities, the likelihood of the data is the product of the predicted probabilities of the correct labels. Taking the negative log of that product gives exactly the cross-entropy loss. So minimising cross-entropy is maximum likelihood for a categorical (multinomial) model, just as minimising squared error is MLE for a Gaussian. This is the unifying insight: nearly every loss function in machine learning is "negative log-likelihood under some assumed distribution." Once you see it, the losses stop looking arbitrary and start looking inevitable.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Maximum likelihood is the quiet backbone of the whole field. Training a language model to predict the next token is maximum likelihood (cross-entropy) over text; fitting a diffusion model, a VAE, or a logistic regression all come down to maximising a likelihood (or a bound on one, the ELBO — you'll meet it in Track 11). The Gaussian, meanwhile, is the default assumption behind least-squares fitting, Kalman filters, Gaussian processes (a whole track later), and the noise models in diffusion. Understanding "choose parameters that make the data probable" gives you a single lens that unifies almost every training objective you'll encounter — and that unification is exactly the kind of deep structure a researcher learns to see.

Recap — say it to a friend

"The Gaussian is the bell curve set by a mean and a width; maximum likelihood fits any model by choosing the parameters that make the data most probable — and for Gaussian noise that turns out to be exactly minimising squared error, which is why regression works."
