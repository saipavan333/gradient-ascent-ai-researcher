Transfer learning: stand on a pretrained network's shoulders

Almost nobody trains a vision model from scratch. The features a network learns on a huge dataset — edges, textures, shapes — are generic, so you reuse them and train only a small head on your own scarce labels. It's the most practical trick in applied deep learning.

Track 15 · Lesson 4 Concept + code ~22 min Builds on: Lessons 2–3

Training a strong vision model needs millions of labelled images and a lot of compute — resources most projects don't have. Transfer learning sidesteps this: take a network already trained on a large dataset, keep its learned feature extractor, and adapt it to your task with a small amount of data. Because the early features are generic across almost all natural images, you inherit years of others' training for free and only learn the last, task-specific step.

💡 Concept: reuse features, retrain the head

Recall the CNN's two parts (Lesson 2): a feature extractor (the conv/pool stack) and a head (the final classifier). The extractor learns general-purpose visual features; the head maps them to your specific classes. Transfer learning keeps the pretrained extractor and swaps in a fresh head for the new task. Two flavours:

Feature extraction (freeze): hold the extractor fixed, treat it as a fixed function that turns images into feature vectors, and train only the new head. Fast, needs little data, hard to overfit. Fine-tuning (unfreeze): also continue training the extractor's weights, usually at a small learning rate, so the features adapt to your domain. More powerful when you have moderate data, but needs care not to wash away the good pretrained weights. Rule of thumb: very little data → freeze; more data and a domain gap → fine-tune. 

# pretrain the conv features on ABUNDANT source data (once)
base = pretrain(TinyCNN(), Xsrc, ysrc)

# freeze: use conv->relu->pool as a fixed feature function
def extract_features(model, x):
    c = conv_forward(x, model.W); r = relu_forward(c)
    p, _ = maxpool_forward(r, model.size)
    return p.reshape(-1)

# train only a small linear head on the FEW target labels
F = np.array([extract_features(base, x) for x in Xfew])
W, b = train_linear(F, yfew)
The data-efficiency win, measured. With only 16 target labels, the frozen pretrained features + a linear head reach 1.00 test accuracy, while a linear model on raw pixels gets ~0.92. The features carry position-invariant orientation information that raw pixels don't, so the head's job becomes trivially easy. On real problems the gap is far larger — this is why "start from a pretrained model" is the default first move in computer vision.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Transfer learning reuses a pretrained feature extractor and trains a new head on your task — inheriting generic visual features.
  • Freeze (feature extraction) when data is very scarce; fine-tune (small LR on the extractor too) when you have more data or a domain shift.
  • Early features transfer best (edges/textures are universal); later, task-specific features transfer less and are the first to retrain.
  • Massive data efficiency: good features turn a hard learning problem into an easy one for the small head.
  • It's the default in practice — foundation models and pretrained backbones exist precisely to be transferred.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. In "feature extraction" transfer learning, what is trained on the new task?

  • The whole network, from scratch.
    That defeats the purpose — you'd gain nothing from the pretrained model.
  • Only the new head; the pretrained extractor stays frozen.
    Correct. You compute fixed features from the frozen extractor and fit only the small head, which is fast and data-efficient.
  • Only the first conv layer.
    The early layers are exactly what you keep; you retrain the end, not the start.
  • Nothing — transfer learning needs no training.
    The head must still be trained on your labels.

Q2. Why do early convolutional features transfer across tasks so well?

  • Because edges and textures are generic to almost all natural images.
    Correct. Low-level structure is shared across domains, so a good edge/texture detector is useful whether you're classifying cats or car parts.
  • Because they have the most parameters.
    Early layers usually have fewer parameters; transferability is about generality, not size.
  • Because they are task-specific.
    The opposite — their generality is exactly why they transfer; task-specific layers are the ones that don't.
  • Because they're randomly initialized.
    They're pretrained, not random; randomness would carry no transferable knowledge.

Q3. You have only ~50 labelled images for your task. Freeze or fine-tune?

  • Fine-tune the whole network aggressively.
    With 50 images you'd overfit and erode the good pretrained weights.
  • Train from scratch.
    Far too little data to learn features from zero.
  • Fine-tune at a high learning rate.
    High LR on tiny data is the fastest way to destroy the pretrained features.
  • Freeze the extractor and train just the head.
    Correct. Very little data favours freezing — few parameters to fit means low overfitting risk while still using the strong features.

Q4. In the demo, why did frozen CNN features beat raw pixels given the same 16 labels?

  • Because the head had more parameters.
    If anything the feature vector was smaller; it's the quality of the inputs that mattered.
  • Because raw pixels can't be classified at all.
    They can, just less easily — the raw-pixel model still reached ~0.92.
  • Because the features encode position-invariant orientation, making the classes linearly separable from few examples.
    Correct. Pretraining folded the hard part (becoming shift-invariant to orientation) into the features, so the head needed only a trivial boundary and generalized from 16 labels.
  • Because pixels are noisier by definition.
    Both saw the same noisy images; the difference is the representation, not extra noise.

✍️ Practice

1. Run transfer.py and sweep the number of target labels (4, 8, 16, 64). Plot transfer accuracy and raw-pixel accuracy vs label count. Where does the gap close?

Show solution

The transfer curve is high and nearly flat even at very few labels; the raw-pixel curve starts lower and climbs as labels increase, closing much of the gap by the time you have plenty. That's the signature of transfer learning: its advantage is data efficiency, biggest in the low-label regime and shrinking as data grows (because with enough data you can eventually learn good features yourself). The practical reading: the less labelled data you have, the more transfer buys you.

2. The demo freezes the extractor. Sketch how you'd fine-tune instead, and one risk to guard against.

Show solution

To fine-tune, you'd let gradients flow into the conv filters too and keep updating them — but at a small learning rate, often after first training the head alone so the initial gradients aren't wild. The risk is catastrophic forgetting: a large learning rate or too many steps on little data can overwrite the pretrained features with noise, ending up worse than freezing. Mitigations: low LR, few epochs, freeze the earliest layers (most general), and early-stop on a validation set. (We'll meet forgetting head-on in the continual-learning track.)

3. Conceptual: transfer learning here reuses vision features. The same idea powers modern NLP (pretrain a language model, fine-tune on a task). What's the shared principle?

Show solution

Learn general representations once on abundant (often unlabelled) data, then adapt them cheaply to many downstream tasks. Whether it's a CNN pretrained on ImageNet or a transformer pretrained on web text (Track 5, 11), the pattern is identical: pretraining amortizes the expensive discovery of good features, and fine-tuning specializes them. This "pretrain then adapt" recipe is the backbone of the foundation model era — the reason a single base model can seed thousands of applications.
