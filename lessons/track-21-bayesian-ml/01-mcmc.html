MCMC: sampling a distribution you can't solve

Conjugacy gave us a tidy formula, but almost no real posterior has one. Markov Chain Monte Carlo draws samples from any distribution you can evaluate — even up to an unknown constant — by taking a clever random walk that spends time in each region in proportion to its probability.

Bayes' rule (Lesson 0) told us posterior \(\propto\) prior \(\times\) likelihood — but that \(\propto\) hides a problem. To turn "proportional to" into a real distribution you must divide by \(p(D)\), an integral over all possible parameter values, which for any interesting model is impossible to compute. So we can evaluate the posterior's shape (the un-normalised height at any point) but not the distribution itself. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) is the brilliant escape: it produces samples from the posterior using only that un-normalised shape, and from samples you can compute anything you want — means, intervals, predictions. The trick is to build a random walk that lingers in high-probability regions and rushes through low-probability ones, so that where the walker spends its time traces out the distribution. The simplest version — Metropolis–Hastings — is just "propose a step, accept it if things look better, sometimes accept even if worse." That one rule samples distributions no formula can touch.

💡 Intuition: a hiker exploring hills in the fog

Imagine a hiker wandering a landscape in thick fog, where height means probability. They can't see the whole map (no formula), but they can feel whether a step goes up or down. Their rule: take a random step; if it goes uphill, always take it; if it goes downhill, take it anyway sometimes, with a chance that depends on how much lower it is. Follow this rule for a long time and something magical happens — the fraction of time the hiker spends in any area becomes proportional to its height. Record where they've been, and that pile of footprints is a sample from the distribution. The occasional downhill steps are essential: they stop the hiker from getting stuck on one peak, letting them cross valleys to explore other high regions.

Precisely, Metropolis–Hastings at each step proposes a nearby point \(x'\) and accepts it with probability

\[ A = \min\!\left(1,\ \frac{p(x')}{p(x)}\right), \qquad \text{(only the } \textit{ratio} \text{ matters, so the unknown constant cancels)} \] (1.1)

Read it: compare the target's height at the proposed point \(p(x')\) to its height where you are \(p(x)\). If the ratio is \(\ge 1\) (uphill), accept for sure. If it's less than 1 (downhill), accept with probability equal to that ratio — a small downhill step is often accepted, a big drop rarely. The masterstroke is the ratio: since \(p(x')/p(x)\) divides out the pesky normalising constant \(p(D)\), you never need it. You only ever evaluate the un-normalised posterior, which is exactly the thing you can compute.

✍️ Worked example: one Metropolis step

The walker is at \(x=1.0\); the un-normalised target has \(p(1.0)=2.0\). It proposes \(x'=1.6\) with \(p(1.6)=3.0\), then later \(x''=0.2\) with \(p(0.2)=0.5\).

1
Uphill proposal (1.6). Ratio \(=p(1.6)/p(1.0)=3.0/2.0=1.5\). Since \(1.5\ge1\), accept with probability \(\min(1,1.5)=1\). Always move. The walker goes to 1.6.
2
Downhill proposal (0.2). From 1.6, ratio \(=p(0.2)/p(1.6)=0.5/3.0=0.17\). Accept with probability 0.17 — draw a random number in [0,1]; if it's below 0.17, move; otherwise stay.
3
Why accept downhill at all? If the walker only ever went uphill it would climb one peak and freeze there, never sampling the rest of the distribution. Occasional downhill moves let it escape and explore — that's what makes the footprints match the whole shape, not just one mode.
4
The constant never appears. Every decision used only ratios of \(p\), so the unknown normaliser cancels. That's why MCMC works on posteriors we can only evaluate up to a constant — which is essentially all of them.

🛠️ From scratch: Metropolis–Hastings

def metropolis(log_p, x0=0.0, steps=200000, step=1.0):
    x = x0; lp = log_p(x); samples = []
    for t in range(steps):
        xp  = x + step * rng.standard_normal()      # propose a nearby point
        lpp = log_p(xp)                             # target height there (log, unnormalised)
        if np.log(rng.random()) < lpp - lp:         # accept with prob min(1, p'/p)
            x, lp = xp, lpp                         # move
        samples.append(x)                           # (staying still also records a sample)
    return np.array(samples)

# work in LOG space: compare log p' - log p, so tiny probabilities don't underflow

Two subtleties make it robust. We work with log-probabilities (comparing \(\log p' - \log p\)) so that multiplying many small numbers doesn't underflow to zero. And when a proposal is rejected, we still record the current point as a sample — staying put is how the walker accumulates weight on high-probability regions. Run it on the two-humped target and the samples reproduce it faithfully, visiting both modes, with the estimated \(E[x^2]\) matching the exact grid value to two decimals.

Diagnostics you must run. MCMC needs care. Burn-in: throw away the first chunk of samples, taken before the walker found the high-probability region. Step size: too small and it crawls (high acceptance, slow exploration); too big and it rarely moves (low acceptance) — aim for roughly 25–50% acceptance. Convergence: run several chains from different starts and check they agree. Modern samplers (Hamiltonian Monte Carlo, NUTS — the engine inside Stan and PyMC) use gradients to propose much smarter steps, but they're all descendants of this simple accept/reject idea.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Forgetting burn-in. The first samples come from wherever you happened to start, not the target. Discard them, or your estimates are biased toward the starting point.
  • Never accepting downhill moves. A pure hill-climb gets trapped on one mode. The probabilistic acceptance of worse points is what lets MCMC explore the whole distribution.
  • Treating samples as independent. Consecutive MCMC samples are correlated (each is a small step from the last), so 10,000 samples carry less information than 10,000 independent draws. Report the effective sample size.
  • Trusting one short chain. A chain can look converged while stuck in one mode. Always run multiple chains and check they mix and agree (the standard \(\hat R\) diagnostic).

🔑 Key results to remember

  • MCMC draws samples from a distribution using only its un-normalised height — no need for the intractable normaliser.
  • Metropolis–Hastings: propose a step, accept with probability \(\min(1, p(x')/p(x))\); the ratio cancels the constant.
  • Downhill moves are accepted sometimes — that's what lets the walker escape one mode and explore all of them.
  • Where the walker spends time ∝ probability, so the samples reproduce the distribution; compute means/intervals from them.
  • Diagnose it: burn-in, ~25–50% acceptance (step size), and multiple chains for convergence.

Q1. What problem does MCMC solve?

  • It speeds up matrix multiplication.
    Unrelated; MCMC is about sampling distributions.
  • It removes the need for data.
    It still uses data via the likelihood; it samples the posterior.
  • It draws samples from a posterior we can only evaluate up to an unknown constant.
    Correct. The intractable normaliser is exactly what MCMC sidesteps.
  • It makes priors unnecessary.
    The prior is still part of the target being sampled.

Q2. Why does the accept probability use the ratio \(p(x')/p(x)\)?

  • The ratio cancels the unknown normalising constant, so we never need it.
    Correct. Only relative heights matter, which is what we can compute.
  • Ratios are faster to compute than sums.
    Speed isn't the reason; it's about cancelling the constant.
  • It guarantees every step is accepted.
    Downhill steps are only sometimes accepted.
  • To make the walk deterministic.
    The walk is deliberately random.

Q3. Why does Metropolis–Hastings accept some downhill moves?

  • To speed up convergence guarantees.
    It's about coverage, not a speed guarantee.
  • Because uphill moves are forbidden.
    Uphill moves are always accepted; downhill sometimes.
  • To reduce memory use.
    Memory isn't the reason.
  • So the walker can escape one mode and explore the whole distribution.
    Correct. Pure hill-climbing gets stuck; occasional downhill steps enable full exploration.

Q4. What is "burn-in"?

  • The step size of the proposals.
    That's a separate tuning parameter.
  • The early samples, taken before the chain reaches the high-probability region, which you discard.
    Correct. Keeping them biases estimates toward the arbitrary starting point.
  • The number of chains.
    That's about convergence checking, not burn-in.
  • The acceptance rate.
    Acceptance rate is a diagnostic, not burn-in.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, run the sampler and watch how often the walker crosses the valley between the two humps. What would happen to the histogram if it never crossed?

Show solution

The walker crosses occasionally — each crossing requires a run of accepted steps through the low-probability valley. If it never crossed (e.g., if the humps were far apart or the step size tiny), the histogram would fill in only one hump and completely miss the other, giving a badly wrong picture of the distribution. This is the notorious failure mode of MCMC on multimodal posteriors: a chain can look perfectly converged while having explored only part of the distribution. It's why you run multiple chains from different starts and check they agree — and why samplers that mix between modes are an active research area.

2. (transfer) Your MCMC acceptance rate is 2% — almost every proposal is rejected. What's likely wrong, and how do you fix it?

Show solution

The proposal step size is too large: nearly every proposed jump lands in a low-probability region and gets rejected, so the walker sits still and barely explores. Fix it by shrinking the step size until acceptance rises into the healthy 25–50% range. (The opposite failure — 95% acceptance — means steps are too small, so it accepts everything but crawls, exploring slowly.) Tuning the step size to hit that sweet spot is the single most important knob in Metropolis–Hastings, which is exactly why gradient-based samplers like HMC/NUTS, which propose much better moves automatically, have largely replaced hand-tuned random-walk Metropolis in practice.

3. (stretch) MCMC is exact in the limit of infinite samples but can be slow. The next lesson (variational inference) is fast but approximate. When would you choose each?

Show solution

Choose MCMC when you need accuracy and can afford the compute — final scientific analyses, smaller models, or when you must faithfully capture a complicated or multimodal posterior; given enough samples it converges to the true posterior. Choose variational inference when you need speed and scale — huge datasets, big models (Bayesian neural nets), or real-time settings — accepting that you get an approximate posterior (often too narrow, since VI tends to underestimate uncertainty). In practice: prototype and check with VI, validate or finalise with MCMC; or use VI when MCMC simply won't finish. The trade-off — exact-but-slow vs approximate-but-fast — is the central tension in scalable Bayesian inference, and picking the right tool for the job is the practical skill.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

MCMC powers probabilistic programming languages — Stan, PyMC, NumPyro — that let scientists specify a model and get posterior samples automatically, and it's used across astrophysics (fitting cosmological parameters), epidemiology (disease-spread models), genetics, and ecology. Modern samplers like the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) use the gradient of the log-posterior to propose long, efficient moves, making Bayesian inference practical for models with thousands of parameters. The frontier pushes toward samplers that handle multimodal and high-dimensional posteriors, and hybrids with variational methods and normalising flows. What you built here — the humble accept/reject walk — is the conceptual seed of all of it.

"MCMC samples a distribution you can't solve by taking a random walk that lingers where probability is high. The footprints reproduce the distribution. Just remember burn-in, tune the step size to ~25–50% acceptance, and run several chains."
