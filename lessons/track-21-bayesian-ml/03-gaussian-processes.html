Gaussian processes: distributions over functions

Bayesian inference over parameters is powerful, but a Gaussian process goes further — it puts a probability distribution over entire functions. The result is a regression that fits smoothly through your data and, crucially, reports growing uncertainty exactly where it has none.

Track 15 · Lesson 3 Concept + code + interactive ~20 min Builds on: Lesson 0

So far we put distributions over a handful of parameters — a coin's bias, a weight. A Gaussian process (GP) makes a bolder move: it puts a distribution over whole functions. Instead of assuming a fixed form (a line, a polynomial) and inferring its coefficients, a GP considers all smooth functions at once and asks which ones are consistent with your data. Feed it a few observed points and it returns not a single curve but a distribution of curves — summarized by a mean prediction and, at every input, an honest measure of uncertainty. Near your data the plausible curves all agree, so the uncertainty is tiny; far from your data they fan out wildly, so the uncertainty is large. This "knows what it doesn't know" behaviour is the GP's superpower, and it makes GPs the tool of choice whenever you must act on predictions and account for their reliability — from tuning expensive experiments to modelling noisy sensors. The whole model rests on one idea: define how similar two inputs are (a kernel), and let similar inputs have similar outputs.

💡 Intuition: connect the dots, but honestly

Give a person a few scattered data points and ask them to draw the curve. Near the points, everyone draws roughly the same line — the data pins it down. In the big gaps between points, people's guesses diverge — there's genuine freedom in how to connect them. A GP captures exactly this: it draws the "average" curve through the data and shades a band showing how much the plausible curves spread at each location. The band squeezes to nearly nothing where you have data (all curves must pass through the points) and balloons in the gaps and beyond the edges (many curves fit there equally well). It's connect-the-dots that's honest about the parts it's guessing.

The engine is a kernel \(k(x,x')\), a similarity function saying how alike the outputs at two inputs should be. The common choice is the RBF kernel — similarity fades smoothly with distance:

\[ k(x,x') = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-x')^2}{2\ell^2}\right), \qquad \text{posterior: } \; \mu_* = K_* K^{-1} y, \quad \sigma_*^2 = k_{**} - K_* K^{-1} K_*^\top \] (3.1)

The kernel \(k(x,x')\) is near 1 when \(x\) and \(x'\) are close and decays toward 0 as they separate (the length-scale \(\ell\), ell, sets how fast). Given training inputs, \(K\) is the matrix of kernel values between all training points, and \(K_*\) between test and training points. Then the posterior mean \(\mu_*\) at a new input is a kernel-weighted blend of the observed \(y\)'s (nearby data counts most), and the posterior variance \(\sigma_*^2\) shrinks wherever test points are close to data and grows where they're far — that's the honest uncertainty, and it comes out of the same clean Gaussian algebra.

✍️ Worked example: uncertainty at a data point vs a gap

From gaussian_process.py: fit a GP to six points from \(\sin(x)\) and compare its variance in two places.

1
Fit. Compute \(K\) (kernel between the six training points), invert it, and use equation (3.1) for the mean and variance across a grid of test inputs.
2
At a data point (x=0). The posterior variance is ≈ 0.0004 — essentially zero. The curve is pinned to the observation, so there's almost nothing to be uncertain about.
3
Far from data (x=6, past the last point). The posterior variance is ≈ 0.998 — huge. With no nearby data, the GP falls back toward its prior spread and admits it's essentially guessing.
4
The fit itself. At x=0 the predicted mean matches \(\sin(0)=0\) to within 0.03. So the GP interpolates the data accurately and reports calibrated error bars — small where it knows, large where it doesn't. That pairing is what makes GPs special.

🛠️ From scratch: GP regression

def rbf(A, B, l=1.0):
    d = A[:, None] - B[None, :]
    return np.exp(-(d**2) / (2 * l**2))          # similarity fades with distance

K    = rbf(Xtr, Xtr) + noise * np.eye(len(Xtr))  # train-train kernel (+ tiny noise)
Kinv = np.linalg.inv(K)
Ks   = rbf(Xte, Xtr)                             # test-train kernel
mean = Ks @ Kinv @ ytr                           # posterior mean  (eq 3.1)
var  = 1.0 - np.einsum('ij,jk,ik->i', Ks, Kinv, Ks)  # posterior variance -> error bars

That's the whole model — no training loop, no gradient descent. The "learning" is exact linear algebra: invert one kernel matrix and read off the mean and variance. (In practice you'd also tune the kernel's length-scale and noise by maximizing the marginal likelihood, but the core prediction is this closed form.)

The catch: it scales badly. Inverting \(K\) costs \(O(N^3)\) time and \(O(N^2)\) memory for \(N\) data points — fine for hundreds or a few thousand points, but hopeless for millions. That single limitation is why GPs, despite their elegance, aren't used for large-scale problems directly, and why a whole research area (sparse/inducing-point GPs, GPU kernel methods) exists to approximate them. GPs shine in the small-data, high-stakes regime — exactly where knowing your uncertainty matters most.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Ignoring the length-scale. The kernel's length-scale \(\ell\) controls smoothness: too small and the GP wiggles through every point and is uncertain everywhere; too large and it's too stiff to fit. It's the single most important knob.
  • Forgetting GPs scale as \(O(N^3)\). Throwing a million points at a plain GP will run out of memory and time. Know the regime GPs belong in.
  • Reading the mean without the variance. The mean alone is just another regressor; the whole point of a GP is the variance. Always report and use the band.
  • Assuming the uncertainty is always right. The error bars are only as good as the kernel and noise assumptions. A badly chosen kernel gives confident-but-wrong bands — calibration (next lesson) is how you check.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • A GP is a distribution over functions — it returns a mean curve and an uncertainty band, not a single line.
  • A kernel \(k(x,x')\) defines input similarity; similar inputs get similar outputs (RBF = similarity fades with distance).
  • Uncertainty is small near data, large in gaps — the GP "knows what it doesn't know."
  • Prediction is closed-form (eq 3.1): invert the kernel matrix, read off mean and variance — no training loop.
  • Costs \(O(N^3)\) — great for small/medium data, impractical for millions of points.

Q1. What does a Gaussian process put a distribution over?

  • A single parameter.
    That's ordinary Bayesian inference; a GP goes further.
  • The learning rate.
    Not a distribution target; that's a hyperparameter.
  • Entire functions.
    Correct. A GP reasons over all smooth functions consistent with the data.
  • The training labels.
    Labels are observed data, not what the GP is distributed over.

Q2. Where is a GP's predictive uncertainty largest?

  • Far from the training data (gaps and beyond the edges).
    Correct. With no nearby data, many functions fit, so the band widens.
  • Exactly at the training points.
    There the curve is pinned; uncertainty is smallest.
  • It's constant everywhere.
    The defining feature is that it varies with distance to data.
  • Only at x=0.
    Location of data, not the origin, determines uncertainty.

Q3. What role does the kernel play?

  • It sets the learning rate.
    GPs have no learning-rate loop; prediction is closed-form.
  • It counts the data points.
    Not its role; it measures similarity.
  • It labels the outputs.
    Labels are given; the kernel relates inputs.
  • It defines how similar two inputs are, so similar inputs get similar outputs.
    Correct. The kernel encodes the smoothness assumption at the heart of the GP.

Q4. Why don't people use plain GPs on datasets with millions of points?

  • They can't represent uncertainty.
    Uncertainty is their strength.
  • Inverting the kernel matrix costs \(O(N^3)\) — infeasible at that scale.
    Correct. The cubic cost limits plain GPs to small/medium data.
  • They require labels for every function.
    They use ordinary labelled data points.
  • They only work in one dimension.
    GPs work in many dimensions; scale is the real limit.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, place several points close together, then leave a wide gap before adding one more. Describe the uncertainty band across the gap, and why it does that.

Show solution

The band is thin at each cluster of points and bulges outward across the gap, peaking in the middle where you're farthest from any data. This is the GP correctly reporting that it has strong evidence near the points but is essentially interpolating (guessing) in the gap — and the further from data, the less sure. It's the visual signature of "honest uncertainty": the model's confidence is tied directly to how much relevant data supports each location, which is exactly the property you want when deciding whether to trust a prediction.

2. (transfer) Slide the length-scale from small to large with a fixed set of points. What changes, and how would you choose the right value?

Show solution

A small length-scale makes similarity decay fast, so the mean curve wiggles sharply to hit each point and the uncertainty band snaps back to wide almost immediately between points (the GP assumes the function can change quickly). A large length-scale makes the curve smooth and stiff, sharing information over long distances, with a band that stays narrow across bigger gaps (it assumes the function changes slowly). You choose it by maximizing the marginal likelihood — the GP's built-in, principled way of balancing fit against smoothness (a Bayesian form of Occam's razor) — or by cross-validation. The length-scale encodes your assumption about how quickly the underlying function varies, and getting it right is the main modelling decision in a GP.

3. (stretch) "Bayesian optimization" uses a GP to tune expensive things (like a model's hyperparameters or a chemistry experiment) in as few trials as possible. Explain how the GP's uncertainty makes this work.

Show solution

Each expensive trial gives one data point (a setting and its measured result). Fit a GP to the trials so far: its mean predicts which untried settings look good, and its variance flags which settings are unexplored. Bayesian optimization then picks the next setting by balancing exploitation (try where the mean is high) against exploration (try where the variance is high — you might discover something), using an "acquisition function" that combines both. Because the GP tells you not just the best guess but where you're most uncertain, you avoid wasting precious trials on settings you can already predict, and you deliberately probe the unknown regions that might hold the optimum. This is why GPs dominate expensive-to-evaluate optimization — from AutoML to drug and materials discovery — and it's a direct payoff of having calibrated uncertainty rather than just a point prediction.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Gaussian processes are the backbone of Bayesian optimization — the standard way to tune hyperparameters, design experiments, and search chemical and materials spaces where each trial is costly. They model time series and spatial data (geostatistics, sensor networks), and provide uncertainty in robotics and control. The frontier tackles their scaling limit: sparse GPs with inducing points, GPU-accelerated kernel solvers (GPyTorch), and deep kernel learning that puts a neural network inside the kernel to combine deep features with GP uncertainty. And GPs connect deeply to deep learning — an infinitely wide neural network is exactly a Gaussian process, a result (the "neural tangent kernel") that helps explain why big networks train and generalize as they do.

Recap — say it to a friend

"A Gaussian process puts a distribution over functions: give it data and it returns a mean curve plus an uncertainty band that pinches tight at the data and balloons in the gaps — it knows what it doesn't know. A kernel defines input similarity, and prediction is exact linear algebra (invert the kernel matrix). It's perfect for small, high-stakes problems but scales as N-cubed."
