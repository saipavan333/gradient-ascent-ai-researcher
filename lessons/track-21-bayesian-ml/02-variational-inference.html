Variational inference: turn inference into optimization

MCMC samples the posterior but can be slow. Variational inference takes the opposite bet: pick a simple distribution and adjust it until it hugs the posterior as closely as possible. Inference becomes optimization — fast, scalable, and the workhorse of Bayesian deep learning.

Track 15 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~20 min Builds on: Lesson 1

MCMC (Lesson 1) is beautiful and, given enough time, exact — but "enough time" can mean hours or days for a big model, and it doesn't scale to the millions of parameters in a neural network. Variational inference (VI) makes a pragmatic trade. Instead of sampling the true posterior, it picks a family of simple distributions — most often a Gaussian — and searches within that family for the member that best matches the posterior. That "search for the best match" is an optimization problem, so we can attack it with the same gradient descent that trains everything else (Track 2). The result is an approximate posterior you can compute in a fraction of the time. You give up some accuracy — a single Gaussian can't capture every twist of a complicated posterior — but you gain the speed and scalability that make Bayesian methods usable on large models. VI is how uncertainty gets bolted onto deep learning.

💡 Intuition: the best-fitting simple shape

Suppose the true posterior is a complicated, awkward blob you can't describe with a formula. VI says: "I'll approximate it with a shape I can describe — a Gaussian bell — and slide and stretch that bell until it overlaps the blob as much as possible." You're not trying to reproduce the blob exactly; you're finding the single bell curve that's least wrong. Once you have it, everything is easy: a Gaussian has a known mean, known spread, and instant samples. The art is in measuring "how well does my bell match the blob?" and then adjusting the bell's position and width to maximize that match.

The measure of match is the ELBO (Evidence Lower BOund). Maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to minimizing the KL divergence (the information-theory "distance" between distributions, from Track 3) between your approximation \(q\) and the true posterior \(p\):

\[ \text{maximize } \;\text{ELBO}(q) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log p(x,\theta)]}_{\text{fit the data \& prior}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{H}[q]}_{\text{stay spread out}} \;\;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\;\; \text{minimize } \; \text{KL}\big(q \,\Vert\, p(\theta\mid x)\big) \] (2.1)

Unpacking it: the first term \(\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x,\theta)]\) pulls \(q\) toward regions where the model explains the data and prior well (high posterior); the second term \(\mathbb{H}[q]\) is the entropy of \(q\), which rewards it for staying spread out rather than collapsing to a spike. Their balance lands \(q\) on the best-fitting bell. And the profound fact is the "\(\Longleftrightarrow\)": maximizing this ELBO is exactly the same as minimizing the KL divergence from \(q\) to the true posterior — so by optimizing something we can compute (the ELBO), we're provably making \(q\) as close to the posterior as the family allows.

✍️ Worked example: fitting a Gaussian by the ELBO

From variational_inference.py: approximate a coin's log-odds posterior with a Gaussian \(q=\text{Normal}(m, s)\).

1
Parameterize \(q\). Two knobs: the mean \(m\) and width \(s\). Start at \(\text{Normal}(0,1)\) — a generic guess.
2
Estimate the ELBO's gradient. Use the reparameterization trick: write a sample as \(\theta = m + s\cdot\varepsilon\) with \(\varepsilon\sim\text{Normal}(0,1)\), so the randomness is separated from \(m,s\) and gradients flow through. Average over a few \(\varepsilon\) samples to estimate \(\partial\text{ELBO}/\partial m\) and \(\partial\text{ELBO}/\partial s\).
3
Gradient-ascend. Nudge \(m\) and \(s\) uphill on the ELBO, repeatedly — ordinary gradient ascent, no sampling of the posterior itself.
4
Result. \(q\) converges to \(\text{Normal}(1.20, 0.68)\) — matching the true posterior's mean 1.19 and sd 0.69 (computed by brute-force grid) almost exactly. A fast optimization reproduced the posterior, no MCMC chain required.

🛠️ From scratch: maximize the ELBO

m, logs = 0.0, 0.0                        # q = Normal(m, exp(logs)); learn m and width
for it in range(4000):
    s = np.exp(logs)
    eps = rng.standard_normal(S)          # reparameterization: theta = m + s*eps
    theta = m + s * eps
    g = grad_log_joint(theta)             # pathwise gradient of the model log-density
    m    += lr * g.mean()                 # ascend the ELBO in the mean...
    logs += lr * ((g*eps).mean() + 1/s) * s   # ...and the width (+1/s is the entropy term)
# q now approximates the posterior:  Normal(m, exp(logs))

The +1/s is the entropy term doing its job — without it, \(q\) would collapse to a zero-width spike on the posterior's peak (a point estimate), throwing away all uncertainty. With it, \(q\) settles at the right width, capturing how uncertain the posterior really is. That single term is the difference between Bayesian inference and ordinary point estimation.

The reparameterization trick powers modern AI. Writing a random sample as \(m + s\cdot\varepsilon\) — pushing the randomness into a fixed \(\varepsilon\) so gradients can flow through \(m\) and \(s\) — is the exact trick behind the Variational Autoencoder (Track 8). VAEs are variational inference with neural networks for \(q\) and \(p\). So the machinery you're building here is the same machinery that generates faces, compresses data, and underlies diffusion models' probabilistic foundations. VI is not a niche technique; it's load-bearing.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Forgetting VI underestimates uncertainty. Minimizing KL\((q\Vert p)\) makes \(q\) "mode-seeking" — it tends to fit one mode snugly and be too narrow, reporting overconfident posteriors. Know this bias.
  • Dropping the entropy term. Without it, \(q\) collapses to a spike and you're back to a point estimate with no uncertainty — the whole Bayesian benefit gone.
  • Expecting a simple \(q\) to fit a complex posterior. A single Gaussian can't capture multiple modes or heavy skew. Richer families (mixtures, normalizing flows) exist for when the mean-field Gaussian is too crude.
  • Confusing the two KL directions. VI minimizes KL\((q\Vert p)\) (mode-seeking, narrow); the other direction KL\((p\Vert q)\) is mean-seeking (would over-spread). The asymmetry is why VI is characteristically overconfident.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • VI approximates the posterior with a simple family (e.g., a Gaussian) by optimization, not sampling — fast and scalable.
  • Maximize the ELBO ⇔ minimize KL\((q\Vert p)\): fit the data/prior while staying appropriately spread (entropy term).
  • Reparameterization trick (\(\theta=m+s\varepsilon\)) lets gradients flow so you can gradient-ascend the ELBO.
  • It's fast but approximate — and tends to underestimate uncertainty (mode-seeking).
  • VI + neural nets = the VAE; the same machinery scales Bayesian ideas to deep models.

Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. How does variational inference differ from MCMC?

  • VI needs no data.
    Both use the data through the likelihood.
  • VI draws exact samples from the posterior.
    That's MCMC; VI fits an approximation.
  • VI turns inference into optimization — fit a simple distribution to the posterior.
    Correct. It searches a family for the best match, using gradient ascent.
  • VI is always more accurate.
    It's faster but approximate, usually less accurate than converged MCMC.

Q2. Maximizing the ELBO is equivalent to...

  • minimizing the KL divergence between \(q\) and the true posterior.
    Correct. That's why optimizing the computable ELBO makes \(q\) as close to \(p\) as the family allows.
  • maximizing the number of samples.
    VI doesn't sample the posterior; there are no such samples to maximize.
  • minimizing the prior.
    The prior is part of the model, not the objective being minimized.
  • maximizing the learning rate.
    The learning rate is a hyperparameter, unrelated to the ELBO.

Q3. What does the entropy term in the ELBO prevent?

  • The mean from moving.
    The mean is free to move; entropy is about width.
  • Overfitting the learning rate.
    Not what entropy controls.
  • Slow convergence.
    Entropy isn't a speed device.
  • \(q\) collapsing to a zero-width spike (a point estimate).
    Correct. Entropy keeps \(q\) spread out so it captures uncertainty instead of becoming a single point.

Q4. A known limitation of standard VI is that it...

  • always overestimates uncertainty.
    It's the opposite — VI tends to be too confident.
  • tends to underestimate uncertainty (too narrow / mode-seeking).
    Correct. Minimizing KL\((q\Vert p)\) makes \(q\) hug one mode and under-report spread.
  • cannot use gradients.
    It relies on gradient ascent of the ELBO.
  • requires conjugacy.
    VI works precisely where conjugacy fails.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, set the width slider very small and maximize the mean's fit. Does a razor-thin \(q\) give a good ELBO? What does that tell you about the width's role?

Show solution

A razor-thin \(q\) placed on the peak fits the first ELBO term (it sits where the posterior is high) but scores badly on the entropy term and misses most of the posterior's mass, so the total ELBO is not maximal. The best ELBO comes from a \(q\) whose width matches the posterior's — wide enough to cover the mass, not so wide it spills into low-probability regions. This is the entropy term earning its keep: it stops the approximation from collapsing to a point and forces it to represent genuine uncertainty. The optimum is a real distribution, not a spike.

2. (transfer) Your posterior is clearly bimodal (two separate humps), but you fit it with a single Gaussian via VI. What will \(q\) do, and why?

Show solution

Because VI minimizes KL\((q\Vert p)\), which heavily penalizes \(q\) putting mass where \(p\) has none, a single Gaussian will typically snap onto one of the two modes and ignore the other entirely (mode-seeking), rather than stretching to cover both (which would place mass in the empty valley and incur a big KL penalty). So you'd get a confident but incomplete answer — missing half the story. The fix is a richer variational family: a mixture of Gaussians, or a normalizing flow, that can represent multiple modes. This is the central weakness to remember: VI's answer is only as expressive as the family you chose for \(q\).

3. (stretch) Explain, in one or two sentences each, how a Variational Autoencoder (Track 8) is "just" variational inference — what plays the role of \(q\), of \(p\), and of the ELBO.

Show solution

\(q\): the VAE's encoder network outputs the mean and width of a Gaussian \(q(z\mid x)\) — the approximate posterior over the latent code \(z\) for each input \(x\) (instead of one fixed \(q\), a neural net produces a tailored \(q\) per data point, called "amortized" inference). \(p\): the decoder network defines \(p(x\mid z)\), how latents generate data, together with a simple prior \(p(z)\). The ELBO: the VAE's training loss is exactly the negative ELBO — a reconstruction term (the \(\mathbb{E}_q[\log p]\) fit term) plus a KL term pulling \(q(z\mid x)\) toward the prior (the entropy/regularization term) — optimized with the reparameterization trick. So training a VAE is running variational inference at scale, once per data point, with neural networks standing in for the distributions. Everything in this lesson is the VAE's backbone.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Variational inference is the scalable arm of Bayesian ML. It trains variational autoencoders, powers probabilistic programming's fast "ADVI" mode (automatic differentiation VI in Stan and Pyro), and provides uncertainty for Bayesian neural networks used in safety-critical settings. The frontier is making \(q\) more expressive — normalizing flows that warp a simple distribution into a complex one, and mixture/structured approximations — to fix VI's overconfidence, plus tighter bounds than the ELBO. The deep idea you take away is a recurring theme in all of ML: when an exact computation is intractable, replace it with an optimization over a tractable family. That move — inference as optimization — shows up far beyond Bayesian statistics.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Variational inference approximates a hard posterior with a simple distribution and fits it by gradient ascent — maximizing the ELBO, which is the same as minimizing the KL distance to the true posterior. The entropy term keeps it from collapsing to a point, so it captures uncertainty. It's fast but a bit overconfident, and with neural nets it becomes the VAE."
