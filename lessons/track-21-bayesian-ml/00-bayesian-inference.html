Bayesian inference: updating beliefs with evidence

Most models give you a single answer. A Bayesian model gives you a whole distribution of plausible answers — and updates it as evidence arrives. It's a principled machine for reasoning under uncertainty, and it starts with one line: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood.

Every model so far handed you a point estimate — one number, one line, one prediction — with no sense of how sure it was. But real decisions need to know the difference between "0.7, and I'm certain" and "0.7, but it could easily be 0.4 or 0.9." Bayesian inference is the framework that carries that uncertainty all the way through. Instead of committing to a single best value for an unknown, it maintains a probability distribution over all possible values — your belief — and updates that belief every time new data arrives, using one rule (Bayes' theorem). You start with a prior (what you believed before seeing data), you weigh it against the likelihood (how well each possibility explains the data), and you get a posterior (your updated belief). The beauty is that the posterior isn't just an answer — it's an answer with honest error bars. This lesson builds the whole idea on the simplest possible example: figuring out whether a coin is fair.

💡 Intuition: how a detective updates a theory

A good detective doesn't lock onto one suspect immediately. They start with prior suspicions (the butler is usually guilty in these stories), then each clue shifts those suspicions — strengthening some theories, ruling out others — until the evidence points clearly one way. Crucially, they hold degrees of belief, not a single fixed verdict, and they revise smoothly as facts come in. Bayesian inference is exactly this, made mathematical. Your prior is the initial suspicion; each data point is a clue; the posterior is your revised suspicion after weighing the clue. Enough strong clues and the posterior sharpens to near-certainty; with only a little evidence, it stays appropriately vague.

The engine is Bayes' theorem. For an unknown \(\theta\) (theta — say, a coin's bias) and observed data \(D\):

\[ \underbrace{p(\theta \mid D)}_{\text{posterior}} \;=\; \frac{\overbrace{p(D \mid \theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{p(D)} \;\;\propto\;\; p(D \mid \theta)\,p(\theta) \] (0.1)

Read it in plain words: your belief about \(\theta\) after seeing the data (the posterior, \(p(\theta\mid D)\)) is proportional to how well \(\theta\) explains the data (the likelihood, \(p(D\mid\theta)\)) times how plausible \(\theta\) was to begin with (the prior, \(p(\theta)\)). The denominator \(p(D)\) is just a normalising constant that makes it a proper probability distribution — which is why we usually write the handy \(\propto\) ("proportional to") version and normalise at the end. That single line is the entire foundation of Bayesian machine learning.

For a coin, there's a lovely shortcut. If your prior belief about the bias \(p\) is a Beta distribution \(\text{Beta}(a,b)\) (a flexible curve on the interval 0–1, where \(a\) acts like "heads seen so far" and \(b\) like "tails"), then after observing some flips the posterior is also a Beta — you just add the counts: \(\text{Beta}(a+\text{heads},\, b+\text{tails})\). This tidy "prior and posterior are the same family" property is called conjugacy, and it makes updating as easy as counting.

✍️ Worked example: updating on ten coin flips

Following bayes_inference.py: a coin with true bias 0.7, a Beta(2,2) prior, and a handful of flips.

1
Prior. Beta(2,2) is a gentle hump centred at 0.5 — "probably fairish, but I'm not sure." Its mean is \(a/(a+b)=2/4=0.5\).
2
Data. Flip 10 times, see 7 heads, 3 tails. The likelihood \(p^7(1-p)^3\) peaks at the data-only estimate (MLE) \(7/10=0.7\).
3
Update (just add counts). Posterior \(=\text{Beta}(2+7,\,2+3)=\text{Beta}(9,5)\). Its mean is \(9/14=0.64\) — sitting between the prior's 0.5 and the data's 0.7. That compromise is the prior and data each pulling their weight.
4
Uncertainty. The posterior isn't a point — it's a whole curve. Its 95% credible interval (the range holding 95% of the belief) is about [0.39, 0.86]: still wide, because 10 flips isn't much. Flip 200 times and it tightens dramatically. The Bayesian answer always comes with this honest measure of how sure it is.

🛠️ From scratch: Bayesian updating is counting

a0, b0 = 2.0, 2.0                      # prior Beta(2,2): "probably fairish"
heads, tails = flips.sum(), len(flips) - flips.sum()
a, b = a0 + heads, b0 + tails          # conjugate update -> posterior Beta(a, b)

post_mean = a / (a + b)                # the Bayesian point estimate
# 95% credible interval: the middle 95% of the posterior
lo, hi = np.percentile(rng.beta(a, b, 200000), [2.5, 97.5])
# post_mean sits BETWEEN the prior mean and the data's MLE — a weighted compromise

Because of conjugacy, the entire update is two additions. As data accumulates, the \(+\text{heads}\) and \(+\text{tails}\) swamp the prior's \(a_0,b_0\), so the posterior mean slides from the prior toward the data — evidence gradually overrules your starting assumption, exactly as it should.

The prior is a feature, not a bug. Critics say "priors are subjective." But every model encodes assumptions; Bayesians just make theirs explicit and let data override them. With little data, a sensible prior keeps you from wild conclusions (one heads in one flip doesn't mean the coin is 100% biased); with lots of data, the prior washes out and everyone agrees. Priors are also how you inject real knowledge — a physicist's prior on a constant, a doctor's prior on a disease rate — which is often exactly what you want.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Confusing likelihood with posterior. \(p(D\mid\theta)\) (how well \(\theta\) explains the data) is not \(p(\theta\mid D)\) (belief in \(\theta\) given the data). Bayes' rule is precisely the machinery for flipping one into the other.
  • Reporting only the posterior mean. The whole point is the distribution. A mean of 0.64 with a credible interval of [0.39, 0.86] is a very different situation from [0.62, 0.66] — don't throw the uncertainty away.
  • Using an overconfident prior. A prior that's too sharp can refuse to budge even against strong evidence. Unless you truly have strong prior knowledge, keep priors gentle and let the data speak.
  • Thinking a credible interval is a confidence interval. A 95% credible interval genuinely means "95% probability the value is in here" (given the model) — the intuitive reading that the frequentist confidence interval famously does not support.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Bayes' rule: posterior \(\propto\) likelihood \(\times\) prior — update beliefs with evidence.
  • The output is a distribution, not a point: the posterior carries honest uncertainty.
  • Prior + data = compromise: the posterior mean lies between the prior mean and the data's estimate, sliding toward the data as it accumulates.
  • Conjugacy (e.g., Beta prior + coin flips → Beta posterior) makes updating as simple as adding counts.
  • Credible interval: a range that holds a stated probability (e.g., 95%) of the posterior belief.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. In Bayes' rule, what does the posterior represent?

  • The belief before seeing data.
    That's the prior; the posterior comes after.
  • The updated belief about the unknown after seeing the data.
    Correct. Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior combines prior belief with evidence.
  • How well each value explains the data.
    That's the likelihood, one ingredient of the posterior.
  • A single best guess with no uncertainty.
    The posterior is a whole distribution, carrying uncertainty.

Q2. With a Beta(2,2) prior and 7 heads / 3 tails, the posterior is...

  • Beta(7,3).
    That ignores the prior counts.
  • Beta(2,2).
    That ignores the data.
  • Beta(5,9).
    Heads add to the first parameter, not the second — check the order.
  • Beta(9,5).
    Correct. Add heads to \(a\) and tails to \(b\): Beta(2+7, 2+3) = Beta(9,5).

Q3. Why does the posterior mean sit between the prior mean and the data's estimate?

  • It's a weighted compromise: prior and data each pull on the estimate.
    Correct. With more data, the data's pull dominates and the prior washes out.
  • Because the prior is always right.
    The prior gets overruled by enough data; it isn't assumed correct.
  • Because likelihoods are ignored.
    The likelihood (data) is exactly what pulls the estimate toward the MLE.
  • By coincidence.
    It's a structural property of combining prior and likelihood, not chance.

Q4. What extra thing does a Bayesian answer give you over a single point estimate?

  • A faster computation.
    Bayesian methods are often slower, not faster.
  • A guarantee of being correct.
    No method guarantees correctness; it quantifies uncertainty.
  • A full distribution with honest uncertainty (e.g., a credible interval).
    Correct. You get not just an estimate but how sure to be about it.
  • More training data.
    It doesn't create data; it reasons about what you have.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, reset and flip just 3 times, then flip 200 times. How does the width of the belief curve change, and what does the width represent?

Show solution

After 3 flips the curve is broad — the belief spans a wide range of plausible biases. After 200 flips it's a narrow spike near the true value. The width is the uncertainty: a wide posterior means "I'm not sure yet," a narrow one means "I'm confident now." This is the headline feature of Bayesian inference — the amount of data automatically controls how sure the model is allowed to be, and the width shrinks roughly like \(1/\sqrt{n}\) as flips \(n\) grow.

2. (transfer) You flip a coin once and get heads. The MLE says the coin is 100% biased toward heads. Why is the Bayesian posterior more sensible?

Show solution

The MLE (heads/flips = 1/1 = 1.0) confidently claims the coin never lands tails — an absurd conclusion from one flip. The Bayesian posterior with a gentle prior (say Beta(2,2)) gives Beta(3,2), mean 0.6 — leaning toward heads but nowhere near certain, with a wide credible interval. The prior prevents the wild overreaction to tiny data, encoding the common-sense fact that one flip barely constrains the bias. This regularising effect — pulling extreme estimates back toward reasonable values when data is scarce — is one of the most practically useful things priors do, and it connects directly to regularisation in Track 3.

3. (stretch) Conjugacy made this update trivial, but it only works for special prior–likelihood pairs. What do you do when the posterior has no closed form — which is nearly always in real models?

Show solution

You approximate it. Two big families, which are the next lessons: (1) Sampling — draw samples from the posterior even without a formula, using MCMC (Lesson 1); you get the distribution as a cloud of samples. (2) Optimisation — approximate the posterior with a simple distribution by fitting it, called variational inference (Lesson 2); fast but approximate. Both let you do Bayesian inference far beyond the handful of conjugate cases, which is what makes Bayesian deep learning and probabilistic programming possible. Conjugacy is the clean special case that builds intuition; MCMC and VI are the general tools.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Bayesian thinking is everywhere uncertainty matters: A/B testing (a credible interval on the conversion rate), medical diagnosis (updating disease probability as tests come in), spam filters (the original naive-Bayes classifier), robotics and self-driving (Bayesian filters fuse noisy sensors), and scientific inference across physics and genetics. In modern ML, the frontier is Bayesian deep learning — putting distributions over neural-network weights to get uncertainty estimates, crucial for safety-critical AI that must know when it doesn't know. The whole track builds toward that: exact inference here, then MCMC and variational methods to scale it, Gaussian processes for uncertainty over functions, and calibration to make any model's confidence trustworthy.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Bayesian inference keeps a distribution of beliefs instead of one guess, and updates it with data using Bayes' rule: posterior ∝ prior × likelihood. For a coin, the belief is a Beta curve, and each flip just adds to its counts, sharpening the curve around the truth. The payoff is an answer with honest error bars — you know not just the estimate but how sure to be."
