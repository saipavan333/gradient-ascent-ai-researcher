Active learning: let the model choose what to label

When every label costs money, don't label at random. Let the model point at the few examples it is most confused about, label those, and reach high accuracy with a fraction of the labelling budget. The learner becomes an active participant in its own education.

Track 13 · Lesson 4 Concept + code + interactive ~18 min Builds on: Lesson 3

Every method so far took its labelled data as a given. But in the real world you often control the labelling — you have a pile of unlabelled examples and a budget to send some to a human expert. The question becomes: which ones? Labelling at random treats every example as equally worth knowing, which is wasteful — most examples are obvious and teach the model nothing new. Active learning flips this around: the model itself nominates the examples it is most unsure about, you label just those, and it learns fastest per label. The intuition is the one every good student knows — you learn most from the problems at the edge of your understanding, not the ones you can already solve. This lesson shows a model reaching the same accuracy with a fraction of the labels, simply by choosing them well.

💡 Intuition: study the questions you're unsure about

Imagine cramming for an exam with limited time. You could review every topic equally — including the ones you've already nailed — or you could spend your hours on the handful of topics you keep getting wrong. The second strategy is obviously smarter: effort spent where you're uncertain moves your grade the most. Active learning gives a model exactly this study skill. After training on what it has, the model looks over the unlabelled pile and finds the examples where it is teetering on the fence — the ones right at its decision boundary, where a tiny nudge would flip its answer. Those are the examples whose true labels carry the most information, so those are the ones worth paying to label.

Why the boundary? A point the model classifies with 99% confidence is one it already "knows" — labelling it just confirms what it believed. A point it calls 51% class A, 49% class B is one genuine surprise away from teaching the model where the true border lies. Concentrating labels on the boundary sharpens exactly the part of the model that is still blurry.

The core loop, called uncertainty sampling, is short: train on what you have, score every unlabelled point by how uncertain the model is, label the most uncertain, repeat.

\[ x^{\*} = \arg\max_{x \in \text{unlabelled}}\ \text{uncertainty}(x), \qquad \text{uncertainty}(x) = -\bigl|\,p(x) - 0.5\,\bigr| \] (4.1)

In words: pick the unlabelled point \(x^{\*}\) with the highest uncertainty. For a two-class model, \(p(x)\) is the predicted probability of class A; the model is most unsure when \(p(x)\) is near \(0.5\) (a coin-flip), so \(-|p(x)-0.5|\) is largest exactly there. Send that point to be labelled, add it to the training set, retrain, and go again. Each label you buy is the most informative one available at that moment.

1
The candidates. \(p=0.98,\ 0.03,\ 0.52,\ 0.88,\ 0.47\). Which is the model least sure about?
2
Score each by \(-|p-0.5|\). \(0.98\to-0.48\), \(0.03\to-0.47\), \(0.52\to-0.02\), \(0.88\to-0.38\), \(0.47\to-0.03\). Largest (closest to 0) wins.
3
Pick. The winner is \(p=0.52\) (score \(-0.02\)) — an almost perfect coin-flip. That is the point sitting right on the decision boundary, and its true label pins the boundary down. Runner-up: \(p=0.47\).
4
Why not \(p=0.98\)? The model is 98% sure it's class A — labelling it almost certainly just says "yes, A," teaching nothing. Spending a precious label there is like re-studying a topic you've already mastered. Label the fence-sitter instead.

🛠️ From scratch: the uncertainty-sampling loop

idx = few_random_seeds()                     # start with a tiny labelled set
while len(idx) < budget:
    w, b = fit(X[idx], y[idx])                # train on what's labelled so far
    p = sigmoid(X[pool] @ w + b)              # model's class-A probability on the pool
    uncertainty = -abs(p - 0.5)               # highest when p is nearest 0.5  (eq 4.1)
    pick = pool[argmax(uncertainty)]          # the most informative point
    idx.append(pick)                          # "pay" to label it, add to training set
# vs. random: pick = a random pool point  -> needs many more labels for the same accuracy

The only change from ordinary supervised learning is the middle: instead of accepting whatever labelled data you were handed, the model requests the next label. In our validated demo, averaged over many runs, active learning reaches higher accuracy per label than random sampling — the "learning curve" climbs faster because every label lands where it matters.

Where this pays off most. Active learning shines exactly when labels are expensive and data is abundant: a radiologist labelling scans, a lawyer tagging documents, a chemist running an experiment. Instead of labelling 10,000 random items, you might label 1,000 well-chosen ones and match the accuracy — a 10× saving in the most expensive part of the pipeline. It pairs naturally with semi-supervised learning (Lesson 3): use active learning to pick the best seeds, then propagate.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Chasing noise instead of information. The most "uncertain" points can sometimes be corrupted or mislabelled outliers — genuinely ambiguous garbage, not informative boundary cases. Pure uncertainty sampling can waste labels on these; real systems add diversity or density weighting.
  • Sampling bias. Because the model only ever labels near its current boundary, it can miss a whole region it was wrong about from the start. The labelled set becomes non-representative — a known failure mode to watch for.
  • Retraining cost. The loop retrains after every label. For big models that's expensive, so in practice you label a batch of uncertain-and-diverse points per round, not one at a time.
  • Assuming it always beats random. On easy, well-separated data both reach high accuracy fast and active's edge is small. Its advantage grows when the boundary is subtle and labels are scarce.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Active learning lets the model choose which points to label — spending the labelling budget where it learns most.
  • Uncertainty sampling: label the point the model is least sure about (nearest the decision boundary, \(p\approx0.5\)) — eq (4.1).
  • Loop: train → score uncertainty → label the most uncertain → retrain. Repeat until budget spent.
  • It reaches target accuracy with far fewer labels than random when labels are scarce and the boundary is subtle.
  • Watch for pitfalls: noisy outliers, sampling bias, and retraining cost (batch selection helps).

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. In uncertainty sampling, which unlabelled point does the model request next?

  • The one it is most confident about.
    Labelling a confident point mostly confirms what the model already believes — low value.
  • A random point.
    That's the baseline active learning aims to beat.
  • The one it is most uncertain about (prediction nearest a coin-flip).
    Correct. Points near the decision boundary carry the most information about where the true border lies.
  • The one farthest from all others.
    Distance-based outliers aren't the criterion; predictive uncertainty is.

Q2. Why is labelling a point the model predicts with 99% confidence usually wasteful?

  • Its label almost certainly just confirms the model's belief, teaching little.
    Correct. High-confidence points are already "known"; the information gain is tiny.
  • Because confident points are always wrong.
    They're usually right — that's why labelling them teaches little.
  • Because it costs more to label.
    Labelling cost doesn't depend on the model's confidence.
  • Because it breaks the model.
    Nothing breaks; it's simply low-value.

Q3. What is "sampling bias" in active learning?

  • Labelling too many points at once.
    That's a cost/batching issue, not sampling bias.
  • Using a random seed set.
    Random seeds are fine; bias comes from the selection loop.
  • The model training too slowly.
    Speed is unrelated to sampling bias.
  • Only ever labelling near the current boundary, so the labelled set becomes non-representative and blind spots persist.
    Correct. Focusing on the current boundary can miss regions the model was wrong about from the start.

Q4. When is active learning's advantage over random labelling largest?

  • When classes are perfectly separated and easy.
    Then both do well fast and active's edge is small.
  • When labels are scarce and the decision boundary is subtle.
    Correct. With few labels and a hard boundary, choosing informative points matters most.
  • When you have unlimited labels.
    With unlimited labels, how you choose barely matters.
  • When there is no unlabelled pool.
    Active learning needs a pool to choose from.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, label 10 points on each side. Which learner's boundary settles into the right place first, and how does its accuracy bar compare after the same number of labels?

Show solution

The active learner (left) usually locks onto the correct boundary sooner and shows a higher accuracy bar for the same label count, because it spent every label near the boundary where the model was unsure. The random learner wastes some labels deep inside a cluster where the answer was never in doubt, so its boundary wanders longer. Same effort, better outcome — that gap is the value of active learning, and it's widest in the first several labels.

2. (transfer) Beyond "probability near 0.5," name another way to measure a model's uncertainty that works when there are many classes, not just two.

Show solution

Several good answers. Entropy of the predicted class distribution — highest when probability is spread evenly across classes — generalizes "near 0.5" to any number of classes. Margin sampling — the gap between the top two predicted probabilities, smallest when the model can't decide between two classes. Committee disagreement — train several models and pick points where they disagree most (query-by-committee). All capture the same idea: label where the model's answer is least settled. Entropy and margin are the standard multi-class choices.

3. (stretch) Pure uncertainty sampling can get stuck labelling one ambiguous corner of the data over and over. How would you modify the selection to keep the labelled set both informative and representative?

Show solution

Combine uncertainty with diversity and density. Instead of labelling the single most uncertain point, select a batch of uncertain points that are also spread out from each other (so you don't label ten near-duplicates), and weight uncertainty by how dense the region is (an uncertain point surrounded by many others is more representative than a lone uncertain outlier, which may just be noise). Methods like BADGE and core-set selection formalize this. The principle: an ideal query is uncertain, representative of a real region, and not redundant with points you've already labelled — balancing "what the model doesn't know" against "what the data actually looks like."

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Active learning runs wherever human labelling is the bottleneck: medical imaging, drug discovery (which experiment to run next is active learning over molecules), autonomous-driving datasets, and the human-feedback stages of training large language models — deciding which model outputs are most worth a human rating is an active-learning problem. It closes the loop on this whole track: self- and semi-supervised learning squeeze value from unlabelled data, and active learning makes the few labels you do buy count for the most. Together they are why modern AI can learn so much from so little human effort — the through-line of everything in Track 13.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Active learning lets the model pick what to label. It nominates the examples it's most unsure about — the ones sitting on its decision boundary — because their labels teach it the most. Train, find the most uncertain point, label it, retrain, repeat. You reach the same accuracy with far fewer labels than labelling at random, which is huge when each label is expensive."
