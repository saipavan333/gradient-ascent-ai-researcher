Learning without a full answer key

Everything you have built so far learned from labelled examples — an input paired with the right answer. But most data in the world has no answers attached. This track is about the family of methods that learn anyway, and it starts by mapping the whole family and meeting its simplest member.

Track 13 · Lesson 0 Concept + code + interactive ~18 min Builds on: Track 5

Up to now, "learning" has meant one thing: you hand the model a pile of examples where every input x comes with its correct answer y — a photo labelled "cat," a house with its sale price — and it learns the mapping from one to the other. That is supervised learning, and it is powerful. It also has an expensive secret: someone had to write down all those answers. Labelling a million images, transcribing a thousand hours of speech, having doctors annotate scans — that human effort is often the single biggest cost in a real project. Meanwhile, unlabelled data is nearly free and nearly infinite: every photo on the web, every sensor reading, every line of text ever written. This track is about the methods that turn that cheap, unlabelled ocean into something a model can learn from. This first lesson maps the whole landscape, then builds the simplest example you can hold in your head: finding groups in data that came with no groups marked.

💡 Intuition: the answer key is expensive, the questions are free

Imagine a giant library where the books arrive in unlabelled crates. Supervised learning is like hiring experts to read and catalogue every single book before you can use the library — thorough, but slow and costly. The other paradigms are clever ways to get value without cataloguing everything. Active learning catalogues only the handful of books the librarian is genuinely unsure about, and guesses the rest. Semi-supervised learning catalogues a few, then notices that books shelved near a catalogued one are probably the same genre, and spreads the labels outward. Self-supervised learning plays a trick: it covers up part of each book — say, the last chapter — and trains itself to predict the hidden part from the rest, learning what books are "about" with no human catalogue at all. And unsupervised learning just sorts the crates into piles of similar-looking books, no labels anywhere.

The single axis that organizes this whole family is how much human labelling it needs — from "every example" on the left to "none at all" on the right. Keep this map in your head; every lesson in this track is one column of it.

Notice the trade-off running left to right. More labels usually mean a more accurate model, but also more cost and less data you can actually afford to use. Less labelling means you can feed the model vastly more data for free — but the learning signal per example is weaker, so you need cleverer methods to extract it. Modern AI leans hard to the right: the reason today's large language models exist at all is that self-supervised learning let them train on essentially the entire internet without anyone labelling it (Track 10). This track walks that axis from right to left.

📚 The five paradigms, precisely

Let us pin down each one in a single sentence, because the rest of the track assumes you can tell them apart.

Supervised. You have pairs \((x, y)\). Learn a function \(f\) so that \(f(x)\approx y\) on new inputs. Classification (is this spam?) and regression (what's the price?) both live here.

Unsupervised. You have only inputs \(x\) — no \(y\) anywhere. Find structure: groups (clustering), a lower-dimensional summary (Track 15's PCA), or which points are unusual (anomaly detection). There is no "right answer" to check against, only structure that is or isn't useful.

Semi-supervised. You have a few labelled pairs and a lot of unlabelled inputs. Use the shape of the unlabelled data to make the few labels go further — the subject of Lesson 3.

Self-supervised. You have only inputs, but you invent a supervised task from them — hide part of each input and predict it from the rest. The invented labels come free from the data, and the representation you learn transfers to real tasks. This is Lessons 1 and 2, and it is the engine behind modern foundation models.

Active. You can request labels, but each one costs money, so the model itself chooses which few inputs are most worth labelling — Lesson 4.

🎯 The simplest case: finding groups with k-means

The purest example of learning with no labels is clustering: given a cloud of points and nothing else, split them into groups of similar points. The workhorse algorithm is k-means, and it is beautifully simple. You decide how many groups you want — call it \(k\) — and drop \(k\) "centre" markers anywhere. Then you repeat two steps until nothing moves:

\[ \textbf{Assign: } c_i = \arg\min_{j}\ \lVert x_i - \mu_j \rVert^2 \qquad\qquad \textbf{Update: } \mu_j = \frac{1}{|C_j|}\sum_{i \in C_j} x_i \] (0.1)

In words: Assign — give every point \(x_i\) to whichever centre \(\mu_j\) (mu, the group's centre) is nearest, measured by squared distance \(\lVert x_i - \mu_j\rVert^2\) (the straight-line distance, squared). \(c_i\) is just the label "which group point \(i\) landed in." Update — move each centre \(\mu_j\) to the average position of the points assigned to it (\(C_j\) is that set of points, \(|C_j|\) how many there are). Assign, update, assign, update. Each round can only lower the total distance from points to their centres, so it always settles down — usually within a handful of rounds. That "always improves, then stops" behaviour is the same descent logic you saw in optimization (Track 2), just with a discrete assignment step.

✍️ Worked example: one round of k-means on six points

Six points on a line, two centres. We will do exactly one Assign and one Update by hand so the arithmetic is transparent.

1
Setup. Points at \(1, 2, 3, 10, 11, 12\). Start the centres badly on purpose: \(\mu_A = 2\), \(\mu_B = 4\).
2
Assign. Each point joins the nearer centre. Distances to \(\mu_A=2\) vs \(\mu_B=4\): point 1 → A (dist 1 vs 3). Point 2 → A. Point 3 → tie-ish, \(|3-2|=1\) vs \(|3-4|=1\); break ties to A. Points 10, 11, 12 are all far closer to 4 than to 2, so → B. Groups: \(A=\{1,2,3\}\), \(B=\{10,11,12\}\).
3
Update. Move each centre to its group's mean. \(\mu_A = (1+2+3)/3 = 2\). \(\mu_B = (10+11+12)/3 = 11\). The B centre just leapt from 4 to 11 — pulled to where its points actually are.
4
Check for convergence. Run Assign again with \(\mu_A=2, \mu_B=11\): the same six points make the same choices, so nothing moves. Converged in one round. The algorithm found the two obvious clumps with no labels at all — it only ever looked at distances.

🛠️ From scratch: k-means in a dozen lines

def kmeans(X, k=3, iters=20):
    C = X[rng.choice(len(X), k, replace=False)]        # start: k random points as centres
    for _ in range(iters):
        d = ((X[:, None, :] - C[None, :, :])**2).sum(2) # squared distance to every centre
        a = d.argmin(1)                                 # ASSIGN: index of nearest centre
        for j in range(k):
            if (a == j).any():
                C[j] = X[a == j].mean(0)                # UPDATE: centre = mean of its points
    return a, C                                         # a = group of each point, C = centres

That is the entire algorithm. The two lines a = d.argmin(1) and C[j] = X[a==j].mean(0) are Assign and Update — the whole of equation (0.1). Run it on three blobs and it recovers them with about 98% purity (the fraction of points whose group matches the true, hidden cluster), even though the word "label" never appears. That is unsupervised learning working.

Two honest caveats. First, you have to choose \(k\) — k-means won't tell you how many groups exist. Second, the random starting centres matter: a bad start can settle into a worse grouping, so in practice you run it several times and keep the tightest result. Both are reminders that "no labels" doesn't mean "no choices" — you have simply moved the human judgment from labelling each point to setting up the method.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking clustering "found the right answer." There is no right answer in unsupervised learning — only groupings that are more or less useful. k-means will happily split a single blob into three if you ask for \(k=3\).
  • Confusing self-supervised with unsupervised. Both use unlabelled data, but self-supervised invents a prediction task (hide-and-predict) and trains a normal supervised model on it; unsupervised has no prediction target at all. This distinction matters for the whole track.
  • Assuming more labels are always better. They help accuracy, but labels cost time and money, and the unlabelled data you ignore may hold most of the signal. The art is spending your labelling budget where it counts — the theme of active and semi-supervised learning.
  • Forgetting to scale features before k-means. Distance is dominated by whichever feature has the largest units (dollars swamp "number of rooms"). Standardize first, or the clusters just track your biggest column.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • The paradigms sit on one axis: how much human labelling they need — supervised (all) → active/semi (some) → self-supervised/unsupervised (none).
  • Labels are the expensive part; unlabelled data is nearly free, so methods that exploit it unlock far more data.
  • k-means = repeat {assign each point to its nearest centre; move each centre to its points' mean} until nothing moves.
  • It always converges (each round lowers total distance) but to a local, start-dependent solution — so restart and keep the best.
  • Self-supervised ≠ unsupervised: self-supervised invents a prediction task from the data; unsupervised has none.

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What single axis best organizes the learning paradigms?

  • How deep the neural network is.
    Depth is an architecture choice, unrelated to how much labelling a paradigm needs.
  • How fast the model trains.
    Speed varies within every paradigm; it isn't what distinguishes them.
  • How much human labelling the method requires.
    Correct. From "every example labelled" (supervised) to "none" (self-/unsupervised), that axis is the organizing idea of the whole track.
  • Whether the data is images or text.
    Every paradigm applies to both; data type isn't the distinction.

Q2. In k-means, what happens in the "update" step?

  • Each centre moves to the mean position of the points currently assigned to it.
    Correct. Assign colours the points; update slides each centre to the middle of its colour.
  • Each point moves toward its centre.
    Points never move in k-means — only the centres do.
  • The number of clusters \(k\) is recomputed.
    \(k\) is fixed by you at the start; the algorithm never changes it.
  • The learning rate is decayed.
    k-means has no learning rate; it uses exact means, not gradient steps.

Q3. Why is unlabelled data so valuable?

  • It is always more accurate than labelled data.
    Accuracy isn't the point; unlabelled data carries no answers to be accurate about.
  • It needs no model to use.
    You still need a model or algorithm; the value is elsewhere.
  • It trains faster.
    Speed isn't the reason; the reason is quantity and cost.
  • It is abundant and nearly free, so methods that exploit it can use far more data.
    Correct. Labelling is the costly bottleneck; tapping the free unlabelled ocean is what scales modern AI.

Q4. What distinguishes self-supervised from unsupervised learning?

  • Self-supervised uses labels; unsupervised does not.
    Neither uses human labels — that's the confusion this question targets.
  • Self-supervised invents a prediction task from the data; unsupervised has no prediction target.
    Correct. Self-supervised hides part of the input and predicts it (a made-up supervised task); unsupervised just finds structure like clusters.
  • They are the same thing.
    They overlap in using unlabelled data but differ in whether a prediction task exists.
  • Unsupervised only works on images.
    Unsupervised methods apply to any data type.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, start with a deliberately bad set of centres (all three near one corner). How many Assign/Move rounds does it take to settle? What does that tell you about the starting point?

Show solution

Usually a few extra rounds compared with a spread-out start — and occasionally it settles into a worse grouping where two centres share one true blob and one centre covers two. That is exactly the "local, start-dependent solution" caveat: k-means always converges, but not always to the best grouping. The fix in practice is to run it several times from different random starts and keep the one with the smallest total point-to-centre distance.

2. (transfer) You run k-means with \(k=3\) on data that truly has only two groups. What do you expect to see, and how would you detect that \(k\) was too large?

Show solution

One of the two real blobs gets split across two centres, since k-means must use all three. You would detect it by watching how the total point-to-centre distance falls as you increase \(k\): it drops sharply up to the true number of groups and then only gently afterward — the "elbow." An elbow at \(k=2\) tells you the third cluster is spurious. This is the standard, if imperfect, way to choose \(k\).

3. (stretch) k-means uses squared distance, which implicitly assumes clusters are roughly round and similar-sized. Sketch a dataset where that assumption fails badly, and name what you'd reach for instead.

Show solution

Two long crescents interlocking (the classic "two moons"), or one tiny dense blob beside one huge sparse one. Squared distance carves space into straight-edged territories around each centre, so it slices a crescent in half and lets the big blob swallow the small one. For shapes like these you'd reach for density-based clustering (DBSCAN, which grows clusters along connected dense regions) or spectral clustering (which uses the same neighbour-graph idea as Lesson 3's label propagation). The lesson: every unsupervised method encodes an assumption about what "a group" looks like — match the method to your data's shape.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Clustering quietly runs a huge amount of the world: customer segmentation, grouping documents by topic, compressing images by their common colours, spotting anomalies in network traffic. But the deeper story of this track is the rightward march along the labelling axis. For decades, progress meant collecting more labels. The breakthrough of the last few years was realizing that self-supervised learning could turn the entire unlabelled internet into training signal — which is precisely how large language models and modern vision models are pre-trained (Tracks 9–10). Labels didn't vanish; they moved to a tiny, precious fine-tuning step at the end. Understanding this landscape is understanding why AI suddenly scaled.

Recap — say it to a friend

"The learning paradigms line up by how many labels they need — from supervised (all) to unsupervised (none). Labels are the expensive part, so the clever methods squeeze signal out of free, unlabelled data. The simplest is k-means: repeatedly assign each point to its nearest centre, then move each centre to its points' average — and out fall the groups, no labels required."
