You have built every part. Embeddings turn tokens into vectors; positional encodings stamp them with order; self-attention lets them share context; multi-head attention gives them several relationships at once; residuals and LayerNorm let the blocks stack deep. What remains is to wire them into one machine with a single, almost laughably simple job — guess the next token — and to notice that this one job, done well enough on enough text, is all that a GPT ever learns. By the end you will see how the model behind ChatGPT is, at heart, an autocomplete engine you now understand end to end.

💡 Intuition: a very good autocomplete

GPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer, but the idea is simpler than the name. Show it some text — a prefix — and it outputs a probability for every possible next token. That is the entire model: prefix in, next-token odds out. To train it, we play "guess the next word" across a mountain of text: at every position the model predicts the next token, we compare its guess to the real one, and we nudge its weights toward the truth. To generate, we run it forward, sample one token from its predicted odds, glue that token onto the prefix, and repeat — the model writing by continuously autocompleting itself. This feed-your-own-output-back-in style is called autoregressive.

The full assembly is a short pipeline. Each token id is looked up in an embedding table \(E\) (a big matrix with one learned vector per vocabulary word) and its positional encoding is added. That stack of vectors flows through \(N\) transformer blocks, a final LayerNorm tidies it, and one last matrix — the unembedding \(W_U\) — turns each token's vector into a score for every word in the vocabulary. Softmax makes those scores a probability distribution.

\[ h = E[x] + PE, \qquad h \leftarrow \operatorname{Block}(h)\ \times N, \qquad p = \operatorname{softmax}\big(\operatorname{LN}(h)\,W_U\big) \] (1)

Reading it: \(E[x]\) looks up the embedding of each input token \(x\); we add the positional encoding \(PE\); we pass the result through \(N\) copies of the transformer block from Lesson 2; a final LayerNorm normalizes; \(W_U\) maps each token's vector (width \(d\)) to a vector of length "vocabulary size," one raw score — a logit — per possible next token; softmax turns the logits into probabilities. (Often \(W_U\) reuses the embedding matrix \(E\), a trick called weight tying.)

There is one crucial new rule. When predicting the token at position \(i\), the model must not be allowed to see the future — otherwise "predict the next word" is trivial, because the answer is sitting right there in the input. So we use causal (masked) self-attention: each token may attend only to itself and to earlier positions. We enforce it by setting the attention score to \(-\infty\) for any future position before the softmax; \(e^{-\infty}=0\), so those tokens receive exactly zero weight.

\[ s_{ij} = \begin{cases} \dfrac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}, & j \le i \\[4pt] -\infty, & j > i \end{cases} \] (2)

In words: token \(i\) computes normal scaled scores for every position \(j\) that is at or before it (\(j \le i\)), but future positions (\(j > i\)) get \(-\infty\), which softmax turns into zero attention. This "no peeking ahead" mask is the only structural difference between the encoder attention of Lessons 1–2 and the decoder-style attention inside GPT.

Now training. Because the model outputs a probability for the true next token at every position, the natural loss is to make those true tokens as likely as possible. That is the cross-entropy loss, which is exactly the average negative log-likelihood of the correct tokens:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \log p_\theta\big(x_{t+1} \mid x_1,\ldots,x_t\big) \] (3)

Every symbol: \(T\) is the number of positions; \(x_{t+1}\) is the actual next token; \(p_\theta(\cdot)\) is the probability the model (with weights \(\theta\)) assigned to that token given everything before it; \(\log\) turns "make the probability big" into "make its log big," and the minus sign turns maximizing into minimizing so gradient descent can chase it. Minimizing this is maximum likelihood estimation — the same principle from Track 1, now training a text generator. And here is the beautiful part: one causal forward pass produces a prediction at every position at once, so a single sentence gives \(T\) training signals in parallel.

Finally, generation with a creativity dial. Given the model's logits \(z\) for the next token, we can reshape how adventurous the sampling is with a temperature \(\tau\): divide the logits by \(\tau\) before softmax.

\[ p_i = \frac{e^{\,z_i/\tau}}{\sum_j e^{\,z_j/\tau}} \] (4)

Small \(\tau\) (below 1) makes the distribution sharper — the model plays it safe, leaning hard on its top guess; as \(\tau\to 0\) it becomes greedy, always taking the single most likely token. Large \(\tau\) (above 1) flattens the distribution toward uniform — more surprising, more creative, and riskier. At \(\tau=1\) you get the model's honest odds. Play with exactly this dial below.

✍️ Worked example: turn the temperature dial by hand

Three candidate tokens A, B, C have logits \(z = [2, 1, 0]\). Watch the probabilities as we change \(\tau\).

1
\(\tau = 1\) (honest odds). \(e^{2}=7.39,\ e^{1}=2.72,\ e^{0}=1\); sum \(=11.11\). \(p=[\,0.67,\ 0.24,\ 0.09\,]\). A is the front-runner but B and C are live.
2
\(\tau = 0.5\) (cautious). Divide logits by 0.5: \([4,2,0]\). \(e^{4}=54.6,\ e^{2}=7.39,\ e^{0}=1\); sum \(=63.0\). \(p=[\,0.87,\ 0.12,\ 0.02\,]\) — sharper, A now dominates.
3
\(\tau = 2\) (creative). Divide by 2: \([1,0.5,0]\). \(e^{1}=2.72,\ e^{0.5}=1.65,\ e^{0}=1\); sum \(=5.37\). \(p=[\,0.51,\ 0.31,\ 0.19\,]\) — flatter, the long shots get a real chance.
4
The two extremes. As \(\tau\to 0\) the weight piles entirely onto A — pure greedy decoding, deterministic but prone to loops. As \(\tau\to\infty\) all three approach \(1/3\) — uniform random, maximally surprising and often incoherent. Temperature never changes what the model believes; it only changes how boldly you sample from those beliefs.

🛠️ From scratch: the whole GPT, forward and generate

import numpy as np

def gpt_forward(idx):                        # idx: (T,) token ids of the prefix
    x = embed[idx] + pos_enc[:len(idx)]      # token meaning + position  -> (T, d)
    for blk in blocks:
        x = blk(x, causal=True)              # each block attends only to the past
    x = layer_norm(x, g_final, b_final)
    return x @ W_U                           # logits: (T, vocab) — a score per next token

def generate(idx, n_new, temp=1.0):
    for _ in range(n_new):
        logits = gpt_forward(idx)[-1]        # scores for the token after the last one
        p = softmax(logits / temp)           # temperature-scaled next-token distribution
        nxt = np.random.choice(len(p), p=p)  # sample one token from those odds
        idx = np.append(idx, nxt)            # feed it back in -> autoregressive
    return idx

# Training target = the input shifted left by one (predict token t+1 at position t).
# Loss = cross-entropy(logits, targets) = average negative log-likelihood.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Forgetting the causal mask. Without it, each position can see its own answer, the loss collapses to near zero in training, and the model generates garbage at test time. The mask is what makes "predict the next token" a real task.
  • Off-by-one targets. The label for position \(t\) is the token at position \(t+1\). Line up inputs and targets with a one-step shift, or you train the model to predict the token it was just given.
  • Thinking temperature changes the model. It only rescales the logits at sampling time. The learned probabilities are fixed; \(\tau\) just decides how boldly you draw from them.
  • Confusing training with generation. Training scores all \(T\) positions in one parallel forward pass with the true tokens; generation runs one token at a time, feeding each sample back in. Same weights, very different loops.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • Assembly: embeddings + positions → \(N\) transformer blocks → LayerNorm → unembedding → softmax = next-token odds.
  • Causal mask: future scores set to \(-\infty\) so each token attends only to itself and the past.
  • Objective: cross-entropy = negative log-likelihood of the true next token; minimizing it is maximum likelihood.
  • Parallel training: one causal forward pass gives a prediction at every position at once.
  • Sampling: temperature \(\tau\) sharpens (\(\tau\to0\), greedy) or flattens (\(\tau\) large, creative) the next-token distribution.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What is the purpose of the causal mask in GPT's attention?

  • To speed up the softmax.
    It doesn't speed anything up; it changes what each token can see.
  • To normalize the attention weights.
    Softmax normalizes; the mask restricts which positions are allowed.
  • To stop a token from attending to future positions, so predicting the next token is a real task.
    Correct. Future scores are set to \(-\infty\) (zero weight after softmax), so the model can't peek at the answer it must predict.
  • To add positional information.
    That's the positional encoding's job, done at the input, not the mask's.

Q2. What quantity does a GPT minimize during training?

  • The distance between attention weights and 1.
    Attention weights already sum to 1; that's not the training objective.
  • The negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy) of the true next tokens.
    Correct. Making the correct next token as probable as possible is exactly maximum likelihood, written as cross-entropy loss.
  • The number of parameters.
    Parameter count is a design choice, not something training minimizes.
  • The temperature.
    Temperature is a sampling knob used at generation time, not a training loss.

Q3. Turning the temperature toward 0 does what?

  • Makes sampling greedy — it almost always picks the single most likely token.
    Correct. Dividing logits by a tiny \(\tau\) makes the top logit dominate, so softmax collapses onto the argmax.
  • Makes the output more random.
    That's high temperature; low temperature is the opposite.
  • Changes the model's learned weights.
    Temperature never touches the weights; it only rescales logits at sampling time.
  • Adds new tokens to the vocabulary.
    The vocabulary is fixed; temperature just reshapes the odds over it.

Q4. How does GPT generate a long passage of text?

  • It outputs the whole passage in one shot.
    It produces one token's distribution at a time, not a full passage at once.
  • It picks the globally optimal sentence by search.
    Plain sampling is local and token-by-token, not a global optimum search.
  • It reads the passage backwards.
    Generation goes left to right, extending the prefix forward.
  • Autoregressively: sample the next token, append it, and repeat with the longer prefix.
    Correct. Each new token is fed back in, so the model continuously autocompletes its own output.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, set the temperature to 0.1, then to 1.8. Describe the "mat" bar and the long-shot bars in each case, and say which setting risks repetitive text and which risks incoherence.

Show solution

At \(\tau=0.1\) the "mat" bar is nearly 100% and the long shots are almost invisible — the model will pick "mat" almost every time, which in real generation causes safe but repetitive, loop-prone text. At \(\tau=1.8\) the bars flatten out and the long shots (roof, grass, ".") get meaningful probability — more variety, but also the risk of picking an unlikely token that derails into incoherence. Practical decoding usually sits near \(\tau\approx0.7\text{–}1.0\), often combined with top-k or nucleus (top-p) filtering to cut off the worst long shots.

2. (transfer) A sequence has 10 tokens. In one causal forward pass, how many next-token predictions (training signals) does the model produce, and why is that efficient?

Show solution

Nine (or ten if you count predicting the token after the last, given a target): position 1 predicts token 2, position 2 predicts token 3, and so on, so each of the first nine positions yields one supervised prediction from a single pass. The causal mask is what makes this safe — every position sees only its own past, so all predictions can be computed simultaneously without leaking future tokens. That parallelism is a major reason transformers train so much faster than step-by-step RNNs, which must unroll one position at a time.

3. (stretch) With logits \(z=[2,1,0]\), you computed the \(\tau=1\) distribution \([0.67,0.24,0.09]\). Show what happens to token C's probability as \(\tau\) grows very large, and explain the limit in words.

Show solution

Dividing by a large \(\tau\) pushes all three scaled logits toward 0, so \(e^{z_i/\tau}\to e^0=1\) for every token and the probabilities approach \([1/3,1/3,1/3]\) — C rises from 0.09 toward 0.333. In the limit \(\tau\to\infty\), temperature erases the differences between logits entirely, leaving a uniform distribution: the model's preferences are washed out and sampling becomes a fair die roll over the vocabulary. This is the mathematical opposite of the \(\tau\to0\) greedy limit, and together they bracket every sampling behavior in between.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

The model you just assembled is, structurally, GPT-3 — the difference is scale. GPT-2 stacked 48 of these blocks; GPT-3 stacked 96 and grew to 175 billion parameters trained on hundreds of billions of tokens, and somewhere along that curve it began to do arithmetic, translation, and reasoning nobody trained it to do — the phenomenon of emergence, mapped by the scaling laws that predict loss from model size, data, and compute. Real chat assistants add two stages on top of this next-token core: instruction tuning (fine-tuning on demonstrations) and reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), which aligns the raw autocomplete toward being helpful and safe. But the beating heart is exactly what is on this page — a stack of transformer blocks trained to predict the next token, sampled with a temperature dial. You did not just study the architecture behind modern AI; you built it.

Recap — say it to a friend

"A GPT is embeddings plus positions, run through a stack of causal transformer blocks and a final projection, trained to predict the next token by minimizing negative log-likelihood — then it writes by sampling one token at a time from its own predictions, with a temperature dial that trades safety for creativity."
