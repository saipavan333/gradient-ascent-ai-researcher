From sequences to attention

Language is a sequence, and the meaning of a word depends on the other words around it — "it" points back to something said earlier. The transformer's answer is one of the most consequential ideas in modern AI: instead of passing meaning down a relay line, let every word look directly at every other word and decide, for itself, which ones matter.

Read this sentence: "The cat sat down because it was tired." You knew instantly that "it" means the cat, not the floor. Your eyes flicked back a few words, found the noun that fits, and moved on. That tiny backward glance — done for every word, in every direction — is the whole idea behind attention, the mechanism at the heart of every transformer and every large language model. This lesson builds the intuition; the next one turns it into three dot products you can compute by hand.

💡 Intuition: every word gets to look around the room

A token is one small chunk of text the model works with — think of it as roughly a word (later we chop words into word-pieces, but "word" is close enough for now). A sequence is just an ordered list of tokens, like the words of a sentence in order.

Here is the problem. The meaning of a token is not fixed — it depends on context, the other tokens nearby. "Bank" means something different next to "river" than next to "money." "It" is empty until you find what it refers to. So a good language model cannot treat each word alone; it has to let words consult each other.

The older approach — the recurrent neural network (RNN), which you meet in full in Track 16 — read the sentence strictly left to right, squeezing everything seen so far into a single fixed-size memory called the hidden state. Picture a game of telephone: each word whispers a summary to the next. By the time the message reaches "it," the details about "cat" have passed through many hands and faded. Information from far back gets diluted, and because the reading is sequential, you cannot process the words in parallel either.

Attention throws out the telephone line. Instead, it seats all the tokens at one round table and lets each one look at every other one at the same time. When it is "it"'s turn, "it" glances at "cat," at "sat," at "tired," at all of them, and asks: how relevant is each of you to me right now? It then builds its updated meaning as a weighted blend of what the relevant tokens are carrying. No relay, no fading, no forced order of processing — just direct, all-to-all comparison.

We measure "how relevant is token \(j\) to token \(i\)" with a single number, a similarity score \(s_{ij}\) — bigger means "more relevant." But raw scores are awkward to combine. We want a clean set of weights: all non-negative, and adding up to 1, like slices of a pie that say "spend this fraction of your attention here." The tool that turns a list of scores into exactly that is the softmax. In plain words: softmax takes any list of numbers and turns it into probabilities that are all positive and sum to 1, giving the biggest scores the biggest slices.

\[ a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(s_{ij}) = \frac{e^{\,s_{ij}}}{\sum_{k} e^{\,s_{ik}}} \] (1)

Reading the symbols: \(s_{ij}\) is the raw similarity of query token \(i\) to token \(j\); \(e^{\,s_{ij}}\) is that score run through the exponential (which makes everything positive and stretches big scores further ahead of small ones); the sum in the denominator runs over every token \(k\) in the sequence, so dividing by it forces the whole row to add up to 1. The result \(a_{ij}\) is the attention weight: the fraction of token \(i\)'s attention that lands on token \(j\).

Now use those weights. Give every token a value vector \(v_j\) — a little bundle of numbers holding what token \(j\) has to contribute. Token \(i\)'s fresh, context-aware representation is the weighted average of everyone's values:

\[ z_i = \sum_{j} a_{ij}\, v_j \] (2)

In words: \(z_i\) — the new vector for token \(i\) — is a mix of the value vectors \(v_j\) of all tokens, where each is weighed by how much attention \(i\) paid it. If "it" put 70% of its attention on "cat," then 70% of "it"'s new meaning is made of "cat." That single move — compare to everyone, softmax into weights, blend the values — is attention. Lesson 1 shows where the scores \(s_{ij}\) and the values \(v_j\) actually come from (three learned matrices). For now, play with the blend itself.

✍️ Worked example: blend "it" by hand

Take a tiny three-word world: cat, sat, it. Suppose the query "it" has already been compared to each and got similarity scores \(s = [\,2,\ 0,\ 1\,]\) for \([\text{cat},\ \text{sat},\ \text{it}]\). Let us turn those into attention weights and blend.

1
Exponentiate each score. \(e^{2}=7.389,\ e^{0}=1.000,\ e^{1}=2.718\). The exponential is what makes the biggest score pull far ahead.
2
Add them up. \(7.389+1.000+2.718 = 11.107\). This total is the denominator that forces the weights to sum to 1.
3
Divide to get the weights. \(a = [\,7.389,\ 1.000,\ 2.718\,]/11.107 = [\,0.67,\ 0.09,\ 0.24\,]\). Check: they add to 1.00. So "it" spends 67% of its attention on "cat," just 9% on "sat," 24% on itself.
4
Blend the values. The new vector for "it" is \(z = 0.67\,v_{\text{cat}} + 0.09\,v_{\text{sat}} + 0.24\,v_{\text{it}}\). Two-thirds of "it"'s refreshed meaning is now literally made of "cat" — the model has resolved the reference just by averaging. No grammar rules were programmed. Similarity, then softmax, then a weighted average is enough to make "it" mean "cat."

🛠️ From scratch: attention weights in eight lines

import numpy as np

def softmax(x):
    e = np.exp(x - x.max())        # subtract the max: same answer, no overflow
    return e / e.sum()             # -> positive numbers that sum to 1

tokens = ["the","cat","sat","because","it","was","tired"]
V = np.array([[0.4,0.2,0.2],[2.0,0.4,0.6],[0.4,2.0,0.4],[0.2,0.4,0.4],
              [1.8,0.4,0.8],[0.4,0.6,1.0],[0.6,0.4,2.0]])   # a meaning vector per token

def attend(i):
    scores  = V @ V[i]             # similarity of token i to every token (a dot product)
    weights = softmax(scores)      # -> attention weights, one per token, summing to 1
    return weights, weights @ V    # the blend: a weighted average of all value vectors

w, z = attend(4)                   # query = "it"
print(dict(zip(tokens, np.round(w,2))))   # where "it" looks: 'cat' gets the most

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking attention reads left to right. It doesn't. Every pair of tokens is compared at once, in parallel. Attention has no built-in sense of order at all — that has to be added back separately (positional encodings, Lesson 3).
  • Thinking the weights are fixed or learned constants. They are recomputed from scratch for every input sentence, from the similarities between that sentence's own tokens. Different sentence, different weights.
  • Reading an attention weight as "how important this word is." A weight \(a_{ij}\) is about a pair — how much token \(i\) looks at token \(j\) — and each token's weights form their own pie that sums to 1. There is no single global importance.
  • Assuming a token only looks backward. Plain attention looks both ways. Only causal (masked) attention, used when a model must predict the next word, forbids looking ahead — we build that in Lesson 4.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • The problem: a token's meaning depends on context, and older RNNs squeezed all context through one fading, sequential memory.
  • The fix: let every token compare itself directly to every other token — all-to-all, in parallel, with no distance penalty.
  • Weights: \(a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(s_{ij})\) turns similarity scores into a pie of attention that sums to 1.
  • Output: \(z_i = \sum_j a_{ij} v_j\) — each token's new meaning is a weighted blend of everyone's values.
  • Why it mattered: parallel and distance-blind, attention scales to long text and huge models in a way recurrence never could.

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What is the single biggest advantage of attention over an RNN's hidden state?

  • It uses fewer parameters.
    Attention doesn't win on parameter count; big transformers have plenty. The win is in how information flows.
  • It reads the sentence backwards.
    Direction isn't the point; plain attention looks both ways at once.
  • Any token can reach any other directly, in parallel, with no fading over distance.
    Correct. There is no relay to dilute far-away information and no forced left-to-right order, so long-range links and parallel computation both come for free.
  • It never needs training.
    The vectors and scoring are still learned; attention is a mechanism, not a shortcut around training.

Q2. Why do we push similarity scores through a softmax before using them?

  • To make the model deeper.
    Softmax adds no depth; it is a normalizing step, not a layer of computation in that sense.
  • To turn arbitrary scores into non-negative weights that sum to 1.
    Correct. That lets us treat the weights as a pie of attention and take a proper weighted average of the values.
  • To make the scores bigger.
    Softmax normalizes; the outputs are between 0 and 1, not bigger than the inputs.
  • To remove the need for value vectors.
    Values are still essential — softmax only produces the weights that blend them.

Q3. In \(z_i = \sum_j a_{ij} v_j\), what does \(z_i\) represent?

  • The raw input embedding of token \(i\), unchanged.
    No — \(z_i\) is the updated representation after mixing in context.
  • The attention weight of token \(i\).
    The weights are the \(a_{ij}\); \(z_i\) is what they produce.
  • The single most relevant token to \(i\).
    Attention blends many tokens, not just the top one; it's an average, not an argmax.
  • Token \(i\)'s new, context-aware vector: a weighted blend of all the value vectors.
    Correct. Each token's output is the attention-weighted average of every token's value.

Q4. Attention by itself has no sense of word order. What does that imply?

  • Order information must be added separately, or "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look the same.
    Correct. Pure attention treats the input as a bag of tokens, so position has to be injected — that's what positional encodings (Lesson 3) do.
  • Attention automatically knows which word came first.
    It doesn't; the all-to-all comparison is symmetric in position by default.
  • Word order never matters in language.
    Order matters enormously; that's exactly why we must restore it.
  • RNNs also ignore order.
    RNNs are the opposite — they are built around order, processing strictly in sequence.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, click "cat" and then "tired" as the query. In one sentence each, describe where their attention concentrates and why it makes sense.

Show solution

"cat" leans on itself and on "it" — the two words sharing its "animate subject" flavor, exactly the pair a reader links. "tired" leans on itself and on "was," the words carrying its "state / description" flavor. Because the weights are just softmax of vector similarity, tokens with similar meaning vectors attend to each other — which is why the pronoun and its antecedent light up together.

2. (transfer) Compute the attention weights by hand for scores \(s = [\,3,\ 1,\ 1\,]\). Which token dominates, and by roughly how much?

Show solution

\(e^{3}=20.09,\ e^{1}=2.718,\ e^{1}=2.718\); sum \(=25.53\). Weights \(=[\,0.787,\ 0.106,\ 0.106\,]\). The first token dominates with about 79% of the attention — roughly seven to eight times each of the others. A gap of 2 in the raw scores becomes a large gap in weight because the exponential amplifies differences before normalizing.

3. (stretch) Suppose every similarity score in a row is equal (say all zeros). What attention weights come out, and what does the output \(z_i\) become? What does this tell you about the "default" behavior of attention?

Show solution

If all \(n\) scores are equal, softmax gives every token the same weight \(1/n\), so \(z_i\) is the plain average of all value vectors — attention with no opinion. This is the useful default: when a token has no reason to prefer anyone, it falls back to "listen to everybody equally." Learning then means learning to bend those scores so the weights concentrate where they should. It also foreshadows a subtlety we fix next lesson: as vectors grow larger in dimension, dot-product scores grow too, which can push softmax to the sharp, all-or-nothing extreme — the reason we divide by \(\sqrt{d}\).

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Attention arrived as a bolt-on to RNN translators around 2015, but the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" made the radical claim in its title: drop the recurrence entirely and keep only attention. That transformer architecture now underpins essentially every frontier model — GPT, Claude, Gemini, LLaMA — plus vision, audio, protein folding, and code. The reason is exactly the property we built here: because attention is parallel and distance-blind, it rides the wave of ever-faster hardware in a way sequential RNNs never could, which is what made training on trillions of tokens possible. The current research frontier is the one cost we glossed over — comparing every token to every other is quadratic in sequence length — and much of today's work (FlashAttention, sparse and linear attention, the state-space models in Track 24) is about keeping the magic while taming that cost.

Recap — say it to a friend

"Attention lets every word in a sentence look at every other word at once, score how relevant each is, softmax those scores into a pie of weights that sums to 1, and rebuild its own meaning as a weighted blend of the others — so 'it' can literally become 'cat' just by averaging."
