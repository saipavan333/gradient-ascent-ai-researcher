Ablations & controls

Your method has five clever parts and it hits 94%. But which part is doing the work? An ablation answers that — by switching off one component at a time and measuring what breaks. It's the difference between "it works" and "I understand why it works."

Track 22 · Lesson 1 Concept + code + interactive ~24 min Builds on: Lesson 0

An ablation (the word comes from medicine — surgically removing a part to see what it did) is an experiment where you disable one component of your system, retrain, and measure how much performance drops. That drop is the component's contribution. Ablations are the natural sequel to designing experiments: instead of asking "does my whole method beat the baseline?", they ask the sharper question "of all the pieces in my method, which ones actually matter?" Papers that skip ablations often turn out to be named after the one component that contributes almost nothing — the real work being done quietly by a mundane part everyone ignored. Ablation is how you find out.

💡 Intuition: muting instruments in a song

Imagine a song sounds great, and you want to know what each instrument contributes. You can't tell just by listening to the whole mix. So you mute one track at a time: mute the drums — the energy collapses, drums matter a lot. Mute the tambourine — you can barely tell, so it's decorative. Mute the bass — the whole thing feels hollow, bass was secretly holding it together. By removing one instrument and hearing what's lost, you learn each one's true role, which the full mix hides.

An ablation mutes components of a model. Turn off the pretraining and re-measure; turn off the fancy attention block and re-measure; turn off the data augmentation and re-measure. The size of the drop tells you how much each part was worth. And just like with music, the surprise is common: the component the paper is proudest of ("our novel gadget") often barely changes the number, while an unglamorous part (pretraining, data augmentation) turns out to carry the whole song.

Two rules keep an ablation honest. First, retrain when you remove a part — don't just delete it at test time, unless "what happens if I disable this at inference?" is genuinely your question; a model trained with a component and then robbed of it isn't the same as one trained without it. Second, hold everything else fixed and average over seeds, exactly as in the last lesson — an ablation is just a controlled experiment where the variable is "is this component present?" A useful third habit is a control ablation: remove something you're confident shouldn't matter and confirm the drop is ~0. If it isn't, your measurement harness is broken, not the component.

One caution the interactive simplifies away: contributions aren't perfectly additive. Here, turning two things off drops the score by the sum of their parts, but in real models components interact — two pieces might be redundant (removing either alone does little, but removing both is disastrous) or synergistic (each is worthless without the other). That's why serious papers report not just single-component removals but a few combined ones too, and why the honest summary is "component X contributes about N points in this configuration," not "X is worth N points, universally."

✍️ Worked example: reading an ablation table

You built a model that reaches 94.0%. It has four ingredients: large-scale pretraining, an attention block, data augmentation, and a "novel gadget" the paper is named after. You run each single-component ablation over 3 seeds. Here's the table — let's read it like a researcher.

1
Full model: 94.0. That's the reference; every ablation is measured as a drop from here.
2
Remove pretraining → 88.0 (−6.0). The biggest drop by far. Whatever else is going on, pretraining is the engine. If you had one sentence, it would be "pretraining carries this model."
3
Remove attention → 90.5 (−3.5); remove augmentation → 92.5 (−1.5). Both real, both worth keeping, in a clear pecking order. These are the supporting instruments.
4
Remove the "novel gadget" → 93.8 (−0.2). The drop is smaller than the run-to-run noise (±0.2 across seeds), so it's indistinguishable from zero. The uncomfortable finding: the paper's headline component does essentially nothing, while unglamorous pretraining and augmentation do the real work. A control check confirms the harness is sane — ablating a truly inert setting also gives ≈0. Reporting this honestly is what separates science from marketing; hiding it is how the field accumulates gadgets that don't matter.

🛠️ An ablation loop

An ablation study is a loop that disables one component at a time, retrains from scratch, and records the drop from the full model — averaged over seeds so the drops aren't just noise.

import numpy as np

COMPONENTS = ["pretraining", "attention", "augmentation", "gadget"]

def evaluate(disabled=()):                          # `disabled` = components turned OFF
    cfg = {c: (c not in disabled) for c in COMPONENTS}
    return train_and_eval(cfg)                       # RETRAIN each time, don't just delete

full = np.mean([evaluate() for _ in range(3)])       # all on, averaged over 3 seeds
print(f"full model: {full:.1f}")
for c in COMPONENTS:
    ablated = np.mean([evaluate(disabled=(c,)) for _ in range(3)])
    print(f"  - remove {c:<12}: {ablated:.1f}   (drop {full-ablated:+.1f})")

# full model: 94.0
#   - remove pretraining : 88.0   (drop -6.0)
#   - remove attention   : 90.5   (drop -3.5)
#   - remove augmentation: 92.5   (drop -1.5)
#   - remove gadget      : 93.8   (drop -0.2)   <- within noise: the namesake barely matters

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Deleting at test time instead of retraining. A model trained with a component and then stripped of it isn't the same as one trained without it — unless inference-time removal is precisely your question, retrain.
  • Changing more than one thing. An ablation is a controlled experiment; if removing "the attention block" also quietly changes the learning rate, the drop is uninterpretable.
  • No seeds. A −0.2 "drop" from a single run may be pure noise. Average over seeds and compare drops to the run-to-run spread.
  • No control ablation. Without removing something inert to confirm a ~0 drop, you can't trust that your measured drops mean anything.
  • Assuming additivity. Components interact; the drop from removing two is not always the sum of removing each. Report a few combined ablations.
  • Only ablating flattering components. Cherry-picking which parts to test — showing only the ablations that make your method look essential — is a subtle dishonesty.

🔑 Key ideas to remember

  • An ablation removes one component, retrains, and measures the drop — the drop is that component's contribution.
  • Retrain, don't just delete at test time, and hold everything else fixed (a controlled experiment where the variable is "present or not").
  • Average over seeds; a drop smaller than the run-to-run noise is indistinguishable from zero.
  • Add a control ablation — removing something inert should give a ~0 drop, proving your harness is sane.
  • Contributions interact, so report a few combined removals and scope claims to "in this configuration."

🧩 Quiz

🧩 Check your understanding
Answer from memory, then read why.

Q1. What does the size of the accuracy drop in an ablation tell you?

  • How fast that component runs.
    Ablations measure contribution to accuracy, not speed.
  • How much that component contributes to performance.
    Correct. A big drop means the component was important; a near-zero drop means it barely helps.
  • How many parameters it has.
    Parameter count isn't what the drop measures.
  • Whether the code has a bug.
    The drop measures contribution; bugs are caught by control ablations, not by the drop itself.

Q2. Why should you retrain the model when you ablate a component, rather than just disabling it at inference?

  • Retraining is faster.
    It's slower, not faster; the reason is correctness.
  • It doesn't matter; the two give identical results.
    They generally differ — a model adapts to the components it trained with.
  • A model trained with the component adapts to it, so removing it only at test time isn't the same as training without it.
    Correct. Unless inference-time removal is your actual question, retraining from scratch is the fair test of the component's contribution.
  • Because inference is random.
    Inference isn't the source of the discrepancy; training adaptation is.

Q3. A control ablation removes a component you believe is inert and you see a 3-point drop. What have you learned?

  • Something is wrong with your setup — an inert change shouldn't move the score.
    Correct. A control ablation is a sanity check; a large drop where you expected ~0 signals a bug in the harness, not a real effect.
  • The inert component is actually the most important.
    More likely your measurement is flawed; that's the point of a control.
  • Ablations don't work.
    The tool is fine; the result is flagging a problem to investigate.
  • You should report the 3-point drop as a contribution.
    No — an unexpected drop from an inert change means debug first, not publish.

Q4. Removing component A alone drops accuracy 0.1, and removing B alone drops it 0.1, but removing both drops it 5.0. What's going on?

  • The measurement is broken.
    This pattern is a real, well-known phenomenon, not necessarily a bug.
  • A and B are each useless.
    Individually they look useless, but together they're clearly essential.
  • Contributions are always additive.
    This is exactly a case where they are NOT additive.
  • A and B are redundant — either alone can cover for the other, but losing both is catastrophic.
    Correct. Redundancy makes single ablations misleading; combined ablations reveal the interaction, which is why you report them.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, turn off the component with the largest drop, then the smallest. Which two are they, and what one-sentence conclusion would you write about the model?

Show solution

The largest drop is pretraining (−6.0) and the smallest is the gadget (−0.2, within noise). A fair one-sentence conclusion: "The model's performance is driven almost entirely by pretraining (and, to a lesser extent, attention and augmentation); the namesake gadget contributes no measurable gain." That honest sentence is more valuable than the headline it deflates.

2. (transfer) A reviewer complains that your ablation "just deleted the module at test time." Why is that a fair complaint, and what would you do to address it?

Show solution

It's fair because a model trained with the module learned to rely on it; yanking it out only at inference cripples a model that never had a chance to adapt, so the drop overstates the module's true contribution (or measures the wrong thing entirely). To address it, retrain from scratch without the module, holding all else fixed, over several seeds, and report that drop. The only time test-time removal is the right experiment is when your actual research question is "how gracefully does the trained model degrade if this component fails at inference?"

3. (stretch) You publish that "component C adds 2 points." A later team reproduces your setup but finds C adds 0 points, because they used stronger data augmentation. Who's right, and how should contribution claims be phrased?

Show solution

Both can be right — that's the point. Contributions are configuration-dependent: C may genuinely add 2 points when augmentation is weak (C compensates for it) and 0 points when strong augmentation already provides what C did. This is an interaction, not a contradiction. The honest phrasing is never "C is worth 2 points" but "C contributes about 2 points in our configuration (weak augmentation); its value may shrink when other components already supply a similar benefit." Scoping the claim to the setting is what makes it survive reproduction — and cases like Melis et al.'s language-model ablations, where careful re-evaluation erased reported gains, are exactly why the field now insists on it.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Ablations have repeatedly deflated celebrated results. Melis et al. (2018) re-evaluated a wave of "improved" neural language models and found that a well-tuned plain LSTM matched or beat most of them — the reported gains were largely from tuning and evaluation differences, not the new architectures. Sculley et al.'s "Winner's Curse?" (2018) argued that a culture chasing leaderboard numbers without ablation and rigor produces apparent progress that doesn't replicate, and Lipton & Steinhardt made the same case for explanation over raw performance. The practical upshot for the frontier — including today's large models — is that a striking benchmark number means little until an ablation shows which ingredient earned it. The most respected papers now lead with their ablations, sometimes admitting their headline idea matters less than a boring data or scaling choice. That honesty is the mark of good science.

Recap — say it to a friend

"To find out which part of my model actually matters, I mute one component at a time — retrain, and measure the drop. A big drop means it's load-bearing; a drop smaller than the noise means it's decoration, no matter how proud I am of it. And I always test an inert change too, to prove my measurement is honest."
