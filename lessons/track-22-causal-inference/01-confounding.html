Confounding: when a real effect flips its sign

A confounder doesn't just fake correlations — it can make a treatment that helps everyone look actively harmful. Simpson's paradox is confounding at its most alarming, and it shows why you must think about causal structure before trusting any comparison.

Track 18 · Lesson 1 Concept + code + interactive ~18 min Builds on: Lesson 0

Lesson 0 showed a confounder faking a correlation between unrelated things. Now the twist that makes causal inference genuinely urgent: a confounder can reverse the direction of a real effect. A drug can improve recovery for sick patients and for healthy patients — helping every single subgroup — yet the overall "treated vs untreated" comparison shows it makes things worse. This is Simpson's paradox, and it is not a mathematical curiosity; it has appeared in real drug trials, university admissions discrimination cases, and hospital mortality statistics, leading to exactly the wrong conclusions. The mechanism is confounding: when a confounder — a variable that influences both who gets the treatment and what outcome they have — is unevenly distributed, the naive comparison mixes up the treatment's effect with the confounder's. Here, sicker patients both get the drug more often and have worse baseline outcomes, so the treated group looks bad not because the drug hurts but because it's full of sicker people. This lesson makes the paradox concrete, names the confounder precisely, and shows the only cure: compare like with like by accounting for the confounder.

💡 Intuition: comparing groups that aren't comparable

The whole trouble comes from comparing apples to oranges. Imagine two hospitals: a top trauma center that takes the sickest patients, and a small clinic that treats minor cases. The trauma center has higher mortality — but not because it's worse; because its patients are far sicker. Naively ranking hospitals by mortality would punish the better hospital. The sickness of the patients is a confounder: it drives both which hospital they go to and their outcome. The fix is obvious once you see it: compare mortality within each severity level — among equally sick patients, which hospital does better? That within-group comparison reveals the truth the overall numbers hide. Simpson's paradox is exactly this, sharpened to the point where the overall comparison doesn't just mislead — it flips.

Formally, the naive comparison and the true effect can have opposite signs when a confounder \(Z\) is imbalanced across the treatment groups:

\[ \underbrace{E[Y \mid T{=}1] - E[Y \mid T{=}0]}_{\text{naive (can be negative)}} \;\ne\; \underbrace{\sum_z \big(E[Y \mid T{=}1,Z{=}z]-E[Y \mid T{=}0,Z{=}z]\big)P(z)}_{\text{true effect (positive in every stratum)}} \] (1.1)

Read it: the left side is the naive difference in average outcomes between treated and untreated — which can come out negative. The right side computes the effect within each level of the confounder \(Z\) (comparing like with like), then averages those, weighting by how common each level is. If the drug helps in every stratum, every within-\(Z\) term is positive, so the true effect is positive — even when the naive number is negative. The disagreement is the confounder \(Z\)'s imbalance leaking into the naive comparison.

✍️ Worked example: the drug that "hurts"

From confounding.py: a drug that adds +10 to recovery for everyone, with severity as the confounder.

1
Setup. Sick patients start low (baseline 30), healthy start high (baseline 70). The drug adds +10 to anyone. Confounder: sick patients get the drug 85% of the time, healthy only 15%.
2
Within subgroups. Among sick patients, treated − untreated = +10. Among healthy patients, +10. The drug clearly helps everyone.
3
Overall (naive). The treated group is mostly sick (low baseline); the untreated group is mostly healthy (high baseline). Naive treated − untreated = −22 — the drug looks harmful!
4
Resolution. The −22 is confounding: you're comparing sick-treated against healthy-untreated. Once you compare within severity (like with like), the true +10 emerges. The confounder must be accounted for — the naive number is not just imprecise, it's backwards.

🛠️ From scratch: manufacturing (and resolving) the paradox

sick = rng.random(n) < 0.5
p_treat = np.where(sick, 0.85, 0.15)         # confounder drives treatment assignment
T = rng.random(n) < p_treat
base = np.where(sick, 30.0, 70.0)            # confounder drives baseline outcome
Y = base + 10.0*T + noise                    # TRUE effect of the drug is +10 for everyone

naive          = Y[T].mean() - Y[~T].mean()          # -22  (looks HARMFUL)
within_sick    = Y[T&sick].mean() - Y[~T&sick].mean() # +10  (helps)
within_healthy = Y[T&~sick].mean() - Y[~T&~sick].mean()# +10  (helps)

The drug's effect is +10*T — unambiguously helpful. The naive comparison reports −22 purely because p_treat and base both depend on sick: the confounder is wired into both who gets treated and the outcome. Splitting by sick compares like with like and recovers the truth.

It happened for real. The most famous case: 1973 UC Berkeley graduate admissions appeared to show bias against women overall (lower admission rate), yet within almost every individual department women were admitted at equal or higher rates than men. The confounder: women applied disproportionately to more competitive departments (with low admission rates for everyone). Department choice drove both who applied where and the admission odds. The overall number was Simpson's paradox in action — and taking it at face value would have meant accusing departments that were, if anything, favoring women.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Trusting the aggregate number. The overall comparison can be the exact opposite of the truth. Always ask what confounder might be imbalanced across the groups.
  • Thinking the paradox is a math trick. It's a real, systematic error that has driven wrong medical and policy decisions. The "paradox" is only that our intuition expects aggregate and subgroup trends to agree — they needn't.
  • Adjusting for everything to be safe. You adjust for confounders (common causes of treatment and outcome). Blindly adjusting for other variables can introduce new bias (later lessons) — you need the causal structure to know what to control for.
  • Assuming randomization isn't needed if you "control for stuff." You can only adjust for confounders you measured and knew about. Unmeasured confounders can still flip the result — which is why randomized experiments remain the gold standard.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • A confounder influences both the treatment and the outcome, biasing the naive comparison.
  • Simpson's paradox: a treatment can help every subgroup yet look harmful overall (the sign flips, eq 1.1).
  • Cause: the confounder is imbalanced across treated/untreated, so you're comparing non-comparable groups.
  • Fix: compare within levels of the confounder (like with like), then average — recovering the true effect.
  • Unmeasured confounders can still mislead; randomization removes confounding by design.

Q1. What is a confounder?

  • A variable caused by the treatment.
    That's a mediator/effect, not a confounder.
  • Any variable in the dataset.
    Only specific variables confound; not all do.
  • A variable that influences both the treatment and the outcome.
    Correct. That common influence is what biases the naive comparison.
  • Random noise.
    Noise doesn't create systematic bias; a confounder does.

Q2. In Simpson's paradox, how can a helpful treatment look harmful overall?

  • The treated group is loaded with worse-off cases (via the confounder), dragging its average down.
    Correct. You're comparing non-comparable groups; the confounder's imbalance flips the sign.
  • The treatment secretly harms some people.
    In the paradox it helps every subgroup; the issue is the comparison.
  • There isn't enough data.
    It happens with huge datasets; it's bias, not variance.
  • The outcome is measured wrong.
    The measurement is fine; the confounding is the problem.

Q3. What resolves Simpson's paradox?

  • Collecting more data.
    More data doesn't fix confounding bias.
  • Using a bigger model.
    Model capacity doesn't address it.
  • Ignoring the subgroups.
    The subgroups hold the truth; ignoring them causes the error.
  • Comparing within levels of the confounder, then averaging.
    Correct. Like-with-like comparison recovers the true effect.

Q4. Why is randomization still valuable even if you can adjust for confounders?

  • It's faster.
    Speed isn't the reason.
  • You can only adjust for confounders you measured; randomization handles unmeasured ones too.
    Correct. Randomizing breaks the link to all confounders, measured or not.
  • It needs no data.
    An experiment still collects data.
  • It removes the treatment effect.
    It reveals the treatment effect cleanly, not removes it.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, toggle between "Within subgroups" and "Overall." What is the sign of the effect in each view, and which one is the truth?

Show solution

Within subgroups, both coloured lines slope up (positive effect — the drug helps sick and healthy alike). Overall, the dashed line slopes down (negative — the drug looks harmful). The truth is the within-subgroup result: the drug genuinely helps everyone, and it's +10 in each group. The overall view is an artifact of the confounder — the treated group is packed with low-baseline sick patients, so its average is dragged below the untreated (mostly healthy) group even though treatment adds to each individual. The lesson: when a confounder is imbalanced across groups, trust the like-with-like comparison, never the raw aggregate.

2. (transfer) Two surgeons: A has a 90% success rate, B has 80%. Should you pick A? What would you want to know first?

Show solution

Not yet — you'd want to know the difficulty of the cases each surgeon takes, because case severity is a likely confounder. If surgeon B is the specialist who handles the hardest, highest-risk operations (which have lower success rates for anyone) while A takes routine cases, then B's 80% on hard cases could reflect greater skill than A's 90% on easy ones. Comparing their raw success rates is comparing non-comparable caseloads — exactly Simpson's setup. To decide fairly, compare success rates within difficulty levels (how does each do on comparable cases?). This is why raw surgeon or hospital "scorecards" can be misleading and why risk-adjustment (controlling for case mix) is standard in medical quality measurement — an everyday, high-stakes instance of confounding.

3. (stretch) Given the same data, how do you actually know whether to look at the aggregate or the subgroups? (Hint: you can't decide it from the numbers alone.)

Show solution

You can't decide from the data — you need the causal structure (which variable causes which). The rule: adjust for a variable if it's a confounder (a common cause of treatment and outcome), like patient severity — then the subgroup analysis is correct. But do not adjust for a variable that lies on the causal path from treatment to outcome (a mediator), because that would remove part of the very effect you want to measure — then the aggregate is correct. Two datasets with identical numbers can require opposite choices depending on whether the third variable is a cause or an effect of the treatment. This is the deep point of causal inference: the same statistics support opposite conclusions, and only a causal model (a diagram of what affects what) tells you which analysis answers your question. The do-operator and backdoor criterion (next lessons) make this rule precise.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Confounding is the central threat to every observational study — in medicine, economics, epidemiology, and increasingly in machine learning systems that make decisions. Simpson's paradox has caused real damage: treatments judged effective on confounded data and reversed by trials, discrimination claims that evaporate (or emerge) under proper stratification, quality rankings that punish the best providers. As ML models are used to allocate resources and make interventions, detecting and adjusting for confounding becomes a core competency, and a large field — from classic epidemiology to modern causal machine learning — is devoted to it. The tools in the rest of this track (the do-operator, backdoor adjustment, propensity methods) are precisely how professionals defend against being fooled by a confounder. Recognizing when a comparison isn't like-with-like is one of the most valuable analytical instincts you can develop.

Recap — say it to a friend

"A confounder influences both treatment and outcome, and it can do more than fake a correlation — it can flip a real effect's sign. Simpson's paradox: a drug that helps sick and healthy patients alike can look harmful overall, because the treated group is loaded with sicker people. The fix is to compare within levels of the confounder — like with like — which recovers the true effect. And you need the causal structure, not just the numbers, to know what to adjust for."
