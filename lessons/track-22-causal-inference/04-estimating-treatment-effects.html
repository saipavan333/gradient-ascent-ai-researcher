Estimating treatment effects: propensity & reweighting

Real confounders are continuous, not neat categories, so you can't just stratify. Propensity scores and inverse-propensity weighting rebalance a confounded dataset into a pseudo-experiment — and recover the average treatment effect.

Backdoor adjustment (Lesson 3) worked by summing over the values of a confounder — fine when the confounder is a handful of categories, but real confounders are continuous (age, income, blood pressure, a whole vector of features), and you can't stratify a continuous variable into groups with enough units in each. This final lesson builds the general-purpose tools practitioners actually use to estimate the average treatment effect (ATE) — the population-level \(E[Y\mid do(T{=}1)] - E[Y\mid do(T{=}0)]\) — from confounded observational data. The key device is the propensity score: each unit's probability of receiving the treatment given its confounders, \(e(z) = P(T{=}1\mid Z{=}z)\). It compresses all the confounding into a single number, and a beautiful result says that adjusting for the propensity score alone removes the confounding as well as adjusting for all the confounders. The most direct method, inverse propensity weighting (IPW), then reweights the data so that confounded groups become balanced — up-weighting the units that were unlikely to get the treatment they got, to simulate a population where treatment was assigned randomly. The result is a "pseudo-experiment" from observational data, and its effect estimate matches the true causal effect. This lesson closes the track by turning the theory of the do-operator and backdoor into a working estimator.

💡 Intuition: reweight to rebalance

The problem with confounded data is imbalance: in the confounding lesson, treated patients were mostly sick and untreated mostly healthy, so the groups weren't comparable. Randomization would have fixed this by making the treated and untreated groups look alike. IPW achieves the same balance after the fact by reweighting. The idea: a sick patient who did get treated is rare and precious (most sick patients did too, actually — but a healthy patient who got treated is rare), so count them extra to represent all the similar people who didn't. Concretely, weight each unit by the inverse of the probability it received the treatment it actually got. Units that got a surprising treatment (low probability) get large weights; units that got the expected treatment get small weights. This up-weighting of "counter-to-expectation" cases exactly cancels the confounding, producing weighted treated and untreated groups that are balanced on the confounders — as if treatment had been randomized.

The IPW estimator of the ATE:

\[ e(z) = P(T{=}1 \mid Z{=}z), \qquad \widehat{\text{ATE}} = \frac{\sum_{i: T_i=1} Y_i / e(z_i)}{\sum_{i: T_i=1} 1/e(z_i)} \;-\; \frac{\sum_{i: T_i=0} Y_i / (1-e(z_i))}{\sum_{i: T_i=0} 1/(1-e(z_i))} \] (4.1)

Read it: first estimate the propensity \(e(z)\) — the probability of treatment given confounders — usually with a logistic regression of \(T\) on \(Z\). Then form two weighted averages of the outcome: for treated units, weight each by \(1/e(z_i)\) (rarely-treated units count more); for untreated units, weight by \(1/(1-e(z_i))\) (rarely-untreated units count more). The difference of these weighted averages is the ATE. The weighting rebalances the confounder distribution to be the same in both arms — the pseudo-experiment.

✍️ Worked example: IPW recovers the ATE

From treatment_effects.py: a continuous confounder \(Z\), confounded assignment, true ATE +4.

1
Confounded data. \(T\) is more likely when \(Z\) is high (\(P(T{=}1)=\sigma(1.5Z)\)); \(Y = 3Z + 4T + \text{noise}\). Naive difference in means: \(+7.2\) — biased upward.
2
Estimate propensity. Fit a logistic regression of \(T\) on \(Z\) to get \(\hat e(z) = P(T{=}1\mid Z)\) for every unit.
3
Reweight. Weight treated units by \(1/\hat e\), untreated by \(1/(1-\hat e)\). This rebalances \(Z\) across the two arms.
4
Estimate. The weighted difference (eq 4.1) gives ATE \(= +4.2\) — essentially the true +4, from confounded observational data. IPW turned a biased comparison into a valid causal estimate.

🛠️ From scratch: inverse propensity weighting

naive = Y[T==1].mean() - Y[T==0].mean()             # +7.2  (confounded)

# 1) estimate the propensity e(Z) = P(T=1 | Z) by logistic regression
e_hat = logistic_regression(Z, T)                    # per-unit treatment probability
e_hat = np.clip(e_hat, 0.05, 0.95)                   # avoid extreme weights

# 2) inverse-propensity-weighted difference of outcomes  (eq 4.1)
ate = (T*Y/e_hat).sum()/(T/e_hat).sum() \
    - ((1-T)*Y/(1-e_hat)).sum()/((1-T)/(1-e_hat)).sum()
# ate = +4.2  ==  true ATE (4)

The propensity score \(e(z)\) does the heavy lifting: it summarizes all the confounding into one number, and reweighting by its inverse rebalances the data. The clip guards against a real danger — units with propensity near 0 or 1 get enormous weights and can wreck the estimate (the positivity problem again). IPW is one of a family of estimators (with matching, regression adjustment, and doubly-robust methods) that all target the same ATE.

Doubly robust: two chances to be right. IPW needs the propensity model to be correct; regression adjustment needs the outcome model to be correct. Doubly robust estimators (like AIPW) combine both and give the correct answer if either model is right — a remarkable safety net. This is where modern causal inference meets machine learning: use flexible ML models for both the propensity and the outcome, and doubly-robust "double machine learning" delivers valid ATE estimates with confidence intervals even with many confounders. The propensity score idea you built here is the foundation those methods stand on.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Extreme weights. A unit with propensity 0.01 gets weight 100 and can dominate the estimate. Clip weights, or use methods robust to this (positivity is essential).
  • Forgetting IPW still needs all confounders. The propensity score only summarizes the confounders you put in. Unmeasured confounding biases IPW just like everything else.
  • Trusting a single model. If the propensity model is misspecified, IPW is biased. Doubly-robust methods hedge by also modeling the outcome.
  • Reporting a point estimate with no uncertainty. A treatment effect needs a confidence interval; the reweighting adds variance that must be accounted for.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • The ATE is the population causal effect \(E[Y\mid do(T{=}1)] - E[Y\mid do(T{=}0)]\).
  • Propensity score \(e(z)=P(T{=}1\mid Z)\) compresses all confounding into one number; adjusting for it suffices.
  • IPW reweights by the inverse propensity to rebalance the arms into a pseudo-experiment (eq 4.1).
  • Watch extreme weights (positivity) and remember IPW still needs all confounders measured.
  • Doubly-robust methods model both propensity and outcome — correct if either is right; the basis of causal ML.

Q1. What is a propensity score?

  • The outcome given treatment.
    That's an outcome model, not the propensity.
  • The probability of receiving treatment given the confounders, \(P(T{=}1\mid Z)\).
    Correct. It compresses all confounding into one number.
  • The treatment effect.
    It's an input to estimating the effect, not the effect itself.
  • The learning rate.
    Unrelated.

Q2. How does inverse propensity weighting remove confounding?

  • By deleting confounded units.
    It reweights, doesn't delete.
  • By adding noise.
    That's differential privacy, a different topic.
  • By reweighting units so the confounder is balanced across treatment arms (a pseudo-experiment).
    Correct. Up-weighting counter-to-expectation units rebalances the groups.
  • By using a bigger model.
    Model size isn't the mechanism.

Q3. What is the danger of extreme propensity scores (near 0 or 1)?

  • They speed up training.
    Not a speed issue.
  • They make the model overfit.
    The issue is the weights, not overfitting.
  • They have no effect.
    They can dominate the estimate.
  • Their inverse gives huge weights that can dominate and destabilize the estimate.
    Correct. That's the positivity problem; clip or use robust methods.

Q4. What makes a "doubly robust" estimator attractive?

  • It's correct if either the propensity model or the outcome model is right.
    Correct. Two chances to be right — a valuable safety net.
  • It needs no confounders.
    It still needs the confounders measured.
  • It doubles the accuracy.
    "Doubly" refers to two models, not double accuracy.
  • It requires no data.
    It uses data like any estimator.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, slide the confounding strength from zero to maximum. What happens to the naive estimate versus the IPW estimate, and what does that show?

Show solution

At zero confounding both estimates sit near the true +4 (with no confounding, naive is already unbiased). As you increase the confounding, the naive estimate drifts steadily away from +4 (its bias grows with the confounding), while the IPW estimate stays anchored near +4 across the whole range. This shows that IPW's reweighting successfully removes the confounding regardless of how strong it is: by rebalancing the treatment arms on the confounder, it recovers the causal effect that the naive comparison increasingly mis-measures. It's a live demonstration that the right causal method is robust to confounding that would completely fool a naive analysis.

2. (transfer) "Matching" is another method: pair each treated unit with an untreated unit that has a similar propensity score, then compare. How is that the same idea as IPW?

Show solution

Both remove confounding by comparing units that were equally likely to be treated, using the propensity score as the summary of confounding. IPW does it by reweighting — up-weighting units to make the weighted treated and untreated groups have the same propensity (and hence confounder) distribution. Matching does it by construction — explicitly pairing a treated unit with an untreated unit of similar propensity, so each pair is like a mini-randomized comparison, then averaging the within-pair differences. In both, two units with the same propensity are exchangeable on the confounders (that's the propensity-score theorem), so comparing them is like-with-like. They differ in bookkeeping — matching discards unpaired units and can be more intuitive; IPW uses all units but risks extreme weights — but conceptually they're two ways to exploit the same fact: conditioning on the propensity score balances the confounders. Regression adjustment and stratification on the propensity score are yet more variations on the theme.

3. (stretch) Step back over the whole track. You have a big observational dataset and want to know if a treatment works. Walk through the decisions you'd make.

Show solution

(1) Frame it causally: recognize this is a \(do(T)\) question, not a prediction — a model that predicts the outcome from the treatment won't answer it (Lesson 0). (2) Draw the causal graph: what plausibly causes the treatment and the outcome? Identify confounders (common causes), mediators (on the path), and colliders (common effects) (Lessons 1–2). (3) Check identifiability: can you block all backdoor paths with variables you measured (the backdoor criterion)? If a key confounder is unmeasured, the effect may not be estimable from this data — consider an instrument, a natural experiment, or, ideally, running a randomized trial (Lesson 3). (4) Choose the confounders to adjust for — the valid backdoor set, not "everything" (adjusting for a collider or mediator would inject bias). (5) Estimate with an appropriate method: stratification for a few categorical confounders, or propensity-based IPW / matching / doubly-robust ML for continuous or high-dimensional ones (Lesson 4). (6) Diagnose: check overlap/positivity (are there comparable units in both arms?), watch for extreme weights, and report a confidence interval. (7) Sensitivity analysis: ask how strong unmeasured confounding would have to be to overturn the result. The unifying lesson of the track: the arithmetic is the easy part — the credibility of a causal claim rests on the causal assumptions (the graph) you're willing to defend, and being honest about them is what separates real causal inference from confidently mislabeled correlation.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Propensity scores and IPW are everyday tools in medicine (comparing treatments from health records), economics and policy (evaluating programs from observational data), and tech companies (estimating the effect of a change without a clean A/B test). The frontier is causal machine learning: doubly-robust "double machine learning," causal forests and meta-learners that estimate not just the average effect but heterogeneous effects (who benefits most — the key to personalized medicine and targeting), and neural estimators for high-dimensional confounders. This closes the causal-inference track and the "beyond supervised learning" arc: you've gone from recognizing that correlation isn't causation, through the do-operator and backdoor adjustment, to estimators that recover causal effects from messy real data. That toolkit — reasoning about interventions, not just predictions — is what lets machine learning inform decisions and actions in the world, arguably the most important frontier for AI that helps rather than merely predicts.

Recap — say it to a friend

"To estimate a treatment effect from confounded data with continuous confounders, use the propensity score — the probability of treatment given the confounders. Inverse propensity weighting reweights each unit by the inverse of that probability, rebalancing the treated and untreated groups into a pseudo-experiment, and the weighted difference recovers the true ATE (+4 from a naive +7). Watch extreme weights, measure all confounders, and prefer doubly-robust methods that get two chances to be right."
