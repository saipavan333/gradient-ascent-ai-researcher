Multimodal models: one space for images and text

Models like CLIP learn images and language in a single shared space, so a picture of a dog and the words "a dog" land in the same place. That one idea enables zero-shot classification and powers the text-to-image generators behind modern AI art.

Track 20 · Lesson 2 Concept + code + interactive ~18 min Builds on: Track 13

So far our models handled one kind of data at a time — images, or text, or sequences. But the world is multimodal: meaning lives across pictures, words, sound, and video together. The breakthrough model here is CLIP (Contrastive Language–Image Pre-training), and its idea is beautifully simple: train two encoders — one for images, one for text — to map their inputs into a single shared embedding space, such that an image and its caption land close together while mismatched pairs land far apart. You've seen the machinery: it's contrastive learning (Track 13) across two modalities, trained on hundreds of millions of image–caption pairs scraped from the web, with no manual labels. Once trained, this shared space is astonishingly useful. The headline capability is zero-shot classification: to classify an image into categories it was never explicitly trained on, you simply write the category names as text ("a photo of a cat," "a photo of a dog"), embed them, and pick whichever text embedding the image is closest to — no task-specific training at all. The same shared space is the backbone of text-to-image generation (models like DALL·E and Stable Diffusion use CLIP-style text embeddings to steer image generation) and of image search. This lesson builds the shared space and shows zero-shot classification working.

💡 Intuition: a universal translator between seeing and reading

Imagine a magical map where you can place both photographs and phrases, and the map arranges them by meaning: a photo of a beach sits next to the words "a sunny beach," and far from "a snowy mountain." On this map, comparing a picture to a description is just measuring distance. That's the shared embedding space CLIP builds. Because pictures and words live on the same map, you can do things that seem impossible: hand the model a brand-new photo and a list of possible descriptions, and it tells you which description fits by seeing which is nearest — even for categories it never saw during training. It learned the general relationship between images and language, not a fixed list of classes, so it generalizes to new ones for free. The training that builds this map is pure contrast: pull true image–caption pairs together, push false pairs apart, millions of times, until the map self-organizes by meaning.

Training minimizes a symmetric contrastive loss: for each image, its true caption should be the most similar text among all captions in the batch (and vice versa):

\[ \text{maximize } \text{sim}\big(f_{\text{img}}(I),\, f_{\text{txt}}(T)\big) \text{ for matched } (I,T), \text{ minimize it for mismatched pairs} \] (2.1)

Read it: \(f_{\text{img}}\) and \(f_{\text{txt}}\) are the image and text encoders mapping into the shared space; \(\text{sim}\) is the cosine similarity (alignment) between the two embeddings. Training pushes the similarity up for genuine image–caption pairs and down for mismatched ones (the InfoNCE contrastive loss from Track 13, applied across the two modalities in both directions). The result is that semantically matching images and texts get aligned in the same region of the space.

✍️ Worked example: matched vs mismatched similarity

From multimodal_clip.py: toy image and text vectors aligned by contrastive training.

1
Train. Two encoders map image and text features into an 8-dim shared space, trained contrastively so matched image–text pairs align.
2
Check the space. After training, a matched image–text pair has cosine similarity 0.86; a mismatched pair, −0.17. Meaning-aligned things are close; unrelated things are far.
3
Zero-shot classify. For a new image, embed it and every candidate class name as text, then pick the nearest text prompt — no training on these classes.
4
Result. Zero-shot accuracy: 1.000. The shared space lets text prompts act as classifiers for images they were never paired with — the core CLIP superpower.

🛠️ From scratch: contrastive alignment + zero-shot

Ei = normalise(imgs @ Wi)          # image embeddings in the shared space
Et = normalise(txts @ Wt)          # text embeddings in the same space
S  = Ei @ Et.T / tau               # similarity of every image to every text
# contrastive loss: image i should match text i (the diagonal) — pull matched, push rest
Wi -= lr * imgs.T @ (softmax(S)  - I) @ Et
Wt -= lr * txts.T @ (softmax(S.T) - I) @ Ei

# zero-shot: label a new image by its nearest class-name text prompt
label = (normalise(new_img @ Wi) @ normalise(class_names @ Wt).T).argmax()

The training is the two-modality version of the contrastive loss you built in Track 13. The payoff line is the last one: classification with no classifier — just embed the class names as text and measure similarity. Change the class names and you have a new classifier instantly, for any categories you can describe in words.

The engine behind AI art. CLIP's shared image–text space is a foundation of text-to-image generation. When you type "an astronaut riding a horse" into DALL·E or Stable Diffusion, a CLIP-style text encoder turns your words into an embedding, and the image generator is guided to produce an image whose CLIP embedding matches that text. The shared space is the bridge between what you say and what gets drawn. The same space powers semantic image search ("find photos that match this description"), content moderation, and robotics grounding (connecting language commands to what a camera sees). One contrastive idea, an enormous range of applications.

⚠️ Mistakes everyone makes here

  • Thinking CLIP is a classifier. It's an alignment model — it measures image–text similarity. Classification is a downstream use (compare to class-name prompts), which is why it's zero-shot.
  • Ignoring prompt wording. Zero-shot accuracy depends on how you phrase the class names ("a photo of a cat" often beats "cat"). Prompt engineering matters even here.
  • Assuming it understands like a human. CLIP learns statistical associations from web data — it inherits web biases and can be fooled (e.g., a label written on an object can override the object itself). It's powerful but not grounded understanding.
  • Forgetting the scale. CLIP's magic comes partly from training on ~400M web image–caption pairs. The method is simple; the data scale is essential to its broad zero-shot ability.

🔑 Key results to remember

  • CLIP trains image & text encoders into one shared space by contrastive learning on web image–caption pairs (eq 2.1).
  • Matched image–text pairs align (high similarity); mismatched ones don't — meaning-organized space.
  • Zero-shot classification: embed class names as text, pick the nearest to the image — no task-specific training.
  • Powers text-to-image generation (DALL·E, Stable Diffusion) and image search via the shared space.
  • Simple method, huge data (~400M pairs) — and it inherits web biases.

✍️ Practice

1. (warm-up) In the interactive, click through the images. What makes the model pick the right text prompt for each, and why does it work for classes it wasn't explicitly trained on?

Show solution

For each image, the model embeds it into the shared space and measures its similarity to every text-prompt embedding; the correct label wins because contrastive training placed each image near the words that describe it (and far from unrelated words). It works for classes it wasn't explicitly trained to classify because CLIP didn't learn a fixed list of categories — it learned the general geometry relating images and language. So any new category you can express in words gets a text embedding in the same space, and the image simply lands nearest to the right one. Classification becomes a nearest-neighbour lookup in a meaning-organized space, which is why swapping in new class names instantly yields a new classifier with no retraining — the essence of zero-shot.

2. (transfer) Zero-shot accuracy improves if you use "a photo of a {label}" instead of just "{label}". Why would the exact wording of the text prompt matter?

Show solution

Because CLIP was trained on web captions, which are full phrases like "a photo of a golden retriever," not bare nouns. So the text encoder produces better, more in-distribution embeddings for prompts that look like real captions than for a lone word — "a photo of a cat" sits where actual cat-image captions sit in the space, whereas "cat" is an unusual, out-of-distribution string the encoder handles less well. Matching the prompt style to the training distribution therefore places the class embedding more accurately, improving the nearest-neighbour decision. This is "prompt engineering" for CLIP, and practitioners even average several prompt templates per class to get a more robust text embedding. It's a reminder that these models are pattern-matchers over their training data — you get the best behaviour by phrasing queries the way the training data was phrased, a theme that recurs across all prompt-based AI.

3. (stretch) CLIP can be fooled: put a piece of paper reading "iPod" on an apple and it may label the apple an iPod. What does this reveal about how it "understands," and why does it matter?

Show solution

This "typographic attack" reveals that CLIP learns statistical associations between visual features and text, not grounded, human-like understanding. Because it was trained on web images where text in an image is highly predictive of the caption (product photos, memes, labels), it learned to weight written words heavily — so a literal label can override the object's actual appearance. It matters for two reasons. First, robustness/security: systems built on CLIP (moderation, search, autonomous perception) can be manipulated by adversarial text or misleading captions, so you can't assume the model "sees" what a person would. Second, it's a concrete example of the general lesson that large models exploit whatever correlations exist in their training data, including shortcuts and spurious cues (echoing the correlation-vs-causation and interpretability themes from Tracks 18 and 23) — impressive zero-shot ability coexists with brittle, non-human failure modes. Understanding these failure modes is essential before trusting such models in high-stakes settings, and it motivates ongoing work on more grounded, robust multimodal understanding.

🚀 In the real world & at the frontier

Multimodal models are one of the hottest areas in AI. CLIP itself is everywhere — zero-shot classification, image search, content moderation, and as the text-conditioning backbone of image generators. The frontier has moved to models that don't just align modalities but reason across them: vision-language models (like GPT-4V and its successors) that can look at an image and discuss it, answer questions about it, read charts, and follow visual instructions — and models extending to audio, video, and action for robotics. The unifying dream is a single model that perceives and reasons over all modalities the way humans do. CLIP's core insight — learn a shared representation across modalities by contrast — is a foundational step toward that, and it's why "multimodal" is central to where AI is heading.
